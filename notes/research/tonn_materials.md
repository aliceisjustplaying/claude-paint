# Kendric Tonn: materials and method

*A sourced technical report. Every claim cites a source key; the sources
are listed at the end. Almost all of it comes from the painter's own
words: posts on X (2019–2026), a long materials thread of 2023 and
interviews. No technical study of his paintings exists. "Uncertain"
marks claims that rest on a single remark, may have changed since or are
inferred. Where his practice changed, the dates are given and the newer
statement is the one to rely on.*

## Summary

Tonn trained in a "self-consciously 19th century" academic method and
says he still works "in a manner not unlike" his school taught him
[TRUNK; BC]. He paints almost always from life [ICK], but transfers his
drawings to the canvas with a projector [PROJ]. For medium and large
works his usual support is canvas glued to a cradled plywood panel; he
paints small works on hardboard or commercial gesso board [SUB; MASON;
MAT23]. He primes with an oil ground applied with a wide taping knife,
described in 2020 as "a tinted lead ground" and in 2025 as a "greenish
ground" [LEAD20; PRIME19; GRND25]. Lead (flake) white is his standard
white; by 2022 he "basically [didn't] use titanium" [WHITE21; TI22]. The
palette is low in chroma, built on earths, ultramarine, cerulean, black
and lead white [LOW21; BS20; BLUE21]. Since 2025 it has also included a
quinacridone "permanent alizarin" and lead-tin yellow [ALIZ25; LTY25].
He uses very little medium, basically no liquid oil and no solvent in the
paint [PAL20; MED25; SOLV21]. He paints mostly with natural-bristle flats
and uses nylon brushes to smooth [BR21; BR22]. He calls himself "85% a
direct painting guy" (2026) [DIR26]. A layered work goes through drawing,
a thin first painting, a second painting and final glazes, and he works
over the whole surface at once [SEQ22; IMPR]. Thread counts, ground
thickness and the ground recipes are not documented.

**Terms.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layer between support and
paint. *Imprimatura*: a thin toned wash over the ground. *Grisaille*: a
monochrome underpainting. *Glaze*: a transparent paint layer.
*Impasto*: thick, raised paint. *Alla prima*: painting in a single wet
layer. *Retouch varnish*: a thin temporary varnish that can be painted
over. *Black mirror*: a darkened mirror that simplifies values.

## 1. Supports

- **Canvas on panel (main support).** "For my own work, I've mostly
  settled on a high-grade synthetic canvas adhered to a cradled plywood
  back." The plywood keeps the fabric from flexing and cracking the
  paint; the fabric bridges the plywood's tendency to check. He prefers
  synthetic fabric because it resists damage from the oil, "unlike flax
  or cotton" [SUB 6/–8/]. He cuts the plywood himself with a circular
  saw [GESSO21]. He still made "custom panels" in 2025 [GRND25].
- **Adhesive.** First an acrylic adhesive; from 2020 BEVA film,
  "reversable with low-ish heat," chosen so a conservator could separate
  fabric and panel later [SUB 9/–12/]. After gluing he trims and
  staples the canvas back around the panel [LEAD20]. No later post says
  whether he still uses BEVA. *Uncertain.*
- **Oil-primed stretched canvas.** In 2025 he oil-primed "a bunch of
  canvases" and had a 24 × 48 in. oil-primed canvas "all ready to go"
  [GRND25; CAN25]. For a canvas about 6 ft long he would "consider going
  to an aluminum bar" for the stretcher [STR25].
- **Linen.** He bought "a roll of linen" in 2020 [KIT20]. Which fabric
  goes on which panel is not stated. *Uncertain.* Early listings (2011)
  give "oil on canvas affixed to panel" and oil on stretched canvas
  [SAA].
- **Rigid boards for small works.** "I paint small pieces on Masonite"
  [MASON]. "For small works that I intend to be serious, I often use
  Ampersand Gessoboard; good surface ... smooth" [MAT23]. He also buys
  ready-made wooden panels (for example 12 × 24 in.) and has had supply
  trouble with them [PANELS21]. A small alla prima head of 2025 was "oil
  on panel" [AP26].
- **Views on other supports.** Stretched canvas "lasts only moderately
  well." Solid wood panels last but crack and warp. Copper is "probably
  ideal" but costly at size; works on marble and slate also "look
  amazing after centuries" [SUB 2/–5/]. He advises against cotton duck
  glued to cardboard [MAT23].
- **Sizes.** Figure paintings of about 30 × 40 in. [BC]. Listed works
  run from 9 × 12 to 36 × 48 in., all "oil on panel" [FAA].
- **Thread count.** Not documented. *Gap.*

## 2. Grounds

- **Oil ground, knifed.** "The oil primer I use has a heavy, roughly
  wet-mud consistency, and I want it pretty smooth, so I've been going
  with a taping knife" (a drywall knife) [PRIME19].
- **Lead ground, tinted (2020).** "Prime them with a tinted lead
  ground." He adds that lead "produces a uniquely strong paint film, and
  dries rapidly," and recalls a study finding that a lead ground
  "donates ions to adjacent paint films over time, increasing their
  strength and flexibility" [LEAD20]. The tint is not stated.
- **Greenish oil ground (2025).** In June 2025 he primed custom panels
  with an oil ground that "will be a while drying" and called it "the
  greenish ground" [GRND25]. Whether it contains lead, and its recipe,
  are not stated. *Uncertain.*
- **Acrylic ground when time is short.** In 2021 he applied acrylic
  "gesso" to a new canvas panel: "I usually prefer the feel of an oil
  primed surface, but I don't care to wait a few months for it to dry"
  [GESSO21].
- **Surface.** "I like to have a little bit of life in my surfaces when
  I start working--dont want to have a glassy smooth canvas." He
  experiments with texture "in the ground underneath the paint" and
  likes "a bit of roughness and chaos to it when you get close"
  [TEX21; TEX-B]. When one canvas came out rougher than planned, he used
  "much heavier impasto than I normally do" [TEX21].
- **White ground and transparency.** "If you start with a white ground,
  paint applied transparently will be lighter and substantially more
  intense in color than the same paint applied opaquely" [TRANS19].
- **Toning.** "I used to use a mid grey imprimatura, but haven't in
  ages" (2021) [IMPR]. Later he describes an alla prima head painted over
  "a flat brownish ground layer, or imprimatura" (2024) [AP24]. So he
  tones or tints some grounds but no longer uses the mid gray. Which
  works get which is not stated. *Uncertain.*
- **Thickness.** Not documented. *Gap.*

## 3. Drawing and lay-in

- **Preparatory drawing (large figures).** "Generally will do a highly
  worked-up drawing in pencil & chalks before starting on a painting,"
  working out heads and hands in it [STUD20]. These sheets are 22 × 30
  in. with about 20 hours of model time [DRW21]. He tries to "lock in
  pose and proportions during the drawing stage" [PROJ].
- **Transfer by projector.** He does not draw at painting scale and
  "absolutely hate[s] using a grid-transfer," so he photographs the
  drawing and projects it with "an ancient 640x480 projector" (2024).
  In 2026 he still said that the traditional transfer methods "suck and
  I am absolutely using a projector," and described the step as "throw
  it on my computer onto a projector and trace." He does not think it
  works where alignment has to be exact [PROJ; TRAD26].
- **Studies first (occasional).** For one set of commissions in 2026 he
  planned "studies from life, final drawing from studies, paint that"
  [COV26]. *Uncertain* as a general practice.
- **Drawing on the canvas.** In a still life: "drawing in charcoal
  directly on the canvas" [SEQ22].
- **Drawing never stops.** In the first painting session he keeps
  "working the drawing, that never stops (today I reduced the size of
  the head quite a bit)" [IMPR].
- **Raw umber lay-in.** He explains brown underlayers on unfinished old
  pictures by the drying of earth browns: "rough in your painting in raw
  umber, and it's touch dry the next morning" (2023) [RU23]. He
  recommends raw umber as "good for under paintings and grisaille"
  (2025) [RU25]. Whether he lays in with raw umber himself is not
  stated. *Uncertain.*
- **Aids to seeing.** Squinting, a black mirror ("You lose the
  distinction between similar darks, and between similar lights") and,
  above all, a regular mirror [MIR20; ICK; BC]. He was issued a black
  mirror on his first day at the Academy; it "consolidates your lights
  together and your darks together" (2026) [BM26]. He occasionally
  measures by holding up a brush and marking a distance with his thumb,
  "but mostly I'm purely operating on visual comparison" (2026) [MEAS26].
- **Photographs.** "I'll sometimes grab a photo to remind myself of a
  color or a texture ... They're not a big part of my process" (2025).
  He has "occasionally even used a photo reference though I don't like
  it" (2026) [PHOTO; TRAD26].
- **Restarting.** He wipes an initial drawing off the canvas and starts
  again, or scrapes off a sketch "before it dries" [LPP; SCRAPE20].

## 4. Pigments

His palette changes with the painting ("the specific pigments may vary
by painting") [PAL20]. He carried "ten colors" to one portrait sitting
[KIT20b]. His own description: "I'm a pretty low chroma painter" [LOW21].
Early on he spent "about five years painting in a limited palette"
[LIM21]. A ten-color palette posted in May 2025 was "relatively
unusual," because "I am in fact not normally running four different
yellows": ivory black, ultramarine blue, terre verte, permanent
alizarin, burnt sienna, transparent oxide yellow, yellow ochre, cadmium
yellow deep, cadmium yellow light, flake white [PAL25]. In September
2025 he sorted his tubes into a "standard rotation" tier, a tier for
"as circumstances call for it" and spares; the colors in each tier are
shown only in photographs [SHELF25]. He uses "a much wider palette in
watercolor than in oils" (2026) [WC26].

**Beginner palettes he recommends** (not necessarily his own): flake
white, English red, yellow ochre, ivory black [LIM21]; titanium white,
ivory black, English red, yellow ocher, raw umber, ultramarine blue, "an
entirely satisfactory palette, of cheap-end pigments" [MAT23].

**Paint quality.** He prefers top-grade paint that is just "oil+pigment,"
where "the chemistry and texture falls out however it may," over student
paint with modifiers. He suggests cheaper pigments in an expensive line
and "learning to use the variable drying times to your advantage" [DRY23].

**Brands.** In 2023 he mostly used Old Holland and one other premium
artists' brand [MAT23]. In 2024 he named Williamsburg as well: "I mostly
use Williamsburg, Old Holland, and [the other brand]" [BRAND24].

**Grinding.** "I don't grind my own paint" (2026) [TRAD26]. Earlier posts
mention grinding with a muller (2021) and "decades ... grinding paint"
(May 2026) [GRIND]. *Uncertain* whether he ever ground paint for his own
pictures; the latest statement is that he does not.

## 5. Media and solvents

- **Oil.** Linseed. He mentions its smell often [TRUNK; LIN21; GRND25].
  Among drying oils, linseed is "the most common and the fastest drying
  ... (also forms the most durable paint films, but is more prone to
  yellowing)" [DRY23].
- **Little medium.** "I use very little, which is generally best, but I
  have a gel to loosen paints, and an additive to increase their
  transparency" (2020) [PAL20]. The gel is an oleogel. For glazing he
  uses a commercial "Venetian" medium containing some turpentine [MED21].
  The 2025 wording: "I use very very little medium. I use Natural
  Pigment's Venetian Medium for glazing in the latter stages of a
  painting, and I'll use their oleogel to make paints a little longer,
  but basically I just work with straight paint." The Venetian Medium
  "does also speed up drying a good bit" [MED25].
- **No liquid oil.** "I use basically no liquid oil for my paints" (2025)
  [MED25]. He cut down the oil he uses partly because of studio fire risk
  [OIL19]; with so little oil he rates the risk of spontaneous combustion
  as "very low" [MED25]. Oil-soaked rags should be spread out, not left
  "loosely bunched in a semi contained area" [RAGS25].
- **No solvent in painting.** "I don't use any solvents in painting"
  (2021) [SOLV21]; "I don't use any to actually paint with" (2024)
  [SOLV24]. He is "very opposed to the common practice of painting with
  an open dish of mineral spirits." Solvent is used only for cleaning and
  stays "in the brush cleaning" tin (2025) [SOLV25]. He uses odorless
  mineral spirits to clean brushes [BR21].

## 6. Layers and application

- **Direct by default.** "I'm a pretty direct painter, in most
  circumstances" (2022) [UP22]; "I'm like 85% a direct painting guy"
  (2026) [DIR26].
- **Direct vs. indirect, in his terms.** Direct painting is where "what
  the viewer actually sees--is given by an opaque surface layer of
  paint"; indirect painting "uses various forms of transparency to create
  a visual effect caused by the action of multiple layers." "There is
  obviously a spectrum here" [AP24].
- **Four stages (layered works).** "Drawing ... First painting (a lower
  contrast, thinner, somewhat scratchy looking first draft) ... Second
  painting ... Glazes and finalizing." He calls this "a sort of idealized
  process, often strayed from" [SEQ22]. A portrait painted "in (I think)
  three sessions" shows "layers of paint sitting on top of layers of
  paint" [AP24].
- **Alla prima studies.** Heads painted with "all the paint ... applied
  in a single wet layer," over a flat brownish imprimatura [AP24]. He
  paints them in about three hours and tries "to do a batch of these
  every year or two," especially when he needs "to get used to oils
  again" (2026) [AP26].
- **Whole surface at once.** In the first session he states "the
  shadow-shape on the model and the overall relation of figure to
  supporting elements." Next comes "a rough-in of the parts of the
  figure in light." He works "over the entire surface simultaneously.
  Again and again and again, tightening and refining each time" [IMPR].
  Some elements (patterns) go in after the block-in [PAT20].
- **Monochrome underpainting (occasional).** A grisaille "worked up to
  a fairly high level of finish before laying color over it," but "using
  warms and cools in the first layer" [GRIS19]. In 2022 again: "a worked
  up rending in grisaille ... something I do infrequently" [GRIS22].
  Other monochrome underpaintings needed "to dry for a few days" before
  the next layer [UP22]. He has never painted a verdaccio: "I'd like to
  try an actual verdaccio for a figure" (2026) [VERD26].
- **Glazes.** A glaze is "transparent or semitransparent paint, for the
  purpose of manipulating color or value." He usually applies glazes "in
  neutral colors, as literally the last thing I do" [TV22], with the
  Venetian medium "in the latter stages" [MED25]. Once, as an exception,
  he glazed terre verte early over the monochrome underpainting, then
  repainted it "with solid paint, complicating the color and
  reestablishing values" [TV22].
- **Paint follows the object.** "Rough object? Put down rough paint.
  Smooth? Smooth. Covered in a semitranslucent glaze? Build your
  painting out of semitranslucent paint" [STRUCT21].
- **Stiff paint, controlled.** "I'm a fairly controlled painter, and I
  use fairly stiff paints, so I'm generally not needing to have a brush
  completely soaked down to the ferrule" [BR22].
- **Thickness.** "I'm not the heaviest painter on the planet" [VAR21].
  Heavy impasto is an exception [TEX21]. Oil paint's transparency
  varies "in the paints themselves and in medium you mix with them and
  in applications" [LPP].
- **Surface correction.** He grinds down unwanted texture with a
  cuttlefish bone and refills it with the palette knife, "like doing
  drywall" [TEX-B].
- **Brushes.** "I paint 95% with flat, natural-bristle brushes, usually
  made by Escoda, in various sizes, 5% with cheap white nylon brushes
  that are good for putting down smooth, sharp marks" [BR21]. Most are
  "1/4-1/2ish inch wide" (2023) [MAT23]; "about 1cm wide or ½cm wide"
  (2022) [BR22]. Sables are of little use in oil in his view [SAB21].
  Details from a 2022 thread [BR22]:
  - He ends a session with "about six or eight brushes in my hand,"
    separate ones "for different 'categories' of color, and also for
    different techniques/styles of application." At minimum, larger and
    smaller brushes for light and for dark colors [MAT23].
  - For a flat area he puts the paint down with bristle "and work[s] it
    in place with a synthetic" (nylon) brush; a wider brush helps where
    there is "nothing to work around, no gradient."
  - He paints directly with nylon for "lines and sharp edges, and for
    painting glassy or metallic things."
  - "At the very end of a painting, I might use a small round ... for
    some details or accents," also cheap nylon.
  - Worn, slightly ragged brushes "can be useful for painting things like
    hair or rough-textured objects."
  - "Very rarely do I use the chonky brushes."
- **Palette.** A traditional arm palette, not a white disposable one; it
  "rests back ON your arm" and "MUST balance so it has a center of
  gravity directly on your thumb." For small studio works he also uses a
  portable pochade easel with a built-in palette [MAT23]. Colors sit on
  the edge "grouped by hue, in spectrum order," light to dark within each
  hue, "with transparent pigments considered darker than opaque
  pigments." Mixtures for lit flesh form a grid: dark to light left to
  right, warm to cool top to bottom. Separate areas hold shadow flesh
  (2–3 mixtures), hair and background by depth. He scrapes all mixtures
  off and starts fresh when he loses focus [PAL20].

## 7. Working conditions and time

- **From life.** "I almost always work from life" [ICK]. Portraits are
  "built out of three, or 12, or 50 hours with someone" [ICK]. In 2026:
  "I draw and paint from life" [PROJ].
- **Duration.** A figure in an interior, about 30 × 40 in.: "about two
  months" on the easel, with the model in "for 40 hours, 50 hours" [BC];
  "60 or so hours" in 2019 [DOC]. Two or three works run in parallel
  [BC]. Alla prima heads take about three hours [AP26].
- **Still life.** He sets up a still life and lives with it "a couple
  days" before starting [BC]. In one still life he kept everything "dull
  and muted ... until close to the end" [DUL21].
- **Light.** In 2021: poor natural light, so LED panels "with great
  color rendering" [LED21]. In 2026: "my MODEL is under natural light,
  my EASEL is lit by a clamp-light" [LIGHT26]. The newer post is the
  current setup.

## 8. Drying, varnish and condition

- **Drying rates.** "Earth tones (umbers, siennas), cobalt blue, and
  lead white are rapid dryers, whereas ivory black and titanium white are
  notoriously slow" (2023) [DRY23]. Rates also depend on "atmospheric
  conditions, painting mediums," and "thickness of paint layer" [VAR21].
  "Even the fast drying pigments won't really dry during a working
  session" [FAST20].
- **Rest before varnish.** Paintings rest about six months before final
  varnish [SIX19; VAR21]. "The pigments I use are good dryers ... I'll
  sometimes specifically alter my paint choices if I know something need
  to leave faster" [VAR21]. Touch-dry is not enough: "best to wait a few
  months. Continues drying better without the varnish layer" (2023)
  [VARN23]. Conservators he knows use a thumbnail test: "if moderate
  pressure with your thumbnail won't dent the impasto, it's fine to
  varnish" [VAR21]. He built one commission "to be touch-dry as rapidly
  as possible" [RET21].
- **Retouch varnish.** A light spray of damar, used "very rarely" to
  bring up sunken areas before repainting [RET21; VAR21]. He chooses
  damar because it bonds "very, very well to oil paints" and "much of
  that retouch varnish may end up overpainted"; he sprays "very lightly
  ... on dark paintings, so we've got a minimal, noncontinuous layer"
  [VAR21].
- **No intermediate varnish.** He rejects "intermediate thin layers of
  varnish between layers of paint, instead of restricting varnish to the
  topmost layer," as a bad idea "if you're concerned about the long-term
  condition of your piece" [IVAR19].
- **Final varnish.** High-gloss Gamvar (a Regalrez resin), chosen
  because it does not crosslink with oil, removes with mild solvents and
  stays "water clear" [VAR21]. Still his varnish in 2024 and 2025
  [VARN24; VARN25]. He applies "less gamvar that you think--a pretty
  small amount goes a long ways, and it needs to be spread pretty
  vigorously" [VARN24; VARN25]. Everything gets "two coats of varnish
  before they leave the studio," because dry oil paint ends up "a little
  lighter & a little less saturated" [VARN25; DRYLOOK24]. Varnish "evens
  the sheen and re-darkens or saturates things that have dried somewhat
  matte & light" and protects against "dust and smoke" [VARN24].
- **Reworking.** He strips and re-varnishes when he reworks a painting,
  which is rare [STRIP22; STRIP24].
- **Structure.** He avoids complicating the layer structure. His
  example is a crack pattern from "a fast-drying earth brown ... over a
  slower-drying paint" [CRACK21]. He expects oil paint to become "more
  transparent with age" [CRACK21].
- **Aim.** "I'm really trying to make paintings that can last a few
  centuries with minimal intervention" [LEAD20].
