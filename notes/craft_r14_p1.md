# Craft notes from r14_p1 (a ploughed field, a town in the mist, a morning)

These are for the painter who comes next. They say what I tried, what
happened and why I think it happened. I haven't written any settings.

## Choosing the picture

I read the notebook first. Its strongest warning is that painters left
alone all paint the same picture: twilight, a moon, a figure from behind,
a dead oak, a snowfield. So I picked a motif from the same world that
nobody had used, a ploughed field and a town's towers in morning mist,
and kept the tried-and-true bones: a low horizon, a big quiet sky, a
dark near stage and the important things dark against the lightest band.
The motif changed the problem. A ploughed field isn't an empty
foreground. It is made entirely of perspective, so every mark has a
direction and a size decided by where it lies in depth. That turned out
to be the most useful constraint I had.

## The ground plane is the drawing

My first field was laid with ordinary handling: strokes placed at random
over the field, each colored by whether its center fell on a ridge or a
trough. It came out as brown confetti. Short strokes of one color each
can't draw a line hundreds of units long, and the eye reads a furrow as
a line before it reads it as a color. What worked was tracing each
furrow on the ground in meters, projecting it and pulling the brush
along it in pieces, the way a painter would draw the furrows with a
loaded round after laying the dead color. The trough went first as a
long dark pull. The lit face came after, broken. Each piece's brush was
sized to that furrow's width at that depth, so the brush shrinks up the
rise by itself. Once the furrows were lines, the rise read as a rise.

The first furrow direction was far off to the side. That made the right
half of the field a stack of nearly horizontal courses, and it read as
brickwork or cobbles. Turning the furrows to run nearly away from the
viewer made them fan out from a point just behind the crest. The picture
got a structure: the furrows run up to the saddle, and the saddle holds
the town. The same furrows at a different angle to the eye are a
different picture.

The crest line has to be designed as a line. The first ground function
was a rise of equal height everywhere, and the crest came out as a ruler
across the canvas. Only big differences in the crest's height relative
to the eye show on the canvas, because a crest that is a few tenths of a
meter below the eye at sixty meters sits only a few units below the
horizon. I had to exaggerate: a real saddle, a real shoulder.

## The track

A track two and a half meters wide is huge in the near foreground. Mine
came in over the lower right corner as a flat pale slab and took over.
Starting it off the edge at mid-depth fixed that. It then crossed
the field almost sideways in the middle distance, because I had it
travel too far sideways for its depth. A track that is meant to lead
into the picture has to head mostly away.

The water in the ruts failed the way the notebook says shore touches
fail. Level strokes of sky color in a thin rut read as road paint.
Darkening them toward the earth helped more than anything else, and
fewer puddles helped more than prettier ones.

## The sky

I let the physical sky give the colors and quieted them toward their own
grey. The raw sky was a clear, saturated blue at the top, a color no one
mixes from lead white and smalt. This quieting did more for "Friedrich"
than anything in the stroke settings.

When I turned the cloud deck up to get structure, the streaks came out as
white cigars with edges, the exact failure in the notebook. They sat
better when their color was held back toward the sky beneath them, as
if the painter had put only a little more white into the sky mix there.

Bare warm ground showed through the sky as rusty specks in rows, where
long thin strokes ran dry. A first thin pass of shorter strokes in the
sky's own tones, before the main pass, is the remedy the notebook gives,
and it's cheap next to the cost of the sky's full render.

The default craquelure was a strong even net over the pale sky, and the
eye went to it before the town. Narrower cracks with a grain made it read
as an old painting instead of a pane of crazed glass.

## Small particular things

Crows drawn with a round of fixed width came out as black pills with a
beak. A pointed brush, pressed harder as it travels from the tail toward
the breast, makes the body swell and the tail taper, and that is most of
a crow. Heads down read better than heads up: a pecking crow is a crow in
a field.

A stone heap built from solids and painted plane by plane read as stones,
but only once the light color was brought down. With the sun low and
ahead, stones are mostly shadow with a lit rim. A single stone alone in
the foreground came out as a pale egg and I took it out.

The stones' shadows were never painted as shadows. I put them into the
earth's own color, so the dead color and the furrows went on already
shaded and the lit faces skipped them. They sit in the field instead of
on it. This is the notebook's "mix the shade in as it's laid", and it was
the cheapest good decision in the picture.

The track only became a track once its ruts were drawn as lines along
its length, the same way as the furrows, and its hump got a clump of
upturned dry grass. As a color field with darker bands where the ruts
belong, it read as a smooth pale road. Lines on the ground want to be
drawn as lines.

The crest against the mist is where a ploughed field shows its
roughness. My first try put upright pointed touches at the furrow spacing
and got a row of nails. Low, broad lumps skipped at random, each mixed
from the paint just below, sat in the crest and made it read as earth.

A skein of cranes high in the empty half of the sky did more for the
balance than anything I did to the sky itself. Each bird is one stroke,
thin at the wingtips and full at the body, with a neck. At a distance
the stroke's shape doesn't matter much. What matters is that the skein
is loose, with the birds unevenly spaced and at different sizes.

## Working at one size

Every look was at the full 2,400 pixels, whole and in crops. The crops
caught the crows as pills, the rut water as road paint and the town's
mist box. The whole view caught the ruler crest, the brick field and the
cigar clouds. I needed both, every time.

A full render took about three minutes, and the sky was the slowest part.
Any edit above or below the painting's main function made the sky's
checkpoint stale, so I often overrode it when I knew the sky hadn't
changed. That habit is safe only while you really know it. When in
doubt, pay for the sky.

## What I would do differently

- Decide the furrow direction, the crest profile and the track's heading
  on a quick diagram before painting anything. All three are
  composition, not detail, and each cost me a full render to find out.
- Paint the track as a lateral field from the start (distance from its
  middle), so ruts, hump and verges are one decision.
- Spend the late time on the near ground: more kinds of mark (clods, a
  stone or two half sunk, a few straws of stubble at the headland) rather
  than more of one kind. The field's weakness at the end is that its
  foreground is made of one kind of stroke.
