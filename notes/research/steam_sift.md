# Steam on pastel, and crushed pastel sifted onto the sheet

The numbers behind crates/paint/src/steam.rs (the easel's `--@ steam-sift`; notes/cassatt_support.md gap 6). Tags: [M] measured, [D] derived here from measured values or standard physics, [P] a practitioner's, museum's or historian's account, [E] an estimate, with the reason it is one. No measurement of steam on pastel, or of crushed pastel on paper, was found, so each step is built from the physics of its parts and reuses the engine's models: the wet pastel switch's water, gum and drying (notes/research/wet_pastel.md), the studio (`climate::STUDIO`, 20 °C and 50 %), the dry tools' grain adhesion (sheet.rs, pastel_brushing.md) and charcoal's water bridge (charcoal.md §4).

## 0. What the artists did

- **Degas** [P] (notes/degas-materials.md §5). Steam from a boiling kettle, played over the surface, either dissolved a layer into a soft "vaporous film" or melted thick passages into a paste he reworked with a brush or knife as it dried. It can leave fine pitting. The accounts are anecdotal (Rouart 1945; Boggs & Maheux), and the Musée d'Orsay says he worked pastel "crushed or worked with steam".
- **Cassatt, a 1914 pastel** [P] (notes/research/cassatt_materials.md §2, after the Met's paper conservator and a mock-up). She "selectively exposed" the sheet "to moisture vapor", and seems to have sifted crushed pastel onto the dampened surface, so that "its slight dampness agglomerated the particles into minute clusters as it dried", in the flesh and the green around it. The sheet shows no fixative. The article calls it possibly a one-off experiment.
- What the engine takes from these: steam wets the sheet and the pastel without moving pigment, the gum softens and dissolves, and as it dries the pastel is held (Degas "stabilized" pastel locally with it, Cassatt's clusters stayed unfixed). Crushed pastel falls as crumbs without a stroke, and dampness makes it stick and gather into clusters.

## 1. The kettle's jet (`plume`)

- **Steam out of the spout [D, E].** A boiling kettle turns its heat input into steam at h_fg = 2.257 MJ/kg (steam tables) [M]. The heat is `KETTLE_W` = 1 kW [E]: an electric kettle draws 1.8–3 kW at 80–90 % efficiency, a kettle on a gas ring about 2 kW at 40–60 %, and an 1880s studio kettle sat on a stove or spirit lamp whose output isn't recorded. Bounds 0.5–2.5 kW. That is 0.44 g/s of steam. The spout is `SPOUT_MM` = 10 mm across [E: domestic kettles 8–12 mm], so the steam (0.598 kg/m³ at 100 °C [M]) leaves at 9.4 m/s, Re ≈ 4600: a turbulent jet.
- **Its decay [M].** Past its potential core a round jet's centreline velocity and mass fraction fall as U/U₀ = 6.3 (d/x) √(ρ₀/ρₐ) and C/C₀ = 5.4 (d/x) √(ρ₀/ρₐ) (Chen & Rodi 1980, the variable-density forms as quoted by the LES literature, e.g. Int. J. Heat Fluid Flow 2008, S0142727X08000271; hydrogen-dispersion work uses the same 5.4). Its velocity half-width grows as 0.094 x (Hussein, Capp & George 1994; Pope 2000, *Turbulent Flows*, Table 5.1: 0.094–0.102) [M]. The laws hold from about ten spout widths on, so `steam` takes 100–600 mm.
- **What reaches the sheet [D].** Mixing the steam's share s with the studio's air conserves the enthalpy: s h_v(100 °C) + (1 − s) h_air. Where the mixture holds more water than its air can at its temperature it is a fog (visible steam): saturated, the rest as droplets. At 200 mm the steam's share is 0.19 and the fog is about 67 °C, its dew point the same; at 150 mm 0.25 and 73 °C; at 500 mm 0.08 and 49 °C (`the_kettle_jet_mixes_as_a_round_jet_into_a_warm_fog`).
- **Heat and mass transfer at the sheet [M, E].** Where the jet meets the sheet, Sibulkin's laminar stagnation-point law gives h = 0.763 k Pr^0.4 √(β/ν) (Sibulkin 1952, *J. Aeronaut. Sci.* 19:570). Its velocity gradient β is taken as the centreline velocity over the half-width [E: the order of an impinging jet's strain; no measured β for a free jet at 20 diameters was found]. At 200 mm, h = 46 W/m²K. Measured jets and flames give 1.2–1.8 times the laminar value, so this is the low end. It matters little: within a second the condensation is limited by the heat the board takes, not by the boundary layer (§2). The mass transfer coefficient follows by Chilton–Colburn (h/ρc_p · Le^(−2/3), as wet_pastel.md §4): 0.042 m/s.
- **Left out:** the jet's buoyancy (a warm jet aimed at a vertical sheet bends upward; at 9 m/s and 200 mm its Froude number is high and the bend is small); the fog's droplets depositing (at the stagnation flow's low Stokes number they follow the air); and the Stefan flow of a vapour-rich mixture, which would raise the flux by a few tens of percent at the jet's 0.19 share.

## 2. How much condenses (`condense`)

- **The face's heat balance [D].** The face takes the fog's sensible heat and the latent heat of what condenses: q = h (T_fog − T_s) + L h_m (ρ_v,fog − ρ_sat(T_s)). It conducts the heat into the sheet and the board behind (a pastel sheet pinned to a board, as wet_pastel.md §4 has it), both starting at the room's 20 °C. The engine solves the conduction by backward Euler on a grid fine at the face, in 0.01 s steps.
- **Thermal properties [M].**
  - Paper through its thickness: k = 0.08 W/mK (printing paper; copy papers 0.09; fibreboard 0.12 through the thickness; *Thermal properties of copy paper sheets*; the hot-wire study of paper and corrugated fibreboard). Heat capacity 1.34 kJ/kgK (paper solids; OSTI 2531102, conductive drying of paper and board). Density from the sheet's own grammage and caliper.
  - Board: paperboard at 0.072 W/mK and 480 kg/m³ (IES VE materials table), with the paper solids' heat capacity, 20 mm deep (the heat reaches about 6 mm in ten minutes).
- **What it gives [D].** At 200 mm the face reaches 58 °C in a second and creeps toward the fog's dew point (67 °C). The water condensed is 3.6 µm (g/m²) in 1 s, 9.5 in 5 s, 14 in 10 s, 25 in 30 s and 35 in a minute. Once the face is near the dew point, the water is the heat the board takes over the latent heat, 2e ΔT √(t/π) for a slab of effusivity e held ΔT warmer: nearly √t (`steam_condenses_as_the_board_takes_its_heat` checks it against that closed form). A sheet with air behind it (on a strainer, Cassatt's later mount) would stop condensing once the sheet itself warmed, at a few µm. Mounts aren't modelled (gap 7).
- **Left out:** the vapour the fibres absorb directly (sizing blocks liquid, not vapour; the walls take water over tens of seconds); the water's own heat capacity; the faster first evaporation of a warm face after the jet moves on.

## 3. Where the water goes, and the gum

- **The wet pastel switch's water [D, reused].** The condensate is laid as `wet_pastel::add_water`. The pastel's pores (r ≈ 0.7 µm) draw harder than the paper's (r ≈ 6 µm), so they fill first. What is left stands on the sheet and sinks in at the sheet's measured sizing rate (Cobb 25 g/m² on the default sheet: 3.2 µm in the first second, 25 in a minute), as #322 does with a wash. On a sized sheet the condensate first stands as drops: water's contact angle on a Cobb 25–48 sheet is about 105° (IPPTA 2007, no. 4, art. 15) [M], so condensation on it is dropwise (breath figures).
- **The gum softens and dissolves [D].** At the face's water activity of 1 the gum's glass transition falls far below room temperature (gum arabic: 62 °C at a_w 0.33, 42.6 °C at 0.54, Righetto & Netto 2005, `charcoal::gum_tg_c`), so it is rubbery, then in solution. Gum arabic dissolves to about 50 % w/w, so 1/30 of the pores' water dissolves all of a stick's 1.5 % gum (wet_pastel.md §1). Any steam that wets the pastel past a saturation of about 0.03 therefore dissolves all of it, and the engine takes the wet pastel switch's dissolution as it is. Gum tragacanth (only its tragacanthin, 30–40 %, dissolves; the bassorin swells) or methyl cellulose (it gels above about 50 °C, so it stays out of solution while the face is hot and dissolves as it cools) would bring less gum, or bring it later. Under #322's binding threshold (a share of 0.005 of the gum is enough to bind against a finger) all three bind the same, so the engine keeps one binder.
- **Binding [D, reused].** As the water dries on the clock (#322's psychrometrics), the gum left sets at the contacts and binds the pastel where it kept at least the threshold share (`wet_pastel::binds`). What sank into the sheet took its share of the gum. A steamed passage of loose pastel is bound once dry: in the engine's test the finger moves an eighth as much of it as of the unsteamed half, and the eraser lifts less than a quarter as much (`steamed_pastel_once_dry_lifts_and_smudges_less`). Binding is "partial" in two ways: by the mask (a soft edge gets less water, and pastel outside the jet gets none), and where so much water sank that too little gum was left. It is #322's all-or-nothing threshold per cell, so a cell is either bound or loose.
- **Nothing moves sideways.** Steam carries no pigment. The wet pastel is #322's paste to a wet brush (`b:wash`), the gesture Degas used to work a steamed passage. A finger in wet paste isn't modelled (#322).
- **Darkening.** While wet the pastel darkens by #322's Mie law. Once dry it is back within a few percent of its colour. The paper's own darkening when wet isn't modelled (#322).

## 4. Crushed pastel and the sieve (`ggs_finer`)

- **Crushing [M, E].** Crushed brittle materials follow the Gates–Gaudin–Schuhmann law: the mass finer than d is (d/D)^m. The modulus m falls with hardness: 0.9 for quartz, 0.3 for gypsum (Abouzeid 1994, *Powder Handling and Processing*, via Clark 2005, *Chemical Processing*). Fits to other crushed products run 0.7–2.4 (911Metallurgist's summary of impact crushing; a worked sieve example, 0.73). A pastel stick is a gum-bound chalk compact far weaker than gypsum (σt ≈ 0.2 MPa, wet_pastel.md §3), so `GGS_M` = 0.3 [E: the softest value reported; no crushed pastel was measured]. A larger m would leave fewer fines and let a tap shed more (at m = 0.9, 89 % of a 0.5 mm sift at 100 g).
- **The sieve.** Rubbed through a sieve, every crumb passes below the aperture D (`grain`). If the crushing's own top size is larger, what passes is the same law's lower part, so (d/D)^m holds below D. The finest is the primary particles, whiting's d₃₂ of 1.7 µm (wet_pastel.md §1). Crumbs under 5 µm (the engine's smallest crumb, `pastel::CRUMB_MIN_UM`) settle into the tooth's pores as a deposit does: (5/500)^0.3 = 25 % of the mass for a 0.5 mm sieve. The rest lies on the tooth, its mass-mean size 154 µm for a 0.5 mm sieve.
- **The fall.** The crumbs fall one by one into the pixels under the mask, by size bins (Poisson counts, 12 log bins). The total is scaled to what was sifted, so the mass is exact while the scatter is random: a coarse sieve speckles, a fine one veils. A crumb lying on the tooth covers its own section (a sphere covers 2d/3 of its volume as a layer), never more than a stick's deposit (`PARTICLE_UM`). The crumbs on the tooth are recorded beside the drawing: their volume as crushed, as they lie, the largest crumb there, the clusters' size, and the share held. The record is empty in every painting that never sifted.

## 5. How crumbs are held, dry and wet (`dry_fall_um`, `wet_fall_um`)

- **Dry [M, reused].** A loose grain or crumb is held to what it lies on by 10–150 nN per contact, about the same for every size above a micrometre (pastel_brushing.md §1; sheet.rs `ADHESION_N` = 50 nN). Chalk's pull-off doesn't grow with humidity (limestone, Jones et al. 2003, 10–90 % RH), so charcoal's humid water bridge (charcoal.md §4) isn't added for chalk. A tap at a gravities sheds every crumb of d > (6F/πρa)^⅓ (ρ 1250 kg/m³, a half-porous crumb), as `tap` already does: 43 µm at 100 g, 27 µm at 400 g, and 0.2 mm for a sheet tilted upright (1 g). With a 0.5 mm sieve a tap at 100 g sheds 52 % of what was sifted, the crumbs between 43 µm and 0.5 mm. The fines and the small crumbs stay, a ghost of the colour (`sifted_pastel_on_a_dry_sheet_mostly_taps_off`). A puff moves dry sifted crumbs as it moves heaped pastel (sheet.rs's Shao & Lu threshold): the crumbs lie on the tooth whatever the pores hold.
- **Wet [D].** On a damp sheet the crumbs' pores draw water from what stands and from the sheet beneath (they pull harder than the sheet's pores, #322 §4), and from a damp sheet's own free water (`crate::damp`). A crumb in contact with a wetted sheet is held by a liquid bridge. For a sphere on a flat with both faces wetted the bridge holds with 4πγR (cos θ ≈ 1; Israelachvili, *Intermolecular and Surface Forces*; Butt & Kappl 2009, *Adv. Colloid Interface Sci.* 146:48). This is the same law as charcoal's water bridge (4πγa at an asperity, charcoal.md §4), with the bridge grown to the crumb's own radius once a film stands thicker than the tooth's roughness. With γ = 50 mN/m (the pores' gum solution, #322) and a wet crumb of 1750 kg/m³, a tap at a gravities leaves every crumb of d² < 12γ/(ρ g a): 0.59 mm at 100 g, larger than the sieve's crumbs. While wet, a damp sift stays.
- **Dried [D, reused].** As the cell's water dries the crumbs' dissolved gum sets with the rest (`wet_pastel::binds`), and the record marks them held. Fixative marks them held too. Held crumbs never tap or blow off.

## 6. Clusters (`cluster`)

- **The film breaks into drops [M].** Water standing on a partly wetting sheet (a sized sheet, 105°) dries as drops. Condensation itself lays it as drops (breath figures). Coalescing drops reach a self-similar pattern that covers a constant share of the surface: about 0.5 in simulations on every substrate (Int. J. Heat Mass Transfer 2011, S0017931011000032), 0.55 in Beysens et al.'s θ = 90° breath figures (Beysens, Fritter, Roux, Knobler & Viovy; Fritter, Knobler & Beysens 1991, *Phys. Rev. A* 43:2858), and 0.45 on an insect wing. `DROP_COVER` = 0.5. A drop of contact radius R holds π R³ f(θ)/3, with f(θ) = (2 − 3 cos θ + cos³ θ)/sin³ θ, so a film of mean height h̄ is drops of R = 3h̄/(0.5 f(θ)): 2 µm for a 1 µm film, 10 µm for 5 µm, 39 µm for 20 µm. On an unsized sheet (wetted) the film doesn't bead; drops at 30° stand in for it [E: no breath-figure coverage for a wetting, absorbing surface was found, and an unsized sheet drinks a film within milliseconds anyway].
- **A drop gathers the crumbs smaller than itself [D].** Particles in a film thinner than themselves deform its surface, and the menisci pull them together with the long-ranged lateral capillary ("immersion") force (Kralchevsky & Nagayama 1994, *Langmuir* 10:23; the force behind convective assembly, Denkov et al. 1993, *Nature* 361:26). As a drop shrinks it draws in the crumbs inside it and dries into one island. The crumbs it takes are those smaller than itself (to 2R) whose centres fall within R + d/2 of its centre. Larger crumbs drink the drops they touch and stay single.
- **Island size [D].** The small crumbs (mass-mean d over 1.7 µm to 2R, fines included) cover φ = v_s/(2d/3) of the sheet, so an island a crumb thick has area φ π (R + d/2)², and diameter 2√φ (R + d/2), never less than one crumb. With 2.2 µm of stick volume sifted on the 150 mm sheet: about 10 µm clusters over a 4.4 µm film, 27 µm over 19 µm. With 10 µm sifted, 23 µm over 5 µm and 60 µm over 20 µm. They grow with the film, as R does, and with the sift's density (`sifted_pastel_on_a_damp_sheet_stays_in_clusters_that_grow_with_the_water`). "Minute clusters" [P] is what the Met conservator saw; no size was given.
- **No film, no clusters.** If the crumbs drink all the standing water, or draw only on water already in the sheet, they stick singly. On a sized sheet steam's drops sink within half a minute (Cobb 25: 14 µm in about 20 s), so clusters need a sift straight after steaming, as Cassatt seems to have done. The easel's hand time for steaming a whole sheet is minutes, so a whole-sheet steam leaves a damp sheet, not drops.
- **Left out:** clusters spanning pixels (each falls into one pixel); crumbs gathered by drops on wet pastel rather than paper (the paper's angle is used); the crumbs a later stick or finger presses into the tooth (the record keeps them lying until a tool takes them, and only then follows); coffee-ring edges of pinned drops.

## 7. Estimates, with why

| Constant | Value | Why it is an estimate | Bounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| `KETTLE_W` | 1 kW into the water | a period stove's or lamp's output isn't recorded | 0.5–2.5 kW |
| `SPOUT_MM` | 10 mm | domestic spouts; no period kettle measured | 8–12 mm |
| stagnation velocity gradient | centreline velocity / half-width | the order of an impinging jet's strain; not measured at 20 diameters | ×0.5–2 (h ×0.7–1.4); condensation barely moves |
| `GGS_M` | 0.3 | no crushed pastel measured; softest reported value | 0.3–0.9 (a tap at 100 g sheds 52–89 % of a 0.5 mm sift) |
| wetted-sheet drop angle | 30° | no coverage measured for a wetting, absorbing sheet | only standing films on unsized sheets |
| `steam` distance default | 200 mm | a gesture | 100–600 mm |
| `sift` grain default | 0.5 mm | a kitchen or tea strainer | 0.05–5 mm |

Measured or derived and reused: the jet laws (Chen & Rodi; Pope), Sibulkin's law, paper's and board's thermal properties, the wet pastel switch's water, gum and binding, the dry adhesion of a grain (sheet.rs), the liquid bridge (Israelachvili), breath-figure coverage, the sized sheet's angle, and the studio.
