# Soak-stain on raw canvas: materials and physics (research for the `pour` verb)

Sources are listed at the end; [n] marks them. "Estimate" marks numbers not taken from a source.

## What the painters did
- Frankenthaler, *Mountains and Sea* (1952): turpentine-thinned oil paint poured
  "from coffee cans onto an unprimed canvas on the floor"; "the paint completely
  permeated the canvas"; "the weave of the canvas emphasizing the flatness" [1][2].
  Methods also included blotting with sponges/rags and layering after drying, and
  tilting or rotating the canvas to steer the flow [3].
- Thinned oil on raw canvas leaves "residual, halo-like marks of oil" around the
  colour; she moved to acrylic in 1962-63 partly because it "didn't leave" them [4].
  "The sinking of oil into unprimed canvas can lead to ... darkening, and halos of
  oil staining where raw canvas is exposed" [5]. Oil "will crawl to the back of the
  canvas and pool around the colors" [5].
- Morris Louis (Magna acrylic resin thinned with turpentine on unprimed cotton
  duck) [6]:
  - lighter, more porous No. 12 duck let the paint "penetrate the canvas rapidly";
  - sizing the canvas "produced pastel colors that remain on the surface rather than
    soaking through";
  - excess medium gave "a darkened halo around the color";
  - "an excess of turpentine thinner produced a bleeding effect in which the pigment
    feathers out along the edges of the pour";
  - turpentine thinning has "a dulling effect" and a matte surface; thinned so far
    that "the pigment particles became too separated to form a continuous film",
    bright flecks remain;
  - dark washes over bright ones: the surface "glows with the warmth of the
    underlying layers";
  - ~29 parts thinner (resin + turpentine) to 1 part paint over a year's supplies;
  - a fan to control drying time; tilting the stretcher and varying tautness to
    steer the pours.

## Capillary physics in woven cloth
- Lucas–Washburn: front distance h = sqrt(2K't), K' = r·γ·cosθ/(4μ) [7]. The rate
  falls with viscosity μ and rises with pore radius r and surface tension γ.
- A drop spreading in fabric: area ∝ sqrt(t) while liquid remains on the surface
  (phase I); a slower law once all liquid is inside (phase II) [8].
- Woven cloth is a dual-porosity medium: imbibition inside the yarns (minipores)
  and between them (megapores), largely independent; "weave intersections do not
  allow cross imbibition", giving an anisotropic, four-petal pattern along warp
  and weft at small scale [9][10][11].
- On cotton weaves the wetted trace is "usually elliptical", the long axis along
  the warp; coarser weft shortens it (higher warp take-up) [12]. Warp and weft
  wicking rates differ "slightly", sometimes weft faster (tension differences) [7].
- Particles in liquid draining into a porous substrate are filtered by the pores;
  draining plus evaporation with a pinned edge give a ring deposit (coffee stain)
  at the edge; too-large pores let particles travel with the liquid instead [13].
  Watercolour drops on dry paper form ring stains "regardless of the nature of the
  pigment" [14].

## Optics
- Rough, porous, weakly absorbing materials are darker when wet: liquid in the
  air gaps lowers the relative refractive index and adds total internal reflection,
  so light scatters more times and is absorbed more [15][16]. Cotton fits ("cotton
  tick[s] both boxes") [17]. Turpentine evaporates, so its darkening is temporary;
  oil stays, so the oil halo is a permanent darkening (and linseed oil yellows).
- Refractive indices (estimate from general knowledge, not from the sources above):
  cellulose ~1.53-1.56, linseed oil ~1.48, turpentine ~1.47, water 1.33.

## Numbers used as estimates (not from the sources)
- Viscosity: turpentine ~1.4 mPa·s, raw linseed oil ~30-50 mPa·s; mixtures by
  log-mixing; pigment raises viscosity (Krieger–Dougherty).
- Pore volume of cotton duck: ~0.6 mm thick, porosity ~0.6 → ~0.35 mm³ of liquid
  per mm² (about 14 of the engine's 25 µm coats).
- Turpentine leaves thin fabric in tens of minutes to hours; oil keeps wicking
  slowly for hours to days until it gels.
