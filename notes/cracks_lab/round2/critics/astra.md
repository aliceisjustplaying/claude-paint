Confidence: moderate overall; D versus E is a close judgment.

## 2. What looks synthetic

- **Pattern and spacing:** A’s long runs have too consistent a rhythm; D and E retain that tendency despite more wandering. B escapes the rails but substitutes conspicuously broad, rounded cells. C is less regimented, not fully convincing (`A–E_sky.png`).
- **Straightness and parallelism:** Straight stretches are not inherently wrong. The giveaway is repeated long stretches with similar orientation, separated by comparable gaps and occasionally joined like ladder rungs (`A_tree.png`, `E_sky.png`).
- **Width and contrast:** All five look predominantly like fine gray strokes with limited visible variation in opening width. Curves often feel smoothly drawn rather than fractured. Low contrast is welcome, but does not by itself make a line physically credible (`A–E_tree.png`).
- **Distribution:** Cracks remain broadly spread through the pale sky and snow rather than forming strongly differentiated neighborhoods. Dark tree passages suppress their visibility, although some lines remain traceable across the wood; these images cannot establish whether that is masking or simply contrast (`B_tree.png`, `C_tree.png`, all whole views).
- **Scale:** At whole-image size, A still advertises a directional overlay. B and C integrate better, but their large-scale network lacks a convincing hierarchy of major fractures and localized subsidiary cracking (`A–C_whole.png`).

## 3. Comparison with Friedrich-era canvas

These are expectations, not universal period signatures; no real reference painting was supplied.

- **Lights versus darks — high confidence:** Dark fissures are generally easier to see against pale paint. Dark paint can crack too, with visibility depending on exposed underlying material, illumination and surface relief. “Dark equals uncracked” would be a mistake.
- **Thin versus thick — high confidence in variability, moderate in prediction:** A thin sky layer can display cracks originating in the ground. Thicker passages can develop different spacing or opening widths, but thickness alone does not predict damage: pigment, binder, underlying layers and treatment history matter. None of these images demonstrates that material dependence.
- **Clustering — moderate confidence:** Expect locally dense subdivisions beside quieter expanses, not merely randomized line positions. Connected fractures share a stress history. Some genuine paintings nevertheless have extensive, fairly even networks.
- **Direction — high confidence generally, low for any specific Friedrich here:** Canvas structure and stress can bias orientation. A slight downward-right drift is plausible, but should allow local departures, branching and changes in spacing rather than impose one global comb.
- **Close appearance — high confidence generally:** Real fissures have irregular edges, variable openings, junctions and tapering ends. Some show minute edge relief or exposed ground; others remain nearly flat hairlines. These crops mainly convey lines, not those distinctions.

## 4. Changes, most important first

1. Introduce correlated dense patches, quiet areas and several fracture scales; vary total damage independently between paintings.
2. Keep C’s weaker directional tendency, with locally changing orientation and less persistent parallel spacing.
3. Build connected fractures with unequal subdivisions, terminating branches and occasional angular kinks instead of uniformly smooth strands.
4. Let paint-layer properties affect fracture behavior; separate actual cracking from its visibility against local color.
5. Vary opening width and edge irregularity subtly. Add relief only where warranted, not a universal bright outline or embossed effect.
