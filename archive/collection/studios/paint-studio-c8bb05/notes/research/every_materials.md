# The paints in the box

*A technical report from documented sources. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified or
rest on one account. It describes the 54 tubes of this box as paints: what
each pigment is and what is documented about laying and mixing it in oil.*


## Summary

The box holds every tube the easel knows, from old pigments (lead white,
vermilion, smalt, the earths, verdigris) to the cadmiums, the cobalt colors,
the chromate yellows, the iron-oxide Mars colors and a modern quinacridone. Some are fast driers (the lead colors, the umbers, the copper and
cobalt colors), some slow (ivory
black, the cadmiums, bone brown, bitumen). Several are transparent
lakes or glazing colors; several are documented to fade, darken or change
color with time (§8). The easel guide's tube table gives each tube's
hiding, stiffness, tinting strength and drying as the easel models them;
those numbers are estimates from the pigment literature, not measurements. Fading, darkening and color change with age are not modeled at
the easel.

**Terms.** *Masstone*: the color of the paint laid thick. *Hiding*: how
much one coat covers what is under it. *Tinting strength*: how far a
pigment carries its color into a mixture. *Lake*: a dye fixed on a
colorless base, a transparent pigment. *Glaze*: a transparent layer.
*Scumble*: a thin, semi-opaque layer dragged over dry paint. *Ground*: the
preparatory layer between canvas and paint. *Traction crackle*: wide
cracks where a layer shrinks over paint that was not yet dry. *Fugitive*:
fading in light.

## 4. The paints in the box

### Whites

- **lead white** (basic lead carbonate). Lead "produces a uniquely strong
  paint film, and dries rapidly"; lead white is a rapid dryer. Period tube paints held "linseed oil in the darker colours and
  poppyseed oil in white paint". It is the
  white of documented grounds: gray grounds of lead white and ivory black
  in linseed oil, lead white with calcite
  and a little black, or pink with red iron oxide. Documented as a final scumble over toning layers.
  Aged films: lead white shrank 0.5% over 21–31 years, and its strain at
  break fell from 7.3% at 0.2 years to under 1%. Lead-soap
  protrusions can form in a lead-white ground.
- **zinc white** (zinc oxide). In oil it "tends … to form hard, often
  brittle films" that crack over softer layers. Aged zinc white
  films break at 0.1–0.3% strain, the most brittle white measured; it
  forms brittle soaps with the oil. A canvas of 1935 whose paint
  included zinc white shows old flaking, fractures through and between
  layers and zinc soaps. *Uncertain* (read through a summary). Period
  tube colors sometimes held a little zinc white inside them.

### Yellows

- **chrome yellow** (lead chromate). Made commercially in Germany only from
  1820. It darkens with age. A manual of
  1887 rejects it: "its stability is too doubtful". With
  Prussian blue it makes chrome green; with
  cobalt blue it gives greens too.
- **lemon chrome** (pale lead chromate with lead sulfate). The pale grade
  is coprecipitated with lead sulfate, which makes it hide and
  tint less than chrome yellow. A "pale lemon chrome" was in a
  paint box of about 1884–88.
- **strontium yellow** (strontium chromate). A lead-free chromate yellow of
  the 1850s–80s; measured as a dry powder, it tints at about 78% of lead
  chromate. Found in French paint of the 1880s.
- **barium yellow** (barium chromate, "lemon yellow"). Measured as a dry
  powder, it tints at about 92% of lead chromate. It turns green with
  age. Documented used together with zinc yellow.
- **zinc yellow** (potassium zinc chromate). Measured as a dry powder, it
  tints at about 65% of lead chromate. It browns or turns green with
  age, to a dichromate brown or a chromium-oxide green.
- **lead-tin yellow** (lead-tin oxide). Its hiding, body and drying in the
  catalog are sourced: an opaque, stiff paint that dries
  at lead white's rate. Modern tubes of this name vary.
- **Naples yellow** (lead antimonate). "In use since antiquity until about
  1850"; what a later tube of this name held is *unverified*. A lead
  compound; its drying between average and lead white's is an estimate. Documented in green mixtures with blues.
- **pale cadmium**, **cadmium yellow** and **deep cadmium** (cadmium
  sulfide in three grades). Cadmium pigments were in use "since about 1840" and sold in England by 1846; the names ran from pale
  yellow to "orange" for the darker shades. "Good hiding power"; one maker rates it opaque. The cadmiums are "slow but
  reliable driers". Documented as a scumbling color.
  Emerald green blackens with cadmium sulfide. Deep cadmium's hue (a
  deep yellow or orange) is *inferred*.
- **Indian yellow** (magnesium and calcium euxanthate). Its particles are
  "uniformly clear and transparent". An early account has it
  drying in oil "nearly as soon or sooner than" other colors.
  *Uncertain.* Sold as "genuine Indian yellow" in the 1880s.
- **yellow lake** (flavonoid dye, such as weld or quercitron, on alumina and
  chalk). A yellow lake and an organic yellow are found in late
  19th-century French paintings. Fugitive. Yellow lakes
  of the brown-pink kind are "very bad driers" in oil. Its
  numbers are estimates.
- **Mars yellow** (synthetic iron oxide hydroxide). Mars colors are "good
  driers for oil paints" and "dry well in proportion to their
  depth". The catalog's grade is a modern opaque one (estimate).
- **transparent oxide yellow** (transparent synthetic iron oxide). Little is
  documented: its numbers are all estimates.

### Oranges

- **orange chrome** (basic lead chromate). Its hiding, tinting strength and
  drying in the catalog are sourced: a covering orange
  that dries at chrome yellow's rate. Documented as a scumbling color.
- **Mars orange** (synthetic iron oxide, an orange grade). "A subdued
  orange of the burnt Sienna class, but without the brown tinge … with
  much transparency". Hence much less hiding than the other Mars
  colors.
- **red lead** (lead tetroxide). It "exerts a powerful action on drying
  oils"; the catalog dries it faster than lead white. Found in late
  19th-century French oils and in one painting of 1885–86.
- **orange vermilion** (mercuric sulfide, a yellower grade). A grade of
  vermilion listed by name in a palette of 1887. The shade is
  inferred from the name. *Uncertain.* Body and drying as vermilion.
- (deep cadmium: see the cadmiums under Yellows.)

### Reds

- **vermilion** (mercuric sulfide). Documented mixed with lead white, in "toning layers" with ochers under a red lake glaze, as small admixtures and with smalt in a thin violet
  layer. In paintings analyzed from the 1880s it was mostly
  bound in linseed oil. A slow drier in the catalog (estimate).
- **Chinese vermilion** (mercuric sulfide, a deeper grade). The second
  named grade in the same palette; otherwise as vermilion. Little else is documented.
- **cadmium red** (cadmium sulfoselenide). "An opaque scarlet"; low
  oil content rated low by one maker, so a stiff paint; slow but reliable drying, like the other cadmiums.
- **Mars red** (synthetic iron oxide). A good drier, as the Mars colors are. Opaque in the catalog (a modern grade; estimate).
- **rose madder** (madder lake on alumina). A transparent lake. Purpurin-rich
  madders made from 1861 had "about fifty times the tinting strength" of
  older ones. Madder lakes fade: one shows fading in
  cross-section, another faded where a frame did not cover it, and carmine and madder fade. It stays workable on
  the palette "for many days". Documented uses: a pure red
  lake glazed over opaque toning layers; thin unmixed
  overpaint that leaves lower layers showing; a madder
  mixed with megilp laid thickly over opaque paint. In
  paintings analyzed from the 1880s red lakes were mostly in linseed oil.
- **permanent alizarin** (a quinacridone). The exact pigment behind the name
  is not stated. *Uncertain.* A different pigment from rose
  madder. Little else is documented.
- **carmine lake** (carminic acid from cochineal, on alumina). Cochineal as
  well as madder lakes are found in French paint of the late 19th century. It fades. Its numbers are estimates.
- **magenta** (fuchsine, an aniline dye, laked on alumina). A London
  colorman's "Magenta" of 1896 was an "Aniline Lake". It fades
  in light: "a want of permanence has been fatal to their success. Mauve is
  more durable than magenta". Transparent and strong in the catalog
  (estimates).

### Violets

- **cobalt violet** (cobalt phosphate or arsenate). "Less commonly" found,
  costly and weak. The catalog dries it at cobalt blue's rate, cobalt
  pigments being siccative in oil.

### Blues

- **smalt** (cobalt potash glass, coarse). Semi-transparent and weak; documented as chosen "on account of its semi-transparent
  property". About 3 wt% cobalt. Used alone or with
  lead white, in grades from pale gray to deep blue. It can lose
  its color and turn brown over time. Cobalt blue "almost
  entirely replaced smalt… after about 1820".
- **pale smalt** (a paler grade of smalt). One of smalt's documented grades; otherwise as smalt.
- **cobalt blue** (cobalt aluminate). Sold in "an intense and dark grade". Documented as a glazing color laid over sufficiently dry paint and, with chrome yellow, in mixed greens.
- **cerulean blue** (cobalt stannate). "A light, greenish blue" and "an
  opaque, greenish blue"; one maker rates it opaque, with low oil
  content. Used from 1860.
- **ultramarine blue** (synthetic ultramarine). Tinting strength "quite
  good"; the catalog's masstone is converted from a published
  measurement. Sold as "permanent blue", and in a
  darker, more transparent grade. A model ultramarine oil paint had
  a yield stress of only 10–20 Pa, and its brushstrokes came out regular and
  low in relief.
- **ultramarine ash** (natural ultramarine, a pale last extraction). The
  last extraction, "containing a high proportion of colorless material and
  both few and small blue particles … because of its high degree of
  transparency, was valued as a pale blue glazing pigment". A
  London colorman listed it 1892–1916.
- **Prussian blue** (iron ferrocyanide). Transparent and very strong. Used "very often" for mixed greens, with yellow ochre,
  Naples yellow or chrome yellow.
- **Antwerp blue** (Prussian blue on an alumina base). A Prussian blue on a
  white base: "less transparent and less intense … than Prussian blue".

### Greens

- **viridian** (hydrated chromium oxide). "A deep blackish green in oil", with low hiding. Made c.1838, patented 1859,
  earliest finds 1863; widespread from the 1860s. Sometimes mixed with emerald green. Its French
  name is *vert émeraude*.
- **emerald green** (copper aceto-arsenite). Made from 1814, sold in tubes
  from 1832. Copper pigments promote drying. It blackens with cadmium sulfide.
- **copper green** (verdigris ground in oil). "Poor hiding power in oil"; copper is a drier. "Rarely found in eighteenth-
  and nineteenth-century paintings"; around 1816 still made as green
  "varnishes". Aged verdigris films shrank 1.7%, the most
  of the paints measured, and break at 0.1% strain.
- **Rinmann's green** (cobalt-zinc oxide). Semi-transparent, weak and
  permanent. Known from 1780; "poor tinting strength and high
  cost… kept it in limited use".
- **green earth** (celadonite and glauconite clay). Translucent, weak and
  short of body; "very weak tinting strength …
  transparent". Its masstone is Munsell 7.5G/2.9/1.5.
  Ancient, and in painters' manuals through the 19th century. Documented as a glaze.

### Earths and browns

- **yellow ochre** (hydrated iron oxide earth). A natural iron-oxide earth
  (goethite). Documented in toning layers with
  vermilion, in scumbles and in mixed greens
  with blues. Ocher is also found in the lower layers of
  commercial grounds.
- **brown ochre** (iron oxide earth, a darker grade). A natural iron-oxide
  earth. Little else is documented; its numbers copy yellow
  ochre's.
- **raw sienna** (sienna earth, unroasted). Documented in glazes. The siennas are rapid dryers.
- **burnt sienna** (roasted sienna earth). One maker rates it opaque, but
  siennas are semitransparent in thin films. *Uncertain.* A rapid
  dryer.
- **red earth** (iron oxide earth). Hematite; "light red" is a
  red iron oxide. Documented as a transparent stain on white
  canvas, "not enough to lose the sense of transparency," letting the red
  "come through as a part of the color". *Uncertain* (one
  account). Burnt red earth is found in the lower layers of grounds. Aged red iron oxide films shrank about 0%.
- **Indian red** (nearly pure ferric oxide). A natural haematite,
  purplish, and a separate pigment from Mars red. Little else is
  documented.
- **raw umber** (iron and manganese oxide earth). Umbers are rapid dryers; with white it "will tend to dry v quickly". Documented as
  a light-brown canvas stain, for outlining with "some strong
  drier" and in glazes.
- **Mars brown** (synthetic iron oxide, roasted). A good drier, as the Mars
  colors are, though without the manganese that makes umber
  fast. Little else is documented.
- **bone brown** (bone roasted until brown). Bone "roast[ed] … until by
  partial charring [it] become[s] of a brown colour throughout"; bone and
  ivory browns are "bad driers in oil"; "the palest of these colours are
  the most opaque".
- **bitumen** (asphaltum). Transparent. It slows linseed oil's
  drying and never fully cures. Traction crackle and "crawl" of
  paint in one canvas are attributed to "bitumen or a resinous admixture", and "traction crackle is found in thicker areas of brown
  paint". The easel models no traction crackle: here the tube
  is only slow and transparent.

### Blacks

- **bone black** (charred bone: carbon and calcium phosphate). Ivory black
  is "notoriously slow" to dry. With lead white it made the gray
  commercial grounds of the period.
  Documented in glazes. Blacks were
  usually bound in linseed oil in paintings of 1869 to the 1880s.
- **vine black** (charcoal of vine twigs). Charcoal black is found with bone
  black in paintings of 1877–1906. Its numbers are estimates.

## 8. Combinations and cautions

- **Emerald green and the cadmiums.** Emerald green blackens with cadmium
  sulfide.
- **Mixed greens.** Greens were often mixed from blue and yellow: Prussian
  blue "very often," smalt and cobalt blue too, with yellow ochre, Naples
  yellow or chrome yellow, sometimes in "several (up to four) layers of
  different greens". Chrome green is chrome yellow with
  Prussian blue. Viridian and emerald green were mixed; barium and zinc yellow were used together.
- **Colors that change.** Zinc yellow browns or turns green; carmine and
  madder fade; barium yellow turns green; chrome yellow darkens.
  Magenta fades; smalt can lose its color or brown. A
  green mixed with a fugitive yellow turned blue where light reached it. None of this happens at the easel.
- **Fast over slow.** A fast-drying earth brown over a slower-drying paint
  gives a crack pattern. Traction crackle forms where a layer
  goes over paint not yet dry, and in thick brown or bituminous
  paint. Zinc white's hard films crack over softer
  layers.
- **Drying.** Earth tones (umbers, siennas) and lead white dry fast, ivory
  black slowly; drying also depends on "thickness of paint layer"
  and the medium, and "even the fast drying pigments won't really
  dry during a working session". Lead colors and copper colors
  speed drying; the cadmiums are slow;
  bone brown and bitumen are poor driers.
- **Oils.** Drying and yellowing run linseed, then walnut, then poppy;
  the colormen ground light colors in poppy oil and poor driers in linseed. Linseed is "the most common and the fastest drying … (also forms
  the most durable paint films, but is more prone to yellowing)".
- **Driers.** Oil cooked with litharge, or the siccatives of Courtrai or
  Haarlem, dries much faster but darker and more brittle with too much. Courtrai was "heavily loaded with compounds of lead"; Haarlem was
  copal in oil of spike. Lead white with litharge shrank more and
  aged stiffer than lead white alone (0.9% against 0.5%).
- **Fillers.** Period tube paints held "easily detectable quantities of
  extenders such as kaolin, chalk, talc and barytes"; chalk made
  up 20–47% of some paints analyzed.
- **Bloom.** Dark paint can "become milky or bloomed," linked to glossy dark
  brown tube paints.
