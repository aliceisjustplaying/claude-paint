# How Friedrich painted: process and hand

*Round 7 research (branch r7-process). Scope: how he worked, in what order and
by what hand, from written sources only (conservation reports, technical
bulletins, contemporaries' accounts). Composition and motifs are the other
stream's subject. This note deepens and corrects
`notes/research/friedrich_materials.md` (materials, pigments, grounds) and
`notes/friedrich.md`; it does not repeat them. Source keys are in brackets;
full URLs are at the end. Keys shared with the materials note mean the same
source. [WP-…] marks German Wikipedia articles: secondary sources, used only
where they report a primary source (a visitor's letter, an IRR finding) and
flagged so.*

Every section ends with **Sim:** what the finding means for the engine:
**now** (possible with today's physics and verbs, and how a painter does
it), **new** (needs a physical capability or constraint, in the spirit of
`notes/principles.md`) or **habit** (a painter's habit for the
sketchbook).

## Bottom line

1. **Composed in the head; built from exact studies.** Friedrich invented
   the whole picture without compositional sketches [KÖR p.285; NG p.49],
   but its parts come nearly verbatim from pencil studies made outdoors,
   **enlarged mechanically** (a pantograph, in small whole-number ratios)
   and **transferred with tracings or cartoons** [MOST-abs; WP-ZM]. Carus's
   "never made sketches, cartoons…" is "misleading" [NG p.49].
2. **Drawing first, then fully.** Light chalk or pencil, then the whole
   picture drawn "properly and definitely" in pen and ink or with a thin
   brush, down to grass and leaves [NG pp.49, 58; CATS p.132]. The paint
   "obeys the outlines of the underdrawing" [NG p.49]. He then **removes**
   drawn things while painting, rarely adds [ART].
3. **Sepia method in oil.** A thin brown underpainting like a wash over the
   drawing, then one or two very thin layers, glazes over a light ground,
   with body color only in lights, snow and last accents [NG p.49; KÖR
   fig.1; WP-CDF; NG p.51].
4. **Each passage by its own hand.** Stippled skies and mist, short hatched
   firs, fine upturned grass laid last, pastose snow, dry-brushed ice, bare
   ground left standing, adjacent color fields that **do not even touch**
   [NG pp.50, 56; HH].
5. **Slow, in states, over months.** The Monk's sky was seen in three
   different states over 18 months and finished just before shipping
   [WP-Monk, quoting Semler, H. von Kügelgen and Frommann]; the picture was
   first laid in dark blue and repainted light in the middle [SMB-blog].
6. **The irregularity is layered, physical and mostly unintended.** Knife
   waves and brush striations in the bought ground, a stray scratch he
   ignored [KÖR p.284], coarse smalt specks [HH; MÄD p.102], ground through
   thin paint, pen lines broken by the surface [NG p.58], rubbed-off guide
   lines, pentimenti, then two centuries of cleaning, lining and retouching
   [SMB-blog; NG p.55].
7. **What copies and restorers got wrong is evenness.** The Dortmund copy
   has no underdrawing, "a more even paint surface" and passages "rapidly
   applied" [NG p.50]; restorers retouched "rather larger than the losses"
   and could hardly match the translucent stipple [NG p.55].

## 0. Before the canvas: studies, notes and the idea

- **Outdoor studies, about 1000 surviving.** "Some 1000 drawings, most of
  them made outdoors," record "individual stones, roots or branches with
  great precision" [KÖR p.285]. "Approximately one half" come from
  sketchbooks [KH pp.75–76], about 20 of them, of which only the Oslo
  sketchbook of 1807 survives complete [WP-CDF].
- **Notes written on the studies.** He wrote "in der Ferne" (in the
  distance) beside trees [KH pp.75–76], marked a ground line ("Vorgrund") and
  the horizon line even on studies of roots [KH p.77] and **numbered
  mountain ranges** "decreasing from front to back"; when a study became a
  painting, "these sets of numbers allowed him to render the effects of
  aerial perspective" [KH p.77]. He also wrote **color values** into
  drawings meant for paintings [ART, on Mösl p.126].
- **Oil studies were rare but existed.** Studies of ice floes "in oil on
  canvas scraps" for *The Sea of Ice* [WP-Eismeer]. Carus: "no … colour
  studies" [NG p.49] is too strong.
- **The idea came whole.** Carus: "He never began a painting until it stood
  lifelike before his mind" [NG p.49]. To Zhukovsky: "what my mind was
  looking for stands before me like an apparition – at once I seize my
  pencil and draw; the main thing has been done" [NG p.49].
- **Studies used nearly verbatim.** The left oak of *Dolmen in the Snow*
  matches an 1804 sketchbook study "enlarged and darkened" [KÖR fig.8]; the
  oaks of *Abbey* come from studies of 1804 and 5 May 1809 [WP-Abtei]; the
  trees of *Winter Landscape* from a detailed 1807 pencil study [NG p.49];
  the Monk's beach from a pen drawing of 17 August 1801 [WP-Monk].
- **Enlarged by machine, transferred by tracing.** The SPSG/Hannover study
  of 14 paintings: he made "suitable enlargements of his studies,
  evidently using a pantograph, as small whole-number ratios in the size
  steps show"; "cartoons for transferring a finished composition to the
  ground must be assumed" [MOST-abs, my translation]. In the Berlin *Man
  and Woman Contemplating the Moon*, IRR shows two vertical lines for a
  walking stick that the finished picture lacks: "in transferring the
  tracing (Pause) he took over the stick and then did not paint it"
  [WP-ZM].
- **How the montage was done is still open** ("It remains a mystery how
  Friedrich actually accomplished this cut-and-paste montage") [KÖR
  p.285].

**Sim.** *Now:* a painter can consult a structure tool as a "study"
(`notes/principles.md`, "Structure tools") and draw from it by hand with
`pencil:line` and `sketch`. *New (P1): studies and transfer.* Let a painter
make a study on its own sheet (a pencil drawing of one tree, rock or range
in its own frame, possibly drawn from a grown structure as "nature"), then
**transfer** it: enlarged by a small whole ratio (pantograph) and traced
onto the canvas as a line with a tracer's faults (wobble, dropped bits,
doubled lines where the tracing shifted), which the painter then draws over
or omits. The painter still places and selects everything; the tool gives
the mechanism he had, not a finished shape. *Habit:* write distances on the
study: number the ranges front to back and use the numbers when mixing the
aerial perspective later (not a haze function).

## 1. The studio and the conditions

- **Bare room, controlled light.** Kersting's portraits (1811, c.1812,
  1814–19) show "a room with two tall windows, one of which is completely
  darkened, while the other has wooden shutters that cover the lower part";
  contemporary treatises recommended diffuse light "at an angle from above"
  [BUSCH p.326]. He built the shutters himself after 1806 and took them to
  his next studio [WP-KERSTING].
- **Nothing else in sight.** "An easel, a stool and a table above which a
  single T-square hung as the only decoration" (Wilhelm von Kügelgen) [NG
  pp.46, 49]; paint box, oil bottles and rag were kept in the next room so
  as not to disturb "his inner world" (Kügelgen, quoted in [WP-KERSTING-EN]).
  Tools on the wall: two palettes, a set square, a T-square, a ruler
  [BUSCH p.326].
- **Posture.** In 1811 he sits at the easel, "his arm leaning on the
  mahlstick"; in the Berlin picture he stands leaning on a chair back,
  brush in the right hand, palette, maulstick and more brushes in the left
  [WP-KERSTING-EN]. A maulstick means the hand rests near the canvas:
  close work, small strokes.
- **Undisturbed hours; walks at the edges of the day.** He called studio
  time the "consecrated hour" [WP-KERSTING-EN]; per Carus, "his sole
  diversions were his solitary walks just before sunrise or just after
  sunset" [MET PDF p.15].
- **Two viewing distances were intended.** Close up the pictures have
  "drawing character"; the method gives "a different experience from a
  distance and close up" [WP-Tetschen; WP-CDF]. No source gives his working
  distance in numbers.
- **War and illness interrupted work** (1813, "these circumstances
  paralyzed him in his work again and again") [WP-CDF].

**Sim.** *Now:* hand time, `sitting{}`, `rest()` model a person working
sittings in a day. *Habit:* one light, from above left or right; look at
the work at two distances (`look` whole, then `look --crop … --scale 3.2`)
and let each passage satisfy both. Keep the palette spare: few piles, no
per-point color functions.

## 2. Ground and imprimatura (additions to the materials note)

- **Tone chosen for the motif.** In *Two Men Contemplating the Moon*
  (Dresden) the reddish ocher top ground "serves … as the mid-tone … and
  remains visible beneath the loosely applied paint" [KÖR p.284].
- **Tolerated defects.** The bristle striations of the top ground "shine
  through the thin upper paint layers"; "Friedrich was obviously not
  bothered by these delicate textures and also overlooked the occasional
  deeper scratch mark" [KÖR p.284].
- **Imprimatura.** One account describes a chalk or gypsum ground, then a
  second pigmented light yellow or ocher layer, over which the yellowish
  ground shows even through dark objects, "so the weak moonlight soaks the
  whole picture" [WP-ZM]. (Secondary; the cross-sections in [KÖR p.284]
  agree on a two-tone ground.)

- **His own stock.** The SPSG/Hannover findings "speak for the artist
  using his own supply of primed canvases and stretching them himself";
  the cloth's manufacturing traces match the tools of Saxon oilcloth
  makers [MOST-abs, my translation].

**Sim.** *Now:* the Friedrich grounds exist (knife lower layers, brushed or
rolled top with striations; `crates/paint/src/style.rs`), and a ground
color is set by the style. *New (small, P7): ground defects.* An occasional
deep scratch and a top white that is patchy in hiding (not only in stroke
direction, which `style.rs` already varies patch by patch). *Habit:* pick
the ground's tone for the light of the picture.

## 3. Underdrawing

- **Order and tools.** "First lightly with chalk and pencil, then the whole
  of it properly and definitely with a quill pen and Indian ink, and soon
  proceeded to lay on the underpainting" (Carus) [NG p.49]. Reed pen
  (Rohrfeder) is also named [WP-Tetschen]. In *Abbey* "the lines of the
  architecture are accented with thin brown-red brushwork" [WP-Abtei].
  Late works: a pointed brush [ART].
- **Complete, even to grass.** In the unfinished *Entrance to a Graveyard*
  he "first drew out the entire composition in pencil and then
  systematically overlaid the entire composition, including fine details
  such as the grass and delicate leaf structure" in ink [NG p.58]. "No
  gradation of detail according to significance" [CATS p.132].
- **The pen line has a surface.** "The broken line produced when a pen
  moves over a slightly uneven surface and the accumulation of material at
  the point where the pen is taken off the surface is typical" [NG p.58].
  Ink lines "of varying thickness," fluid; ruled lines "very fine" [NG
  p.58].
- **Guide lines, rubbed off.** In *Winter Landscape*, "faint fine lines …
  bisecting the church and the bridge, which were drawn as a guide to the
  symmetry … and partly rubbed off before painting" [NG p.58]. In the
  Tetschen altar, ruled guide lines for the sun's rays [NG p.58].
- **Removing, not adding.** "He seldom adds elements to his underdrawings
  while painting"; turning the monk is an exception; "he often empties his
  pictures during painting by leaving out and overpainting single motifs"
  [ART, my translation]. Unpainted: three ships, nets [CATS p.128]; a well
  [NG p.58]; a walking stick [WP-ZM]; an altar and priest in *Abbey*
  [WP-Abtei].
- **What survives into the finished surface.** Early: the drawing "often
  remains visible as a line scaffold" through extremely thin paint [ART];
  dune and grass zigzags "still shimmer … through the finished painting"
  [CATS p.130]; in *The Sea of Ice*, "small dark dots" at the boundary of
  two color fields are "last traces of the underdrawing" [HH]; in *Two
  Men*, "pencil underdrawing visible in moon" [KÖR fig.7].
- **Exception.** The Met *Two Men* (c.1825–30): infrared shows "a fluid,
  assured brush that … needed no underdrawing" [MET PDF pp.19, 35].
- **Two underdrawing layers were a period habit.** J.C. Dahl's 1826
  underdrawing was China ink, "executed in detail, in the manner of
  Friedrich, and the painting stage closely follows the drawing" [NG p.58].

**Sim.** *Now:* pencil, chalk, `rule`, `erase`, `fix`, `drawing_guide()`
(README "Drawing"). A painter can draw faint, then firm, rule the
straights, rub off the symmetry guides with `erase` before painting and
paint into the drawing. *New (P2): pen and ink, and the brush line.* A quill
or reed pen: a fluid line whose width follows pressure and speed, broken
where the nib skips over the ground's peaks, with a bead of ink where it
lifts; not erasable and dark under thin paint (carbon ink). A thin brush
line in brown-red paint for accents (a `brush("rigger")` with thin paint
may already do this; check). *Habit:* draw everything, then leave things
out while painting; let lines show through thin passages; never add a
motif in paint that isn't in the drawing.

## 4. Underpainting (dead color)

- **Thin and brown.** "A very thin underpainting" under all later paint
  [CATS p.127]; described as "brown-toned" [WP-CDF]; on the Tetschen
  altar's crucifix, "coloured highlights on thin brown underpainting" [KÖR
  fig.1].
- **The sepia method.** "In his sepias … a careful linear underdrawing is
  overlaid with delicate washes of tone and a similar technique is often
  apparent in his early oils … thin transparent layers … the range of
  colour is restricted as if Friedrich preferred to exploit subtle
  variations in tone" [NG p.49]. His first oil (*Seashore with Fisherman*,
  1807) applied "working steps from sepia drawing" [WP-Fischer].

**Sim.** *Now:* a brown tonal lay-in is `work(m, {hand="glaze", …})` or a
thin `work` with a raw umber mix at high medium, done passage by passage,
in values, before any color. *Habit:* first a value picture in one brown,
like a sepia; color goes over it.

## 5. The paint layers

- **Few and thin, but in states.** One layer in *Abbey*; two in *Monk*: "at
  first laid in as a dark-blue painting. Over the middle sky Friedrich
  later laid a second layer in light blue, pink and white" [SMB-blog, my
  translation]. Visitors saw the Monk's sky change: April 1809 "a gray air
  heavy with vapors"; June 1809 "pure and indifferently calm … no storm,
  no sun, no moon"; 24 September 1810 "the last quarter of the moon and the
  faintly flashing morning star"; "the final sky was painted shortly
  before" it went to Berlin [WP-Monk, quoting the visitors]. The ships
  were gone by April 1809 [WP-Monk].
- **Greens stacked.** "Several (up to four) layers of different greens" in
  green passages [MÄD p.102].
- **Darkening with blue.** "For darkening, Friedrich also liked to use
  blue" [MÄD p.102 n.4].
- **Worked wet.** He "modeled the still-wet paint" [MOST-abs]. One summary
  (unverified in the full text) reports Rinmann's green as "a light,
  pastose top layer in short dabs or zigzags over a yellow-green layer"
  (materials note §9).
- **Glaze and body.** Transparent layers over the light ground everywhere
  in early work [NG p.49]; lead-white body in snow (it shows in the X-ray)
  [NG p.51]; the moon's crescent "applied last in opaque white" [WP-ZM].
- **Surface by surface.** In *The Sea of Ice*: "Dense frozen snow,
  translucent ice, reflections, dark shadows: Friedrich treated each
  surface differently. Sometimes he laid paint on pastose and generously,
  sometimes with little paint in the brush and in places he left the
  reddish ground standing" [HH, my translation]. Foreground and background
  are "often treated differently" [WP-CDF]; in the Düsseldorf *Cross*,
  "more fluid and textured areas of paint in the foreground contrast with
  … precise, graphic techniques" behind [NG p.50].
- **Change over the career.** Early: "painstaking and deliberate,
  sometimes with small strokes of paint placed side by side like a closely
  woven textile" [NG p.49]; first oils "dry and nearly monochrome," later
  lighter and "also pastose" (*Dolmen in Autumn*, 1819) [WP-CDF]. c.1818:
  "elaborate rendition of detail and smoothing of individual brushstrokes"
  [MET PDF p.28]. Middle period (1811–late 1820s): "the painting becomes
  denser"; late: "both the drawn and the painted lay-in become more fluid"
  [ART].
- **Where layers dried.** Not documented as such. Inferred: a repaint over
  the dark-blue Monk and skies seen months apart imply layers left to dry
  for weeks or months; companion pictures were on the easel together
  (*Monk* and *Abbey* from one roll, bought together) [CATS p.127], and
  *Mountain Landscape with Rainbow* was painted in parallel [WP-Monk].

**Sim.** *Now:* all of this is time and layering the engine has: `rest`,
`dry()`, `glaze`, body `work`, hand time. A painter repeats a sky: lay it
in, `dry()`, judge, lay the next state over it (thin enough that the first
shows), which is how two layers of different color make the Monk's sky.
*Habit:* dark with blue, not black; build greens in two to four layers of
different greens instead of one aimed mix; keep the final opaque white for
the last touches (moon, highlights). *New? (P3): lift and scrape.* "Modeled
the still-wet paint" and "rubbed off" guide lines need a rag, a finger or
a brush handle that **removes** open paint (lifting it to show the layer
under it) and a knife point that scratches tacky or dry paint. I found no
such verb in the easel README.

## 6. The hand: strokes and marks

- **Skies, mist, distance: stippled, strokeless.** Stippling in skies and
  gray-blue land "throughout Friedrich's career," probably "to achieve
  effects … similar to his early sepias" [NG p.56]; the moon sky of *Two
  Men* is stippled [KÖR fig.7]. Thin paint pooled in the ground's hollows,
  "the finest dots of color," so he "avoided visible brushstrokes" [CATS
  p.127].
- **Firs: short hatched strokes** [NG p.50]. **Grass: "a characteristically
  fine upturning brushstroke, applied as a final touch on top of the
  snow"** [NG p.56]; in *Woman before the Setting Sun* the blades are
  straight, "absolutely straight in their incline," made with "a stiff,
  inflexible paint brush," each leaning toward the figure by its distance
  from her [UCL p.116]. Clusters of grass are described per blade.
- **Very fine lines.** The rigging in *The Sea of Ice* is "barely visible to
  the naked eye" ("How many hairs did the brush have?") [HH]. Gulls: twenty
  small ones "placed directly on the completed painting" [CATS p.130].
- **Distant things from a few strokes.** "Elements further back he
  executed with less detail … From single delicate brushstrokes on the
  blue-in-blue horizon the eye assembles the object" [HH].
- **Surface overall.** "Smooth paint surface … more akin to graphic works
  on paper than to the painterly quality frequently exploited" in oil [NG
  p.55]; "the most textured areas are in the foreground where a slight
  impasto recreates … melting snow" [NG p.50].
- **Brushes.** Not documented by type, size or hair in any source I could
  read; Kersting shows several brushes, palettes and a maulstick [BUSCH
  p.326]. The one explicit tool note is the stiff brush for grass [UCL
  p.116]. Friedrich himself called the hand "dexterity," "brushwork
  (Pinselfertigkeit)" and "craftsmanship" [KÖR p.281].

**Sim.** *Now:* `stipple` (skies, mist, far hills), `hand="hatch"` (firs),
`b:stroke` with pressure falling to 0 (upturned blades), rigger for
rigging; pooling is in the engine (`crates/paint/src/surface.rs`). *Habit:*
grass as individual strokes placed last, each with its own lean, not
sprayed tufts; distant things as a few strokes the eye assembles; impasto
only in near snow and lights. *Gap:* stroke direction and length in skies
and water aren't documented; stippled skies have no direction.

## 7. Edges

- **Reserved edges.** In *The Sea of Ice*, which came first, the white of
  the snowy floe or the brown crusted break behind it, "is hardly to be
  decided, since neither color covers the other. They don't even touch.
  The brownish ground showing through makes a particular three-
  dimensionality" [HH, my translation]. At that boundary the underdrawing
  survives as dark dots [HH].
- **Paint obeys the drawing** [NG p.49]; pentimenti are small and at edges
  ("at the left and right-hand edge of the largest fir tree") [NG p.49].
- **Lost edges by stipple and pooling** in mist and sky [NG p.56; CATS
  p.127]; **found edges by drawing** (ink under thin paint) and by opaque
  last touches (gulls, moon, grass) [CATS p.130; WP-ZM; NG p.56].

**Sim.** *Now:* a painter can stop each field short of its neighbor with a
clip mask shrunk from the drawn outline (`o:inset(d)`), but that is a
computed edge. *New (P4): painting up to a line.* A hand that brings a
stroke **to a drawn line and stops**, with a person's error: the reserve of
bare ground between two fields varies along the line (none here, a hairline
there, a small overlap elsewhere), so the drawing, the ground and both
fields meet unevenly. This is a constraint on the brush (where a stroke may
end relative to what the painter sees), not a mask. The edges stream
should own it; this note gives the source.

## 8. Final details, varnish

- **Last:** grass [NG p.56], gulls [CATS p.130], the moon [WP-ZM].
- **Varnish:** temporary egg white, mastic by the buyer within a year [KÖR
  p.283] (materials note §7).

**Sim.** *Now:* the README order ("small particulars last") and `varnish{}`
match. *Habit:* the last day is for the few opaque touches that make the
light.

## 9. Pace

- **Months, in states.** *Monk* c.1808–1810 with the sky seen in three
  states and finished at the last moment [WP-Monk]; *Winter Landscape*
  described by studio visitors in 1811 [NG p.46]; *The Sea of Ice* dated
  1823/24 [HH].
- **Several pictures at once** (pairs from one canvas roll; parallel works)
  [CATS p.127; WP-Monk].
- **Hours per day, sittings, working distance:** not documented in any
  source I found.

**Sim.** *Now:* `rest(16)` for days, `dry()` for weeks; a painter can leave
a passage for a "month" and come back to repaint it. *Habit:* stop, dry,
look, repaint the part that is wrong; expect the sky to take several
states.

finishing verb for the painting's history: past cleanings that wear the
peaks of the texture and thin paint (micro-losses showing the ground),
concentrated where the film is thinnest and highest. **New (P6):
mechanical crack events.** Pressure on the back or a knock (the grain-ear
pattern), in addition to drying and stretcher cracks (`crack.rs` has
stretcher-bar stress under `vary`). **Optional (P8): old restorations.**
Retouchings slightly larger than a loss that discolor differently from the
original. This is real, but it is a later hand, so opt-in only.

## 11. What imitations and restorations got wrong

- **The Dortmund *Winter Landscape*** (copy or version): "No underdrawing is
  visible … a fact that immediately sets it apart"; "a more even paint
  surface and in some areas … the paint appears to have been rapidly
  applied … more freely painted … less evidence of the deliberation and
  care" [NG p.50]. The church is "an indistinct silhouette," where the
  original is "drawn in great detail" [NG p.46].
- **Carl Lieber's copy (1812)** was "his own interpretation," a "pastiche"
  that got him dismissed from the studio [NG p.46].
- **The de-attributed *Landscape with a Bare Tree*:** "the crude,
  ill-considered lines of the pencil underdrawing could not be reconciled
  with Friedrich's drawings," and it contains chrome yellow and cobalt
  blue [KÖR p.282; MÄD p.102].
- **Restorers:** retouchings "rather larger than the losses," an overpainted
  sun "resting on a misunderstanding of the composition" [NG p.55]; hard
  to match "the translucency of the shade of smalt … as well as the
  artist's stippled painting technique" [NG p.55]; hatched retouchings
  that whitened into false mist; heavy varnishes that turned the blues
  gray and hid the reds of the Monk's sky [SMB-blog; WP-Monk].

**Lesson for us.** The copies fail in the same direction as our renders:
**even surfaces, no drawing underneath, detail softened into silhouette,
paint applied fast and freely.** The original is precise in drawing and
uneven in matter.

Habits for the sketchbook (no engine change): the brown sepia lay-in; draw
everything, then omit; number the distances on studies; repaint the sky in
states over dried layers; dark with blue; greens in up to four layers;
grass blade by blade last, straight or upturned, each with its own lean;
opaque white only for last touches; stop each field at the drawn line and
let the ground show between; judge at two distances.

## Gaps

No source I could read documents: brush types and sizes; stroke direction
in skies and water; hours per sitting; working distance; drying intervals
between layers; Friedrich-specific craquelure types beyond the grain-ear
pattern; film thickness in µm. The SPSG/Hannover full text [MOST-abs]
(pantograph ratios, wet modeling), Mösl's dissertation [ART] and the
Körber essay beyond p.285 probably hold more; they were behind a bot
check or not in the accessible excerpt. Web search was unavailable for
part of this round (no API credits, then search engines blocked automated
queries), so this note leans on sources fetched directly.
