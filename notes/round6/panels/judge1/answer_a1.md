pair1: prefer Y (strength: slight)
why: Y groups the upper-left lights into more legible foliage masses and lets some edges soften into the sky; X has livelier small irregularities but a more uniformly clipped silhouette. Compare pair1_X.jpg and pair1_Y.jpg, with their crops.
X defects: The lower and right crown collapse into one nearly black slab, without enough internal separation to turn the form. Sky holes and the outside contour stay insistently sharp, making the foliage look punched out. Fine, evenly distributed surface agitation substitutes for larger leaf-group decisions.
Y defects: Rounded scallops repeat around the crown and sky holes, giving the foliage a rubbery appearance in the crop. Diagonal ridges run through the dark crown without describing its separate masses. The straight brown bars crossing sky holes attract attention without convincingly connecting to visible branching.

pair2: prefer Y (strength: clear)
why: Y's warm light face, cool upper plane and darker front face explain the rock economically, while its base merges more convincingly with the ground shadow. X's grass and brilliant surface pitting distract from that simple solidity (pair2_X.jpg, pair2_Y.jpg and their crops).
X defects: The lit face is covered with tiny bright pits and ridges, reading more like foam than broad stone planes. Hair-thin grass crowds the base and crosses the shadow with excessive individual definition. The cast shadow has a smooth, sharply bounded outer shape that sits on the field like a separate sheet.
Y defects: Repeated slanting wrinkles cover all the rock's planes, weakening their different orientations. The shadow is a densely textured dark pad whose far edge does not sufficiently dissolve into the grass. The lit face ends in a conspicuously straight, fringed lower edge rather than settling naturally into the ground.

pair3: prefer X (strength: clear)
why: X makes a quieter, more continuous passage from blue sky to warm light, and its broken horizon belongs to the paint surface rather than cutting across it. Y's stronger cloud accents do not compensate for its rigid land boundary (pair3_X.jpg, pair3_Y.jpg and their crops).
X defects: The two low cloud banks have soft, blunt ends and sausage-like bodies; softness is too uniform to suggest changing cloud density. Salmon accents beneath them read as separate smears rather than light turning through vapor. Pale facets along the dark foreground ridge compete unnecessarily with the sky.
Y defects: The horizon is a razor-cut, visibly stepped boundary above an almost featureless dark strip. Repeated orange dabs beneath the low clouds look hung from their undersides. Dense grain across the yellow sky makes the luminous opening feel dry and dirty in the crop.

pair4: prefer Y (strength: clear)
why: Y's broken vegetation edge and downward-dragged reflection connect land, air and water more persuasively than X's sealed shapes. The softer distant shore also creates better recession (pair4_X.jpg, pair4_Y.jpg and their crops).
X defects: The near shore and its reflection share an unnaturally continuous, hard contour, making the reflected shape look like a second solid island. The narrow projecting tip ends with a squared-off cutoff. The distant shore forms two flat, stacked bands with too little atmospheric edge variation.
Y defects: Upright foliage strokes repeat in a comb-like rhythm along the near island. In the crop, wiry loops around the point and their dangling reflected counterparts look like brush debris. Scattered peach strokes across the open water compete with the main reflection instead of reinforcing its stillness.

pair5: prefer X (strength: clear)
why: X's twig network remains recognizably connected to the tree, whereas Y surrounds the branches with conspicuous gray scribble cushions. X is overdrawn, but its winter silhouette is more coherent (pair5_X.jpg, pair5_Y.jpg and their crops).
X defects: Tiny forks repeat at similar sharpness throughout the crown, producing diagrammatic clutter rather than selective observation. Smooth, dark main branches look like ink lines against the more textured sky. Individually drawn grass blades add another competing network across the foreground.
Y defects: Dense gray hatching forms fluffy patches around the crown instead of readable twig growth. Major branches disappear abruptly inside those patches, with broken fragments that do not convincingly taper. The narrow bright trunk edge and blunt base make the trunk feel outlined and planted on top of the ground.

Across all ten, the costliest recurring defect is unvaried separation: clipped contours, sealed reflections and rigid horizons keep forms from sharing an atmosphere, especially in pairs 1, 3 and 4. Next is repeated small texture replacing form-specific brushwork: crown ridges, rock wrinkles and winter-tree hatching become surface patterns rather than useful descriptions. Third is poor allocation of detail: grass hairs, twig forks and scattered sky or water dabs compete with the principal masses. Finally, broad dark areas often receive too little internal value structure, most noticeably in the summer crowns and evening foregrounds; texture alone cannot give those masses depth.
