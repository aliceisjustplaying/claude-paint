# Kendric Tonn: materials and method

*A technical report from documented sources. Almost all of it comes from the painter's own
words: posts on X (2019–2026), a materials thread of 2023 and interviews.
"Uncertain" marks claims that rest on a single remark, may have changed
since or are inferred.*

## Summary

Tonn reports working mostly from life and transferring his drawings
to the support with a projector. For medium and large works his
usual support is synthetic canvas glued to a cradled plywood panel; he
paints small works on hardboard or commercial boards.
He primes with an oil ground applied with a wide taping knife, described
in 2020 as "a tinted lead ground" and in 2025 as a "greenish ground". Lead (flake) white is his standard white. His documented pigments are listed in §4. He reports
using very little medium, basically no liquid oil and no solvent in the
paint. He paints mostly with natural-bristle
flats and uses nylon brushes to smooth. He calls himself "85% a direct painting guy" (2026). For layered works he describes drawing, a thin first painting, a
second painting and final glazes, working over the whole surface at once.

**Terms.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layer between support and
paint. *Imprimatura*: a thin toned wash over the ground. *Grisaille*: a
monochrome underpainting. *Glaze*: a transparent paint layer.
*Impasto*: thick, raised paint. *Alla prima*: painting in a single wet
layer. *Retouch varnish*: a thin temporary varnish that can be painted
over. *Black mirror*: a darkened mirror that simplifies values.

## 1. Supports

- **Canvas on panel (main support).** "For my own work, I've mostly
  settled on a high-grade synthetic canvas adhered to a cradled plywood
  back." The plywood keeps the fabric from flexing and cracking the
  paint; the fabric bridges the plywood's tendency to check. He prefers
  synthetic fabric because it resists damage from the oil, "unlike flax
  or cotton". He cuts the plywood himself and still
  made "custom panels" in 2025.
- **Adhesive.** First an acrylic adhesive; from 2020 BEVA film,
  "reversable with low-ish heat," chosen so a conservator could separate
  fabric and panel later. After gluing he trims and staples
  the canvas around the panel.
- **Stretched canvas.** In 2025 he oil-primed "a bunch of canvases" and
  had a 24 × 48 in. oil-primed canvas "all ready to go".
  For a canvas about 6 ft long he would consider an aluminum stretcher
  bar.
- **Linen.** He bought "a roll of linen" in 2020. Listings of
  2011 give "oil on canvas affixed to panel" and oil on stretched canvas.
- **Rigid boards for small works.** He reports using hardboard and
  commercial acrylic-ground boards for small works and buying ready-made
  wooden panels.
- **Sizes.** Reported sizes include about 30 × 40 in.; listings range
  from 9 × 12 to 36 × 48 in., all "oil on panel".

## 2. Grounds

- **Oil ground, knifed.** "The oil primer I use has a heavy, roughly
  wet-mud consistency, and I want it pretty smooth, so I've been going
  with a taping knife" (a drywall knife).
- **Lead ground, tinted (2020).** In 2020 he reported priming with a
  tinted lead ground. He adds that lead "produces a uniquely strong paint film, and
  dries rapidly".
- **Greenish oil ground (2025).** In June 2025 he primed custom panels
  with an oil ground that "will be a while drying" and called it "the
  greenish ground". The next day he wrote that he had
  "oil-primed a bunch of canvases" and the house smelled "strongly of
  linseed oil".
- **Acrylic ground when time is short.** In 2021 he applied acrylic
  "gesso" to a new canvas panel: "I usually prefer the feel of an oil
  primed surface, but I don't care to wait a few months for it to dry".
- **Texture.** He reports varying the ground texture. A rougher-than-planned
  surface led him to use heavier impasto on one canvas.
- **Toning.** He reported discontinuing a mid-gray imprimatura in 2021. A
  2024 example used "a flat brownish ground layer, or imprimatura".

## 3. Drawing and lay-in

- **Preparatory drawing.** He reports detailed preparatory drawings in
  pencil and chalk before some paintings, with a cited sheet size of
  22 × 30 in.
- **Transfer by projector.** He does not draw at painting scale and
  "absolutely hate[s] using a grid-transfer," so he photographs the
  drawing and projects it (2024). In 2026 he described the step as "throw
  it on my computer onto a projector and trace." He does not think it
  works where alignment has to be exact.
- **Studies first (occasional).** For one set of commissions in 2026 he
  planned "studies from life, final drawing from studies, paint that". *Uncertain* as a general practice.
- **Drawing on the canvas.** He has documented drawing directly in
  charcoal on canvas and revising the drawing during painting:
  "that never stops".
- **Aids to seeing.** Squinting, a black mirror and, above all, a regular
  mirror. He occasionally measures by holding up a
  brush, "but mostly I'm purely operating on visual comparison" (2026).
- **Photographs.** "I'll sometimes grab a photo to remind myself of a
  color or a texture... They're not a big part of my process" (2025).
- **Restarting.** He wipes an initial drawing off the canvas and starts
  again, or scrapes off a sketch "before it dries".

## 4. Pigments

His palette changes with the painting ("the specific pigments may vary
by painting"). He carried "ten colors" to one sitting.
Early on he spent "about five years painting in a limited palette". A ten-color palette posted in May 2025 was "relatively
unusual," because "I am in fact not normally running four different
yellows": ivory black, ultramarine blue, terre verte, permanent
alizarin, burnt sienna, transparent oxide yellow, yellow ochre, cadmium
yellow deep, cadmium yellow light, flake white. In September
2025 he sorted his tubes into a "standard rotation" tier, a tier for
"as circumstances call for it" and spares.

- **Paint.** He prefers paints with few added modifiers, just
  "oil+pigment," and reports using pigment drying differences in his
  work. He reports using commercial professional-grade paints
  from several makers.
- **Grinding.** "I don't grind my own paint" (2026). Earlier
  posts mention grinding with a muller (2021) and "decades... grinding
  paint" (May 2026). *Uncertain* whether he ground paint for his
  own pictures at any time.

## 5. Media and varnish

- **Oil.** Linseed. Among drying oils, linseed is
  "the most common and the fastest drying... (also forms the most
  durable paint films, but is more prone to yellowing)".
- **Little medium.** "I use very little... but I have a gel to loosen
  paints, and an additive to increase their transparency" (2020).
  In 2025: "I use very very little medium. I use Natural Pigment's
  Venetian Medium for glazing in the latter stages of a painting, and
  I'll use their oleogel to make paints a little longer, but basically I
  just work with straight paint." The Venetian medium contains some
  turpentine and "does also speed up drying a good bit".
- **No liquid oil.** "I use basically no liquid oil for my paints" (2025).
- **No solvent in painting.** "I don't use any solvents in painting"
  (2021); "I don't use any to actually paint with" (2024). Solvent (odorless mineral spirits) is used only for cleaning
  brushes.
- **Retouch varnish.** A light spray of damar, used "very rarely" to
  bring up sunken areas before repainting, in "a minimal, noncontinuous
  layer".
- **No intermediate varnish.** He rejects "intermediate thin layers of
  varnish between layers of paint" on grounds of long-term condition.
- **Final varnish.** He reports using high-gloss Gamvar (a Regalrez
  resin) and applying two coats before works leave his studio. Paintings rest about six months before final varnish;
  touch-dry is not enough. When he reworks a
  painting, which is rare, he strips and re-varnishes it.

## 6. Layers and application

- **Direct by default.** "I'm a pretty direct painter, in most
  circumstances" (2022); "I'm like 85% a direct painting guy"
  (2026).
- **Four stages (layered works).** He describes an approximate sequence
  of drawing, thin first painting, second painting and final glazes, and
  calls it "a sort of idealized process, often strayed from".
- **Single-layer studies.** He reports some studies painted in one wet
  layer over a brownish imprimatura.
- **Whole surface at once.** He reports repeated work over the whole
  surface, "tightening and refining each time"; some details follow the
  initial block-in.
- **Monochrome underpainting (occasional).** A grisaille "worked up to a
  fairly high level of finish before laying color over it"; in
  2022, "something I do infrequently". Monochrome underpaintings
  needed "to dry for a few days" before the next layer.
- **Glazes.** He usually applies glazes "as literally the last thing I
  do", with the Venetian medium "in the latter stages".
  Once, as an exception, he glazed terre verte early over a monochrome
  underpainting, then repainted it with solid paint.
- **Stiff paint.** "I'm a fairly controlled painter, and I use fairly
  stiff paints, so I'm generally not needing to have a brush completely
  soaked down to the ferrule".
- **Thickness.** "I'm not the heaviest painter on the planet".
  Heavy impasto is an exception.
- **Surface correction.** He grinds down unwanted texture with a
  cuttlefish bone and refills it with the palette knife.
- **Brushes.** "I paint 95% with flat, natural-bristle brushes... in
  various sizes, 5% with cheap white nylon brushes". Most are
  "1/4-1/2ish inch wide" (2023); "about 1cm wide or ½cm wide"
  (2022). Sables are of little use in oil in his view. He
  ends a session with "about six or eight brushes in my hand," separate
  ones "for different 'categories' of color". He reports laying
  paint with bristle and smoothing it with nylon; he also uses
  nylon for some fine marks and final accents.
- **Palette.** He uses an arm palette or a pochade palette, groups
  colors by hue and periodically clears mixtures.

## 7. Working sequence and reworking

- **From life.** "I almost always work from life"; "I draw and
  paint from life" (2026).
- **Several at once.** He works on two or three pieces concurrently.
- **Setup.** For some arrangements he reports a delay of several days
  between setup and starting to paint.
- **Light.** A 2021 post reports LED illumination; a September 2026 post
  reports natural light on the model and a clamp light on the easel.
- **Reworking.** He wipes or scrapes off early drawing (§3); reworking a
  varnished painting is rare.

- **One small painting, filmed (2026).** A timelapse he posted of one
  small painting shows how the paint went on. The ground was
  white, with no imprimatura. The paint was opaque from the first strokes
  and broke over the weave at the edges of strokes; later passes were
  scumbled. The most heavily loaded paint was in the lightest accents. No
  glazing was visible.

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Drying rates.** "Earth tones (umbers, siennas)... and
  lead white are rapid dryers," while ivory black is "notoriously slow"
  (2023). Rates also depend on "atmospheric
  conditions, painting mediums," and "thickness of paint layer".
  "Even the fast drying pigments won't really dry during a working
  session".
- **Structure.** He avoids complicating the layer structure. His
  example is a crack pattern from "a fast-drying earth brown... over a
  slower-drying paint". He expects oil paint to become "more
  transparent with age".
- **Varnish readiness.** He reports checking the paint's resistance to
  indentation before varnishing.

## 9. The tubes here

The historical names in this note correspond to these tubes: flake
white → lead white; cadmium yellow light and deep → cadmium yellow;
terre verte → green earth; ivory black → bone black; red umber → raw
umber (the nearest tube).

## 10. At this easel

- **Support.** Plain-weave linen with ground layers laid from the tubes.
- **Paint.** Pigment from the tubes with an oil-medium share.
- **Taking paint off.** A brush picks up wet paint as it passes; that is
  how paint comes off the canvas.
- **Everything else** the easel does is in the easel guide.
