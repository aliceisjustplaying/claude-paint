# Cassatt, c. 1868–1914: materials and process (oil, pastel, gouache and distemper, prints)

This note covers materials and method only, and the works named in it are there only as evidence. Tags like [Met M&C] point to the source list at the end. "Uncertain" marks a claim where the evidence is thin or the sources disagree.

**How much is known.** Her process is less documented than her contemporaries'. A conservator in the 2024 Philadelphia catalogue says so ("Documentation of Cassatt's process is lacking in comparison to that of her contemporaries") [artnet]. The main technical studies are:
- H. K. Stratis on her pastels [Stratis 1998]
- the Philadelphia/San Francisco *Mary Cassatt at Work* catalogue (2024) [PMA 2024]
- Mathews & Shapiro on the color prints [M&S 1989]

I could read none of these directly. What is below comes from museum object pages, a brochure and articles that quote them. A claim resting only on a search summary is marked as such in the sources.

**Output.** About 320 pastels, 320 oil paintings and 215 prints [PMA 2024 via Weiss]. Pastel and oil carry equal weight, and prints are a third major body of work.

## 1. Oil painting

**Supports.** Oil on canvas throughout. This holds for the Seville paintings of 1872–73, signed and dated there [AIC; Panorama], through the 1878 armchair portrait (89.5 × 129.8 cm) [NGA brochure], to the 1890s figure paintings such as a 1893 canvas of 101 × 67 cm [AIC].
- I found no record of her canvas weave, ground color or colorman's stamp. Uncertain.

**Handling** (from the 2024 technical study, as reported by a reviewer) [PMA 2024 via Weiss]:
- She sometimes laid "paint straight from the tube", "mixing color on the canvas rather than the palette".
- She favored "a vigorous application of paint that she'd blend with sweeps of a dry brush while the pigment was still wet".
- From the late 1870s she painted "freely and in a lighter palette". Her brushwork became "more polished by the late 80s".
- Contours are "alternately crisp or atmospheric". She "selectively eliminated contours" where mother and child touch, so one skin runs into the other.
- She was fascinated by the semi-translucency of skin. A baby's pale skin is built of "a frenzy of pinks and blues", a broken, cool-and-warm flesh rather than one blended mix.
- General descriptions of an 1893 canvas speak of layered, rough strokes and thick lines outlining the figures against a patterned ground [Wikipedia summary]. They are descriptive, not technical.

**Revisions.** She reworked on the canvas. In the 1878 armchair portrait [NGA brochure]:
- Infrared imaging shows a first horizontal floor line and a single back wall.
- Degas added the corner and its diagonal. His strokes are "grayish, almost silvery-brown" and come with "intentional abrasion of the surface" in that area. That handling is his and is absent from her work.
- She then repositioned and "heavily" reworked the couch, and tried the dog on the floor before returning it to its chair.

**Thinned or blotted paint (peinture à l'essence).** I found no evidence that she used blotted, turpentine-thinned oil as Degas did. Her lean, matte effects of 1878–79 came from distemper and gouache instead (§3). Uncertain.
- A Met article speaks of "the unvarnished matte surface of these works" as a modernist value. In context it seems to mean pastel and Impressionist surfaces in general, not her oils [Met M&C].
- Whether she varnished her oils is not recorded in what I could read. Uncertain.

**Palette and pigments.** I found **no published pigment analysis of her oil paintings**.
- The Getty ran a SERS study of organic colorants in "several pastel sticks from a historical pastel box" and two samples from a pastel, "both attributed to" Cassatt. It found colorants of these chemical classes [Getty SERS]:
  - polyphenols
  - rhodamines
  - azo pigments
  - anthraquinones (the madder class)
- Rhodamines and azo colorants are late-19th- and 20th-century synthetics, so that box at least is late. Which pigment was in which stick is not in the abstract.
- The NGA holds three wooden boxes of her pastels from Paris suppliers, given away in about 1920. Analysis of their pigments and of how her pastels were fixed was planned in 2014 [NPR 2014]. I found no results.
- **Estimate:** she bought from the same Paris colormen in the same years as the Impressionists, so the pigments the analyses found across that group are the best guide (notes/research/impressionist_materials.md):
  - lead and zinc whites
  - cobalt and ultramarine blues
  - viridian and emerald green
  - chrome and cadmium yellows
  - vermilion
  - madder and carmine lakes
  - the earths
  - bone and vine black

  This is an estimate, not an analysis of her paint.

## 2. Pastel

**Training.** The painter Charles Chaplin may have taught her pastel [eclecticlight 2022, a blog]. She met Degas in 1877 [NGA brochure], and his example shaped both her technique and how she saw [PMA 2024 via Weiss]. Uncertain how much was taught and how much shared.

**Supports.** She varied her supports and recorded them carefully [PMA In the Loge; Met M&C; eclecticlight 2022 captions]:
- **Paper alone or laid on board:**
  - pastel on paper mounted on paper (1879)
  - green wove paper mounted on illustration board (c. 1881)
  - brown paper laid down on board (1880) [artandobject]
  - tan wove paper (c. 1890)
  - wove paper on cardboard (c. 1897)
- **Paper adhered to canvas and tacked to a wooden strainer.** "Cassatt typically executed her pastels on paper that was adhered to canvas and tacked to a wood strainer." Several Met pastels of 1898–1914 are "pastel on wove paper mounted on canvas" [Met M&C]. This is her usual later support: a taut, slightly giving surface, larger than a board-mounted sheet (67.6 × 57.2 cm and 81.3 × 65.1 cm for two 1914 sheets).
- **Canvas alone, with a gritty ground.** For a c. 1879 pastel she "used a canvas coated with a ground embedded with pumice or coarse fibers to create a rough surface that would hold the powdery pastels more securely" [PMA In the Loge]. Reviews of the 2024 catalogue say she used "both textured paper and canvas coated with a variety of gritty primers" [PMA 2024 via reviews].
- **Japan paper** for the counterproofs (§2, counterproofs) [Adelson].

**Paper tones.** Recorded tones are tan, buff, brown, green and blue [eclecticlight 2022; Met M&C; Met Portrait].
- Late in her career "Cassatt's preference was for blue paper" [Met M&C].
- She left reserves of bare paper on purpose to "reveal the colored sheet below", against the older practice of covering it all [Met M&C].
- Uncertain: whether her colored papers have faded as Degas's aniline-dyed ones did. Nothing I read says either way.

**Sticks and maker.** She used commercial soft pastels from Paris suppliers, kept in large wooden boxes [NPR 2014]. **The maker is not identified in anything I read.** Roché is often named for Degas, and nothing ties it to her. Uncertain.

**Pastel with water.** Some areas of finished pastels are "painted in pastel chalk mixed with water, a technique learned from Degas" [PMA 2024 via Weiss]. The two 1880s examples named there are a full-length figure in a raspberry costume and a head of a girl with her hand to her ear.
- The c. 1879 canvas pastel was built using "moisture to create a multilayered surface" [PMA In the Loge].
- How the water was applied is not recorded in what I read: a dipped stick, a brush through dry pastel, or a paste. Uncertain.

**Moisture vapor and sifted pastel** (a 1914 pastel, studied by a Met paper conservator with a mock-up) [Met M&C]:
- She "selectively exposed" the sheet "to moisture vapor", as Degas did, but for a different end.
- Degas used vapor to stabilize pastel locally so that he could overlap strokes without muddying.
- She seems to have **sifted crushed pastel onto the dampened surface**, so that "its slight dampness agglomerated the particles into minute clusters as it dried". This was done in the flesh and the green around it, a "daring manipulation of the powdery texture" [Met text via arthur.io].
- The article calls it possibly "a one-off experiment".

**Fixative.**
- The same 1914 sheet has "no visibly compact or glossy surface or analytical evidence of a fixative" [Met M&C].
- The NGA meant to test how her pastels were fixed [NPR 2014]. I found no result.
- Treat fixing as unproven for her. Some sheets may be unfixed. Uncertain.

**Pastel with other media.**
- Pastel and gouache with metallic paint (1878–79) [NGA brochure].
- Pastel with gold metallic paint on the pumice-ground canvas, where she "enlivened the broad blended passages with brilliant touches of fresh pastel and bold streaks of metallic paint" [PMA In the Loge].
- A critic of 1879 wrote that she had "sacrificed herself to détrempe au pastel, glue and colored frames" [NGA brochure]. That is pastel combined with distemper (§3).
- Pastel over oil, or over monotype as Degas did: not found for her. Uncertain.

**Counterproofs** (c. 1889–1913) [Adelson]:
- A damp sheet is laid on a finished pastel and pressed, by rubbing or in a press. It takes a mirror image, "flatter and softer" in tone than the original.
- Fifty are known. Most are on **Japan paper** (one is 70 × 54 cm).
- The dealer Vollard encouraged them. Seven are now the only record of lost pastels.
- Whether she reworked counterproofs in pastel afterwards is not in what I read. Uncertain.

## 3. Gouache, distemper and watercolor

**Distemper (détrempe).** Pigment in glue size: fast-drying and matte.
- A standing woman holding a fan, 1878–79, is "distemper with metallic paint on canvas", 129 × 70 cm. It is "one of only two known paintings" in the medium [Amon Carter].
- She and Degas experimented with it together before the 1879 exhibition, and she abandoned it after 1879 [Amon Carter; NGA brochure].
- Degas's work in the same exhibition shows how the medium was used: "broad strokes of metallic paint and areas of distemper (pigment mixed with glue) with passages of delicately handled pastel" [NGA brochure].

**Metallic paint.** Gold, and possibly silver, used 1878–79 in distemper, gouache and pastel works, giving luster to fans and theater interiors [Amon Carter; NGA brochure; PMA In the Loge]. Its binder and metal are not recorded in what I read. Uncertain.

**Gouache and watercolor.**
- A self-portrait of 1878 is "watercolor, gouache on wove paper laid down to buff-colored wood-pulp paper" [Met Portrait, search summary].
- A c. 1880 self-portrait is "gouache and watercolor over graphite" [Huntington, search summary].
- A watercolor head of 1873 is listed by an aggregator [Artchive, weak].
- Watercolor is otherwise rare in her work. Breeskin's catalogue (1970) covers "oils, pastels, watercolors, and drawings" together, and I could not read it.

## 4. Drawing media

- **Graphite and charcoal.** Preparatory drawings for prints are "compressed charcoal and graphite over some charcoal lines" [Met Maternal Caress drawing]. Graphite also lies under gouache [Huntington].
- **Many studies.** She worked by "making sketch after sketch" [PMA 2024 via Weiss]. The drawings show her changing contours and positions (the mother's head and arms, the baby's foot) [Met Maternal Caress drawing].
- **Black or colored chalk** apart from pastel: not recorded in what I read. Uncertain.
- **Soft-ground lines** imitate chalk or pencil drawing (§5) [NGA brochure].

## 5. Printmaking

**Soft-ground etching, 1879–80.** This was for Degas's planned journal of prints *Le Jour et la nuit*, which was never published.
- Degas "introduced them to softground etching, a technique that produces the effect of a chalk or pencil drawing". She "embraced softground etching in particular" [NGA brochure].
- **Method:** "After laying a tacky etching ground on the copperplate, the artist placed this sheet [her drawing] on top of it. She then retraced her drawing with a stylus or a graphite pencil, pulling up the ground where the implement touched the page." The acid then bit the exposed lines [Met In the Opera Box drawing].
- Plates were copper [Met].
- She combined soft-ground with **drypoint, aquatint and etching** on one plate. Across states she "dramatically altered the lighting using aquatint": a face was first hidden in shadow and brought back in later states. That print ran to seven states [NGA brochure].
- **Fabric texture:** one c. 1881 print is "softground etching, drypoint, aquatint, etching, and fabric texture" [NGA brochure]. Cloth was pressed into the soft ground so that its weave printed as a tone.
- She worked through many states: Degas owned "a unique series of thirteen states" of that one print [NGA brochure].

**Drypoint (mid-1880s onward).**
- By the late 1880s "Cassatt was using drypoint almost exclusively". The needle on copper "made corrections difficult" [PMA, search summary].
- Scratching throws up a **burr** of displaced metal, which holds ink and gives the velvety line [FAMSF]. The burr wears down with printing, which is one reason her editions were small.
- Uncertain: a series of drypoints drawn directly from the model c. 1889–90, often reported. I could not confirm it.

**The color prints, 1890–91** (the set of ten, shown at Durand-Ruel in 1891, after the Japanese print exhibition at the École des Beaux-Arts in April 1890) [FAMSF; Met; AIC; Wikipedia summary]:
1. **Drawing.** A full-size drawing on paper [FAMSF].
2. **Transfer by soft-ground tracing.** "After coating a metal plate in soft ground ... Cassatt placed her drawing on top of the plate. She then traced over the drawing." The back of one drawing "bears faint lines of soft-ground that adhered to it in the process of tracing the design to transfer it to the copperplate" [FAMSF; Met Maternal Caress drawing].
   - Uncertain: how the design reached the second and third plates. It could have been a second tracing, or an offset of a proof from the first plate. It is in [M&S 1989], which I could not read.
3. **Drypoint lines** for the figures, cut directly into the copper [Met].
4. **Acid and aquatint** for tone and flat color ("she used drypoint for line work and soft ground or aquatint for color") [FAMSF; Brooklyn].
   - Uncertain: how the aquatint grain was laid (rosin dust is the usual method).
5. **Two or three plates per print**, each carrying several colors [FAMSF; Met; AIC]:
   - Colors next to each other that should not mix (the brown of a desk and the blue of a dress) went on different plates.
   - Several colors on one plate were applied **à la poupée**: dabbed into their areas with small wads of cloth.
   - Some blue from a dress has spread into flowers inked on the same plate [FAMSF].
   - The color was "painted on the plate as it was to appear in the proof" [blog quote, uncertain].
6. **Registration.** "All three plates in perfect registration onto one sheet." One demonstration describes multiple copper plates "aligned precisely with pins" [FAMSF; PMA video summary]. The pin method is uncertain in detail.
7. **Printing with a professional.** Each sheet is inscribed "Imprimée par l'artiste et M. Leroy" (Modeste or M. Leroy, active c. 1875–1900), edition of 25 [Met; AIC; Amon Carter].
   - She described it as hard work: "Sometimes we worked all day (eight hours) both as hard as we could work and only printed eight or ten proofs in the day" [letter quoted in Wikipedia summary].
   - Every impression was inked by hand, so impressions differ. This is inferred from the method; no source states it outright.
8. **States.**
   - The set of ten ran to 4–6 states: a toilette print 5 states, a mother-and-child print 6 [Met].
   - Later color prints ran to 10–11 states: c. 1893–94, from three plates [Met; eclecticlight 2026].
9. **Papers.**
   - Laid papers, off-white or ivory, some with watermarks "ED&Cie" and "PL BAS" [Ellis 2016; AIC; dealer listing].
   - A greenish-gray laid sheet [AIC], and a later print on green paper [Met].
   - Impressions on the watermarked papers have discolored and shifted the print's colors relative to impressions on white sheets [Ellis 2016].
   - The sheets are about 37 × 25 cm to 43 × 30 cm [FAMSF; dealer listing].

**Monotype.** One c. 1894 color print combines drypoint and aquatint "to which she added monotype": ink brushed on a plate and printed once [eclecticlight 2026]. It was perhaps prompted by Degas. This is the only instance I found. Uncertain.

**Lithography.** A few lithographs are counted in her total print output [dealer listing]. Not studied here.

## 6. Transfer and tracing

- **Soft-ground tracing** carried a drawing onto a plate (§5) [Met; FAMSF].
- **Counterproofs** mirrored a pastel onto a damp sheet (§2) [Adelson].
- **Squaring or pouncing** for paintings: not found. Uncertain.
- **Studies feeding later works.** Figures recur across pastels, counterproofs and prints. The tools for this were drawings and tracings, not cartoons, as far as is recorded.

## 7. Strokes, edges and finish

**Faces against everything else.** "She would always retain a high degree of finish in her sitters' faces, [yet] she abandoned this treatment in their garments and surroundings" [Stratis 1998 via Christie's]. In pastel she would "carefully blend and stump the pastel to achieve gradations of tone in the shadows, while using a precise line to record facial features". Looser strokes "evoke, rather than delineate" the surroundings.

**Strokes.**
- Less finished pastels are "constructed of slashing diagonal strokes" [PMA 2024 via Weiss]. Diagonal strokes laying in color are "a feature of many of Degas' pastel paintings" too [eclecticlight 2022].
- In one, "unblended blue chalk was laid atop a pink base to cool the tones", suggesting sheer fabric and movement. The flesh in the same group was "applied more delicately" [PMA 2024 via Weiss].
- She drew with "fingertips" as well as stick, brush and needle [FAMSF exhibition page].
- Zigzag strokes: not named in any source I read. Reproductions of the 1890s–1900s pastels show loose zigzag and parallel strokes in dresses and backgrounds. That is my observation, not a source. Uncertain.

**How much paper shows.** This varies within a sheet.
- Faces are worked up. Dresses, backgrounds and corners are often sketched in open strokes over the colored paper.
- Some areas are left bare on purpose as "nonfini" (deliberately unfinished) [Met M&C].
- Late sheets are dense in the flesh and open elsewhere [Met M&C; Stratis 1998].

**Late work.** Around 1910 she "pared down her earlier explorations with materials and reduced her techniques to only a few" [Stratis 1998 via Christie's]: pastel on paper on canvas, the colored sheet showing, flesh worked densely. Failing eyesight ended her work by about 1914 [eclecticlight 2026].
