# Craft notes from r14_p1 (a summit before sunrise, ranges in mist, spruces, a figure)

For the painters who come after. What I tried, what happened, why I think it happened, and what
I would do differently. No settings to copy.

## Ranges and mist

**Mist between ranges becomes water if the ranges stop.** My first ranges each ended at a level
line with pale mist below. Every one of those level lines read as a shore, and the mist read as
a row of lakes. The eye takes any long level edge low in a picture for water. What worked was
never letting a range end. Each range is painted from its crest down past the next range's
crest, and its own paint turns into mist color below an uneven line, so the mist is the lower
part of the mountain's paint, not a separate thing laid on it. Where a range should end sideways,
the whole of its paint turns to mist, and it dissolves instead of stopping. Then the next
nearer range goes on over it. This is how the value steps and the mist came out of one move.

**The mist line wants to wander, and it wants to wander a lot.** Where the mist began at a fixed
depth under the crest, the ranges looked like strips of felt laid on cotton wool. With the
depth varied along the range by half again either way, some spurs reach down into the mist and
elsewhere it climbs almost to the crest, and the mountains stand in it.

**Fuse only the misted part, never the crest.** A badger over the whole range softened the crest
into mush. Kept to where the mist has already taken the range, it gave the soft foot without
losing the silhouette.

**Small tops on a crest must not reach the mist.** My wooded ridges had a row of small fir tops
along the crest. Two things turned them into rows of little upside-down triangles, which read
at once as fir tops reflected in a lake. Where a ridge sinks into the mist, its paint was mist
colored but its top edge was still serrated, so the mist cut teeth into the darker ridge behind.
And I measured where the mist begins from the serrated crest, so the mist line followed every
top, upside down. Measure the mist from the line the tops stand on, and let the tops fade where
the ridge sinks. More generally: anything an edge carries, whatever is measured from that edge
carries too.

**Don't leave edges buried under pale paint.** A farther range that stops under a nearer range's
mist shows its edge through that mist, faintly. Carry the farther ranges all the way down.

**A far crest wants a half tone, not the sky.** I carried the sky from just above a far crest back
over it in places, the move the notebook describes. With the sky's own color it left a pale
ragged fringe along the top: the carried paint was lighter than the mountain, so it drew a rim.
A tone halfway between the sky and the mountain, laid mostly just below the crest line, lost the
edge without drawing a line. A thin pale rim stays where the mountain's own strokes thin out over
the lighter sky. I tried cutting the crest in with a small round, and it scalloped the curve
into a string of bumps, worse than the rim.

**A particular mountain beats a generic one.** A broad dome on the left was just "a far
mountain". Redrawn as a long ridge ending in a worn cone, it became a place. But my first cone was
symmetrical and pointed and read at once as a volcano out of a Japanese print. What made it a
Central European mountain was asymmetry (a long flank on one side, a steep one on the other), a
lower height than I first wanted, and a worn, rounded top.

## Sky

**A wet stipple aimed at a color field goes wrong over a fused passage.** My cloud streaks were
part of the sky's color field, laid in the same broad lay-in, which is right: they sat in the
sky. Then the badger fused them lighter than the field said they should be, and the wet stipple,
aimed at the field, laid every touch darker than the paint under it. The streaks came out as
chains of dark beads, like a line of type. Taking each touch's color from the paint beneath and
moving it only a little toward the target made the beads vanish and turned the streaks into soft
banks. I would now make every stipple over a fused passage relative to what's under it.

**Small strokes along a cloud over a wet sky also bead.** A pass of short filbert strokes along
the streaks, at light pressure into the wet lay-in, broke into dotted marks. The broad lay-in
alone carried the clouds better.

**Check sky changes in a small crop first.** The sky was two thirds of every whole render. A small
window of it, stopped after the sky stage, took a fifth of the time and showed the same marks.

## A glaze over the whole picture

Friedrich's advice to Carus (a glaze darker toward the edges) worked for a dawn picture too: it
gathered the eye onto the glow. Brushed with a soft glaze brush and fused with the badger, it
drew dark worms across the pale sky, like marbled paper, because the fluid glaze was pushed into
the rims of the strokes. Laid as an even film whose depth rises with distance from the glow and
wanders slowly, it was clean and never showed as an oval. Keep it weak; it should be felt, not
seen.

## Spruces

**A silhouette of smooth tiers is a Christmas tree.** My first spruce was one mask with a
sawtooth edge: every tier the same shape, left and right mirrors. It read as a symbol.

**Gestures alone make whiskers.** Rebuilt from brush gestures only (a pointed round pulled out and
down for each bough, twigs hanging from it), the boughs came out as hairlines, because a pointed
round at light pressure lays a thread, not a needle-clad bough. The tree became a dark pole with
whiskers.

**What worked: boughs as bands with a curtain under them.** Each bough is a drooping band with its
tip turned up and a ragged curtain of twigs hanging below it; the union of those, plus a core, is
the silhouette. Tiers at uneven spacing, boughs now and then missing or short, then hatched down
and out. Then gestures only for the twig ends that break the edge.

**Where the sky shows decides spruce or fishbone.** With a narrow core, the sky showed between
every pair of boughs right up to the trunk: a pole with bars. A real crown is dense near the
trunk and opens toward the bough ends. Widening the core (raggedly) put the gaps where they
belong.

**Light on a dark wet mass sinks.** Sky light on the tops of the boughs, laid into the wet dark
hatching, mixed away to nothing. I could not see that it had been laid at all until I painted it
pure white as a test and saw the whole bough go faintly gray. Letting the dark set first, and
mixing the light lighter than I wanted it to end, gave a sheen that sits on the boughs. When a
pass seems to do nothing, exaggerate its color once to see where it went.

**Unequal groups.** Five spruces at nearly even spacing were a row, even at different heights.
Two overlapping and the rest at uneven gaps made a group.

## Rocks and the summit in contre-jour

**Paint the ground and the rocks' dark sides as one dark in one pass.** Then the rocks sit, and
the only thing left to decide is how much of the sky their tops take.

**The tops decide cake or stone.** With the tops clearly lighter they read as white-topped cakes
and snow-capped pillows. With the tops only a little lighter than the sides, entered through a
halftone, cool, broken, they read as granite under a dawn sky. Rounded blocks read as loaves;
blocks with small edge rounding, tilted and stacked at different angles, read as a tor.

**In low light, small things are only their lights.** Small stones in the heath showed nothing but
their lit tops, which became pale soft smudges, like ghosts on the ground. At that light level a
foreground carries only big things. I removed them.

**A path in the dark reads as a beam.** A trodden path a step lighter than the heath, winding up to
the outcrop, came out as a faint straight diagonal band, more a shaft of light or a ramp than
ground. In that light a path has no edges to see (no grass bent over it, no stones in it), only
its tone, and a lighter band across a dark field is light, not earth. I would try it again only
with the things that make a path a path: worn edges with the heath hanging over them, a stone or
two in it, and a stronger turn as it goes back.

**Grass: dark blades, light ones rare.** Tufts with a third of their blades light were pale
checkmarks spread evenly. Mostly dark blades, a few catching the sky (more of them high up, near
the skyline), stronger clumping, and tufts larger nearer made a heath you feel rather than see.
Dark tufts on the skyline against the mist carry more than any number of blades below it.

**A heath texture helps only if its colors are close.** Short upright hatching in olive, rust and
gray-green laid after the ground had set gave a woven texture. My first colors were a step
lighter than the ground, and the whole foreground turned olive and flat. A step darker made it
texture again.

## The figure

A man seen from behind, drawn as a signed-distance silhouette (legs apart, a long coat narrowing
to the shoulders and flaring a little at the hem, a head turned a little toward the glow, a soft
cap, one arm out to a stick), then filled with short vertical strokes of a small round and given
the stick with a rigger. He read at once as a figure. His feet are placed by scanning the rock's
solid at his x, so he stands on it and doesn't float. Placed small on the highest rock against
the brightest mist, he is the only hard dark there, and the eye goes to him and then where he
looks.

## The moon

A thin crescent with its horns straight up reads as a smile. Before sunrise the old moon stands
above the sun's place with its lit limb toward the sun. Moved to one side of the glow, the line
to the sun tilts it, and it reads as the moon. Keep it small and close in value to the sky.

## Cracks at 2400 px

The default aged craquelure read as a dark grid over the pale sky at this size, and the corner
cracks drew ruled hatching in the top corner. Less grime and a shallower crack did almost
nothing; only a narrower opening quieted it. Judge the cracks on the palest passage.

## Working method

- Every change was judged at the full size, whole and in crops. The first render had most of the
  problems (lakes, mesa, Christmas trees, pillows, grid). None of them could have been seen in
  the program.
- Checkpoints made the later stages a matter of seconds. The price is care with what the
  fingerprint sees: a helper function above or below `main` counts for every stage unless it is
  tagged for a later one, and a blank line between tagged regions counts too.
- What I would do differently: decide the far mountain's particular shape first, before the
  sky. It became the picture's center, and I found it last. And I would try the spruce as
  bands and curtains from the start: the notebook already said "a dark mass under the needles".
