# George Inness: materials and method

*A sourced technical report. Every claim cites a source key; the sources
are listed at the end. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified,
disputed or taken from a single object or a single witness.*

## Summary

Inness painted in oil, mostly on canvas, sometimes on wood panel or board
[MET; ARTIC; SAAM]. He usually began on a toned canvas: a light-brown
stain of raw umber [SON p.251] or, in one account, a transparent stain of
Venetian red on white canvas [SHEL p.32]. A few pictures were scrubbed in
thinly on clean white canvas [SON pp.235–236, 260]. Over a charcoal or
pencil drawing he built the picture in many campaigns. Transparent paint
thinned with turpentine and a siccative was rubbed and scrubbed in, then
lights were scratched out with a thumbnail or brush handle or wiped out
with a rag [MAN pp.31–33]. Opaque lights came next and shadows were kept
transparent. Glazes went over everything, then more opaque paint and
scumbles, again and again [SON pp.252–254; MAN pp.35–36]. He reworked
finished, exhibited and even sold pictures. Technical examination shows
many layers, wet-into-wet work and paint dragged over dry or half-dry
underlayers, with traction crackle in thick brown passages [NGA96
pp.355–356; CORC n.10]. His documented pigments come from eyewitnesses,
not laboratory analysis. No published pigment analysis, medium analysis
or thread count for his work was found.

**Terms.** *Stain/imprimatura*: a thin transparent color over the ground.
*Glaze*: a transparent layer thinned with medium. *Scumble*: an opaque or
semi-opaque layer worked thinly over paint. *Siccative/drier*: an additive
that speeds drying. *Traction crackle*: wide cracks where a paint layer
has shrunk over a softer one. *IRR (infrared reflectography)*: imaging
that shows carbon-based drawing under paint. *X-radiography*: imaging
that shows lead-rich (dense) paint and hidden earlier compositions.

## 1. Supports

- **Canvas.** Almost all museum records describe his oils as oil on canvas
  [MET; ARTIC]. Three canvases examined at the National Gallery of Art:
  a "medium-weight, plain-weave, double-threaded fabric" (c.1856); a
  "relatively fine, plain-weave fabric" (1874); a "finely woven twill"
  with cusping along the top and bottom cut edges (1872–74) [NGA96 pp.350,
  355, 356]. Three fragments of one large canvas of the 1860s have
  "identical canvas weaves" [WAL].
- **Panels and boards.** Some oils are on wood panel (1874, 1885, 1890),
  millboard (1882) or "artist's board" (c.1865–75) [ARTIC; SAAM].
- **Thread count.** No count appeared in accessible sources. *Gap.*
- **Sizes.** From small boards and panels of about 41 × 61 cm [SAAM;
  ARTIC] to canvases of about 200 × 300 cm [MET]. Many late canvases
  (1884–94) measure about 76 × 114 cm (30 × 45 in.) [MET; ARTIC; SAAM].
  A friend describes a new canvas "about 20x30 inches" put on the easel
  for a one-hour demonstration [MAN p.34].
- **Reused canvases.** He painted on "a new canvas or an old one." When
  no fresh canvas was ready "he painted on anything that was convenient,"
  and "the older work was sacrificed to the new" [MAN p.31]. He once
  painted a new picture in two hours over a large earlier landscape; the
  earlier composition's outline stayed visible under the new paint [MAN
  pp.28–29].
- **Cut-down canvases.** A large canvas damaged in 1880 was divided into
  pieces that he repaired and repainted [WAL].

## 2. Grounds and toning

- **Commercial grounds.** Grounds recorded by the National Gallery: "a
  moderately thin off-white ground" (c.1856); "a pale yellow colored
  ground" (1874); "a white ground … followed by a reddish-brown
  imprimatura layer" (1872–74) [NGA96 pp.350, 355, 356]. Who applied them
  is not stated. *Gap.*
- **Raw umber stain.** His son writes that "his method was generally to
  stain a canvas with a light-brown tint, say of raw umber," and to take
  it outdoors when dry [SON p.251].
- **Venetian red stain.** A critic and neighbor describes staining "the
  white canvas with Venetian red, but not enough to lose the sense of
  transparency." The red was allowed "to come through as a part of the
  color" in thin shadows [SHEL p.32]. The same writer dates this method to
  the 1870s. *Uncertain* (single account; dating is his).
- **Clean white canvas.** Some pictures were painted "on a clean white
  canvas, contrary to his usual method." The colors were "frotted, or
  scrubbed in, very thinly, the texture of the canvas being visible
  through the film" [SON pp.235–236]. Another was done "on a pure white
  canvas with thin washes or scrubs of color no thicker than water" [SON
  p.260].
- **Isolating layer before repainting.** When he turned one Italian
  landscape from a morning to an afternoon scene, he "applied a
  rose-colored isolating layer over the original foreground and then
  painted over it" [NGA96 p.355]. *Single object.*
- **His own view (1878).** He wrote that painters differ: some want "a
  smooth canvas and others a rough canvas," some "a white canvas and
  others a stained canvas," so "there are no absolute rules about methods
  of painting" [HARP p.458].

## 3. Underdrawing and lay-in

- **Outdoor studies.** He would "draw in most carefully with charcoal or
  pencil the forms," including "every little crook in the limb." Then
  "with raw umber and some strong drier he would go over all the
  outlines, correcting here and there" [SON pp.251–252].
- **Charcoal, pencil, black.** Another account: charcoal outlines
  "confirmed … with a pencil," with "a few of the prominent shadows" put
  in with "a little ivory-black on a brush" [SHEL p.32].
- **Under the paint.** IRR of an early canvas shows "a ruled grid pattern"
  that matches a ruled study drawing [NGA96 p.350]. IRR of a canvas of
  1872–74 shows underdrawing "possibly in charcoal, consisting of several
  gently curving horizontal lines" [NGA96 p.356].
- **Late work.** On a new canvas "the subject was drawn in with a few bold
  strokes" [MAN pp.31–32]. One late canvas (about 1891) is catalogued as
  "oil and crayon or charcoal on canvas" [MET].
- **Figures.** In 1881 he wrote that he had his canvas "stretched and the
  figures drawn in charcoal from the sketch" [SON p.150].
- **Drawing by another hand.** For a short time he had a younger painter
  draw in new canvases from his sketches. The arrangement "did not last
  long" [MAN p.30].
- A student study of his underdrawing exists (ANAGPIC paper) but was not
  accessible. *Gap.*

- **A short palette.** In a demonstration he told other painters "you use
  too many colors … All that you need to begin a picture with is yellow
  ochre, white and black," and added blue at the end [MAN pp.33–34].
- **Greens.** No green pigment is named in these sources. "Greens with
  umber" made shadow color [SON p.235]. A friend writes that his
  mainly-green pictures "are nearly always pieces of direct painting"
  [MAN p.27]. *Gap* (green pigments unknown).
- **Gray colors.** In 1882 he wrote that his pictures "are painted with
  very gray colors" [SON p.157].

## 5. Media, driers and varnish

- **Vehicle.** In the transparent lay-in "the pigment was always
  transparent and thinned with a vehicle—turpentine and Siccatif de
  Haarlem, or Siccatif de Courtrai if he was in haste for the drying" [MAN
  p.32]. An 1890 chemistry of painting groups both siccatives with those
  that "owe their efficacy to lead, or are resinous mixtures" [CH p.97].
  Exact period recipes are *unverified*.
- **Quick drying.** Outdoors he used "a quick-drying medium which enabled
  him to glaze his picture every day"; "due to the vehicle he had used,
  the canvas would be dry" the next day [SON p.252]. Glazes were "thinned
  down with oil or some such medium to make it flow" [SON p.253].
- **A thick medium.** In one studio session he used "a medium that looked
  like Spaulding's glue" (a commercial glue) with raw umber [SON p.254].
  Its identity is *unknown*.
- **Resinous additions.** Traction crackle and "crawl" of paint in one
  foreground are attributed to "bitumen or a resinous admixture" [NGA96
  p.355]. *Not analyzed.*
- **Oil type.** No analysis of his binding oil was found. *Gap.*
- **Varnish.** On one early canvas (1851) a thick bleached-shellac
  (white lac) layer lay beneath later varnish, "probably applied by Inness
  himself," and was identified by THM-Py-GCMS [SUTH; AIC-blog]. *Single
  object.*

## 6. Layers and application

- **Two starts.** A friend with "full opportunity to witness his
  processes" says his method depended on "whether it was a new canvas or
  an old one" [MAN pp.30–31].
- **Transparent start on a new canvas.** Tones were "not extremely
  varied," and the full contrast was not sought. As the thin paint "set or
  grew tacky, it was rubbed and scrubbed into the canvas, lights were
  scratched out with thumb nail or brush handle, or wiped out with a rag."
  The brush "was spread with extreme force, as it scrubbed until in its
  scratching there developed … grass, or weeds or trees." As the color
  "tightened in the drying, the forms were sharpened … with clear, thin
  color." There was not yet "any touch of opaque color." A few lights were
  sharpened by scratching, "perhaps with a knife, more often with the
  finger nail." This stage "had to be done at one continuous sitting,"
  and some pictures were left in it [MAN pp.32–34].
- **Carrying on.** He would "first establish one or two crisp important
  notes of white, or pure color." Then, "with color not fully opaque," he
  stated the lights in the larger masses, "gradually increasing the
  opacity" until "the lights [were] opaque and shadows transparent." The
  sky was elaborated and the picture left to dry. Later "some great wash
  or rub of color would be spread all over it, and certain parts wiped
  out," worked with "brushes which were little better than mops." The wash
  sometimes dried, but "more often" he worked into it wet. Final work was
  "skilful placings of grey—the middle tone of black and white" [MAN
  pp.35–36].
- **Outdoor study sequence.** Day one: "paint on the lights or opaque
  parts … as near the local color … as he could, and the sky a rather
  neutral tone of yellow ocher, black and white." Day two: "rub in the
  shadows, always keeping them transparent." Then glaze (umber, black,
  sienna or cobalt) and paint opaque lights back into it. If the picture
  darkened too much, he scumbled "white, yellow ocher, or cadmium," which
  "always has to be worked in," and waited a day for it to dry before
  glazing again: "glazing down and painting up the lights, rubbing and
  scrubbing." These studies were then "worked over in the studio" [SON
  pp.252–253].
- **Another sequence.** He "began anywhere to paint, and worked in mass
  from generals to particulars," shadows "thin and transparent," with
  glazing, "delicate touching and scumbling" last [SHEL p.32].
- **Direct painting.** He sometimes "painted frankly" [SON p.254]. One
  picture painted in a day for a mis-sized frame had "no glazes, no
  building up of textures by repeated paintings" [MAN p.24]. A one-hour
  demonstration used the transparent method "except that he used
  partially opaque colors" [MAN p.34].
- **Wholesale changes.** Late in a picture he would glaze a whole area
  with ivory black and wipe some of it out [SON p.129]. He would cover a
  passage with white paint "as if he were plastering a wall" [SON p.139].
  He would spread "a broad scumble of orange-chrome over the most
  delicate … gray effect," then recover it with black or blue [MI95
  p.267].
- **What examination shows.** Early (c.1856): paint in "a medium-thick
  film," impasto in the foreground highlights, "otherwise wet-over-dry"
  [NGA96 p.350]. 1872–74: "built up of many layers in a wet-into-wet
  technique and manipulated by brush, brush handle, and rubbing"; "a
  toned glaze covers the sky, giving the blue paint a yellow cast" [NGA96
  p.356]. Late (1891): "wet-into-wet," with paint dragged "over layers
  that had already dried or into incompletely dried underlayers." Tree
  trunks have "thick dabs and convoluted strokes of opaque paint";
  "palette scraping may have been incorporated" [CORC n.10, citing a
  2005 technical report]. On a large canvas of the 1860s, "layer upon
  layer of very distinct colors, which did not blend into each other"
  [WAL].
- **Thin and thick together.** One thinly washed picture has a single
  thick passage, "a solid dab of white," and small forms "indicated not
  by paint, but by a few deft and telling scratches of the brush-handle"
  [SON pp.260–261]. On another, a thick, solid sky was glazed "all over
  with raw sienna" when dry [SON p.236].
- **Tools.** Bristle brushes, worn until they spread "their bristles"
  [MI95 p.264]; "the largest brush he could find" for a scrubbed glaze
  [SON p.254]; the thumb, used to draw "the color together with a few
  marvelous sweeps—as was often his habit" [AW17 p.253]; thumbnail,
  fingernail, brush handle and knife for scratching [MAN pp.32–33; MI95
  p.264]; rags, including his shirt [SON pp.54, 130]; a palette knife
  to scrape off paint [SON p.145]; a maulstick [SHEL p.32].
- **Color gauges.** A visitor once found "little spots of pure color at
  different points of the canvas," kept "as a key until practically the
  last stroke" [AW17 p.253]. *Uncertain* (single account).
- **No fixed order.** The same visitor heard him announce on one day
  "paint your undertones in warm, rich color; then go over them with cool
  tones," and the reverse the next day [AW17 p.252].

## 7. Studio habits

- **Many canvases at once.** "On the dozen or more canvases in his studio
  he worked as the humor seized him, going from one to another with
  palette and maul-stick, and always standing when painting." He "often …
  painted fifteen hours a day" [SHEL p.32].
- **Bare workrooms.** "His studios were nearly always old barns," with
  "one chair, an easel, and his tubes of paint" [SON p.42].
- **Outdoors, then indoors.** Serious outdoor studies could take "a week or
  more at a time" [SON p.252]. He also made charcoal sketches [SON p.150].
- **Reworking finished pictures.** After a picture was sold he repainted a
  calm sea as a storm, found he could not "rub it off because the paint
  underneath is wet," and the next day "with the aid of a palette-knife
  he scraped off the raging sea" and painted a meadow in its place [SON
  pp.144–145]. He turned a patron's spring landscape into a storm at sea
  in one day [MAN pp.37–38]. He retouched a large canvas "very
  considerably after it was hung" at an exhibition in 1886 and changed it
  again in 1891 [CORC p.174]. X-radiography shows complete revisions
  [NGA96 pp.355, 356].
- **Pictures over pictures.** A friend reports "a common saying among
  painters" that he "has painted out more good pictures than anyone else
  ever painted" [MAN p.23].

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Traction crackle.** "Traction crackle is found in thicker areas of
  brown paint" [NGA96 p.356]. In one repainted canvas "the sky has cracked
  severely," and bitumen or a resin addition "probably also caused the
  considerable traction crackle and 'crawl' of paint in the foreground"
  [NGA96 p.355].
- **Abrasion or scraping.** On one canvas "it is difficult to determine
  what is the result of damage and what was intentional scraping by the
  artist during repainting" [NGA96 p.355].
- **Cleaning risk.** Restorers in the 1930s ruined one of his canvases
  when they removed his own retouching, mistaking it for old restoration
  [WAL]. A well-preserved surface is "somewhat unusual for Inness' work"
  [AIC-blog].
- **Old paint showing through.** On one overpainted canvas the earlier
  composition's "still evident outline" showed under the new paint [MAN
  p.29]. Cracks in an early canvas reveal a lower paint layer [NGA96
  p.350].
