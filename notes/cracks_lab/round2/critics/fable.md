# Craquelure judgment: A–E

## 1. Ranking (most real first)

1. **D** — long, gently sweeping primaries with short secondaries hanging off them, spacing that actually varies and a slight lean down to the right in the sky (about 8° off vertical); the only one with a hint of hierarchy.
2. **C** — finer, more branched cells with a soft vertical drift rather than columns; closest to a fine network on thin paint, but cells are all one size and it has no lean.
3. **E** — A with more cross-bridging; still evenly spaced vertical columns with ladder rungs.
4. **B** — near-isotropic net of smooth S-curved arcs, all the same radius; looks like crazing on a glazed pot or a smoothed Voronoi, not paint on linen.
5. **A** — strongly vertical, evenly spaced hairlines with tidy horizontal rungs, plus one dead-straight vertical crack near the right edge running about half the picture height (x≈927 in the whole); the "digital" version the owner already rejected.

## 2. What reads as fake

- **Uniform amount.** Crack coverage is 0.7–1.1% of the whole in all five, the same over sky, snow, moon and tree. No blank stretches, no dense patches.
- **Uniform width and contrast.** Every crack is a one-pixel hairline about 5 gray levels darker than the sky, end to end. Nothing widens, fades or disappears mid-run.
- **Spacing.** A and E: primaries 18–25 px apart everywhere, like bark or a fingerprint. B: cells all one size.
- **Straightness.** A and E are parallel verticals with low-frequency wobble (noise-displaced straight lines) instead of short straight runs with kinks. B is the opposite: everything curves.
- **Junctions.** A/E rungs meet primaries at neat right angles at even intervals; B has rounded Y-junctions. Real secondaries hit older cracks at T-junctions at irregular intervals.
- **Corners.** All five share identical concentric arcs hugging all four corners. Real corner stress gives diagonal spokes and lines parallel to the stretcher bars, not rings.
- **Ignoring the paint.** The network runs straight across the tree, snow highlights and figure with the same geometry, merely inverted to pale-on-dark. It never bends along a stroke edge or stops at a later thick stroke.
- Polarity (dark on light, pale on dark) is right, but there is no grime-darkened segment next to a clean one and no relief.

## 3. How real Friedrich-era craquelure differs

- **Where it shows.** Age cracks start in ground and canvas and run through every layer, but visibility depends on what is on top. In thin pale sky (lead white over a light ground) the net is fine, dense and dark with grime. In dark thin passages the pale ground shows, so cracks read as faint light lines or not at all. Thick whites crack wider with cupped islands. High confidence.
- **Cell size tracks thickness.** Thicker, more brittle paint gives larger cells and wider cracks; thin paint gives finer, denser ones. One painting carries two or three distinct networks, not one field. High confidence.
- **Clustering.** Cracks concentrate along stretcher-bar lines, at corners (diagonal spokes), around old damage and in heavy-white passages, with quiet zones between. Amount varies enormously painting to painting. High confidence on mechanisms, medium on any one picture.
- **Direction.** Bias comes from weave and stretcher tension: cracks align loosely with warp or weft, one axis dominant, the other as secondaries, plus diagonals near corners. It is a statistical drift, not columns. Medium-high confidence.
- **Hierarchy.** Long primaries form first; later secondaries are shorter and finer and end in T-junctions. Cells are unequal polygons. High confidence.
- **Up close.** Width varies from hairline to visible groove within one crack; cupped edges give a thin bright edge and a shadow in raking light; the path is micro-jagged, follows brush texture and bends along stroke boundaries; later thick strokes carry their own coarser cracks or none. High confidence.
- **Friedrich specifics.** Thin, smooth, glazed paint over a light ground, so cracks stay fine and show mostly in skies. Medium confidence.

## 4. What to change, most important first

1. Drive density from a thickness/lightness map: dense fine net in pale sky, sparse or invisible in dark thin paint, coarser wider cracks in thick whites, with blank zones.
2. Add hierarchy: sparse long primaries, then secondaries that stop at them (T-junctions), cell sizes spanning a 3–4× range.
3. Replace wobble with short straight-ish segments joined by kinks; make direction a mild bias of 5–10° off vertical (as in D), not columns.
4. Vary width and contrast along each crack; add faint cupping (one bright edge, one dark).
5. Let the net respect the painting: bend along stroke edges, break at later thick strokes.
6. Swap the concentric corner arcs for diagonal corner cracks and faint stretcher-bar lines.
7. Randomize the overall amount per painting.
