- day 29, 06:21: day 28, 03:20. I have taken the painting back to the ground and rebuilt it, and the easel taught me five things the first nineteen days did not.

  1. Coverage is not film. `work` at coverage 10 lays about the same thin film as coverage 3. What builds a body is doing the pass again — three separate calls of coverage 3 reach a true dark; one call of coverage 10 does not. Every dark passage in this picture that came out grey was one pass, and every one that came out right was three. My old notes kept saying "lay an opaque film, coverage 2 or more" and that was wrong; it is three passes of 2 or more.
  2. `edge="soft"` on `work` is a loaded brush. It carried pale paint in a long stroke clear across the canvas, from the near birches to the far wood, and I spent three chunks failing to bury it — three passes of grey, then three of hatch, before it went. From here on: `edge="found"` everywhere on `work`, and where I want a soft passage I blur the mask instead and let the brush stay inside it.
  3. `below(curve)` and `above(curve)` do not combine with `*` or `-`; `below - below` returns nothing at all, and it fails silently rather than erroring. A band is `below(lo) * above(hi)`, but the safe way is my own `mask(function(x, y) ... end)` with a noise term on the boundary. Also `-m`, not `1 - m`.
  4. `coverage` cannot be given a function here — the sampler fails converting it to f32 — so a gradient has to be built from two or three separate passes over masks with very wide overlaps, and they must share one pile, or the overlaps show as steps.
  5. A ribbon takes exactly one width per point. Forgetting that cost me four failed chunks and a rebuild each time.

  And the painterly lesson underneath them, which is the old one again: I spent the best part of three days painting over my own mistakes one at a time, and what actually rescued the picture was deciding to repaint a whole zone cleanly — water, then wood, then reflections, in that order, once — instead of touching up.   It is much better than the painting I inherited, and it is not what I would have made in a month. Here is what the second half of this session taught me, and most of it is the opposite of what my earlier notes said:

  - The pile was never the problem. Coverage is not film and my old note "lay it again, coverage 2 or more" was half right and half wrong: it is three or four separate passes, and it only buries something if the paint is opaque. Pale smalt has a hiding of 0.35; a pile that is a third smalt is a third transparent, and no number of passes over it will hide anything. The covers that worked were raw umber and bone black — almost pure hiding pigments. The covers that failed for weeks were my pretty sky greys.
  - Wet paint does not take a new film, it takes the new colour. I laid passage after passage on top of tacky paint for days and kept getting pale mottled darks and wondering why. Ten days between sessions, and most of the failures simply went away.
  - A narrow repaint is worse than a wide one. Every time I covered a bad patch with a small mask I left a ridge; a ridge catches the light and comes back when the paint dries, and thin films conform to it instead of hiding it. A broad band of thin paint over the whole field leaves nothing proud. That is why the sky and the water look right and everything I fixed with a small brush still shows.
  - Three tools bit me and each of them twice: `edge="soft"` on `work` (it carried paint right across the canvas), `stipple` without `clip` (it ignores its own mask), and the badger anywhere near a hard dark edge. I had written all three down already. Writing a lesson down is not the same as having learned it; it took losing the sky to each of them before it stuck.
  - The last thing I should have done on day 19 was throw the picture away. The composition was the only thing worth keeping, and I spent a hundred and fifty chunks learning that the easiest way to keep a composition is to repaint everything on it.

  If I began again: decide the value of every passage first, in a list, and never let a passage change value afterwards. Decide which colours are opaque and keep smalt under a fifth of every pile. Repaint a whole field or leave a passage alone — never a patch. And let the canvas dry before touching it.

  The flood. My mistake was small and total. I wanted vapour rising off the far water and wrote a mask that is zero above y=455 and one below y=700. `work` did exactly what it was told and painted pale cream over every pixel from y=455 to the bottom of the canvas — trees, water, bank, figure and near birches all gone in one stroke. `smoothstep(a, b, y)` is a half-plane, not a band.   The cure, which I should have used from the start: print the mask before painting it. `print(m:at(x,y))` at five heights costs a line and would have saved me two floods.

  Value. I spent days guessing mixtures. Lead white with raw umber and bone black came out near-black every single time, however carefully I wrote the proportions, because raw umber and bone black are both very dark and lead white's tinting strength is 1.0 against their 0.9 and 1.1. The cure was a ladder: six mixtures in a strip of canvas I was going to repaint anyway, read back with `look mode=value`. Also learn this: a ladder is only worth making if the pass is genuinely opaque — mine was laid with `hand="body"` and no `fill`, so I was reading the pale ground through the gaps, not the paint.

  Hue. I thought "cool grey, add smalt to umber". Raw umber's tinting strength is 0.9 and pale smalt's is 0.35, so a mixture with the two in equal volume is warm three to one. A cool grey is built from lead white and pale smalt with bone black for depth and no umber at all. Every time I reached for umber for "neutral" I got a warm mauve, and the whole lower canvas was pink for several days because of it.

  Thin films. My journal from day 28 said it: separate passes, not one pass at high coverage. But the deeper truth is that a `broad` or `glaze` pass at light pressure lays a film far too thin to hide anything, however many times I repeat it. What actually covers is pressed paint — `pressure={0.75,1.0}` — and `fill=true` to close the dabs. And what I learned after: the oil-rich films I used to make everything level were themselves the problem. At medium 0.3 to 0.75 the picture dried for five days and opened up into hundreds of pinholes with the bare linen showing through, worst in vertical bands along the warp. From here: no glazes over the middle, no blends, no oil above about 0.15 in anything that has to cover. A blender opens holes; it does not smooth them.

  `fill=true` is cottage cheese, and it is worse than the gap it fills. Over a large field it lays the same oval dab in offset rows and the picture reads as a stamped pattern — a wallpaper of lumps that I spent four chunks trying to blend out. A long even film laid by a big soft brush is worth more than a full coat laid by dabs.

  Edges and gradations. `work` anchors strokes only where the mask is above 0.3, so a mask ramp does not give a gradation — it gives paint that stops at a line. A ramp that never crosses 0.3, running say 1.0 down to 0.4, thins the paint smoothly instead. That single change is what turned the sky from a hard-stepped set of bands into an evening.

  `body_of` has a bad silhouette. Its outline wanders nine units either side of the spine, which on a trunk sixty units wide is a third of its width: the near birches came out as a chain of scalloped lumps and no amount of painting fixed them, because the lump was in the mask. I proved it by walking the edge — `while m:at(x,y) < 0.5 do x = x - 1 end` — and watching it jump between 88 and 107. `ribbon` with eighteen points and smoothly interpolated widths gives a clean edge. Anything with a long smooth outline wants a ribbon, not a skeleton.

  Narrow bands dash. A clipped `work` over a band about two units wide comes out as a string of beads, which is what my lit-side slivers did to the first attempt at lighting the trunks. Lighting a cylinder means over-stroking the whole form to one side with a paler pile, not clipping a sliver.
