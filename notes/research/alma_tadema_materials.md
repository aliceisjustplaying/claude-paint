# Lawrence Alma-Tadema: materials and method

*A sourced technical report. Every claim cites a source key; the sources
are listed at the end. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified,
disputed or taken from a single object. Most of the evidence is
documentary (his statements, witnesses, catalogs, supplier records);
instrumental examination covers one early panel.*

## Summary

Alma-Tadema painted in oil on wooden panels and on canvas in roughly equal
numbers. Most of the panels are small, and the largest works are on canvas
[RA13]. He bought ready-prepared supports from London colormen and at
least once from a Brussels firm [NPG-W; NPG-M; NPG-R; NPG-W11]. The one
condition report that mentions his ground says it is white [SOTH11]. He
sketched directly onto the support, laid in with rough brushstrokes and
developed passages in thin layers; unfinished work records different
stages of completion within a single canvas [HEIJ p.169; LABEL; STRAND
p.613]. From 1865 he painted with an oil copal varnish ("copal à
l'huile") mixed into the paint, having used boiled oil before that [HEIJ
p.171 and nn.25–26]. A pupil published a palette attributed to him
around 1886 (§4) [MANUAL pp.12–14]. He reworked constantly, painting out
and scraping down finished passages, and this repainting is linked to
cracking in his paint [ZIM02; STAND p.100; STRAND p.613; HEIJ p.171]. A
2004 survey found no technical studies of his work [TOWN]. The only
published instrumental examination found is of a single early panel
[NGC]. No published cross-sections, medium analyses or pigment
identifications were found in the consulted sources.

**Terms.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layers between the support and
the paint. *Lay-in*: the first broad placing of shapes and colors.
*Painting out*: covering a finished passage with new paint. *Scraping
down*: removing paint with a knife or blade before repainting. *IRR
(infrared reflectography)*: imaging that shows carbon-based drawing under
paint. *Craquelure*: the network of cracks in paint or varnish. *Copal*:
a hard fossil resin, used in varnishes and media.

## 1. Supports

- **Panel and canvas.** The 1913 memorial exhibition catalog lists the
  support for about 140 works: about 73 panels and 70 canvases
  [RA13]. The wood species is rarely stated (see below).
- **Sizes.** Panels are mostly small; several measure about 30 × 23 cm
  [RA13; TATE]. A few are large: one of 1869 measures 28½ × 48 in.
  (72 × 122 cm), one of 1874 measures 26½ × 87½ in. (67 × 222 cm) and
  one of 1882 measures 86 × 26 in. (218 × 66 cm) [RA13]. The largest
  works are on canvas, for example 50 × 84 in. (127 × 213 cm), painted
  in 1888 [RA13].
- **Bought prepared.** In answers to the Royal Academy's "Technical forms
  of procedure" questionnaires he recorded panels and canvas from one
  London colorman in 1873, 1877 and 1881, and a panel from the Brussels
  firm Mommen in 1877–78 [NPG-W; NPG-M, both citing DW20]. A work of 1878
  and a small panel of 1889 bear the same colorman's marks; the 1889
  panel carries a "PREPARED PANEL" label [NPG-W; NPG-W11]. A panel of
  1909 and a work of 1902 carry labels of Roberson, another London
  colorman, with whom he had an account [NPG-R8; NPG-R; HKI].
- **Wood species.** One panel of 1868–69 (72.5 × 109 cm) is cataloged as
  "oil on mahogany panel" [BMAG]. An examined student-period panel (1860)
  is radially cut oak and was reused; IRR reveals an earlier painting
  underneath [NGC]. How many of his panels are mahogany is *unverified*.
- **Panel construction.** A panel of 1867 has a join; a crack follows the
  join along its upper edge [SOTH07]. *Single object.*
- **Canvas.** Weave, thread count and fiber are not documented in the
  sources found. *Gap.* One of his canvases in a London museum is still
  unlined [GETTY-CC].

## 2. Grounds

- **White.** A conservator's condition report on a panel of 1883 describes
  "the white ground revealed by the craquelure" and retouching laid in the
  cracks to hide it [SOTH11]. *Single object.*
- **Commercial preparation.** Because he recorded ready-made colorman
  panels and canvases (§1), his grounds were usually commercial primings,
  not his own [NPG-W; NPG-M]. *Inferred.* The Royal Academy forms asked
  whether the ground was absorbent or not and white or tinted [RA-arch].
  His answers were not found in accessible sources. *Gap.*
- **Composition and layers.** Published descriptions do not establish the
  composition, number of layers or thickness of his grounds [HEIJ; SI].
  *Gap.*

## 3. Drawing and lay-in

- **On the support, not on paper.** In 1895 he wrote: "I generally make a
  slight sketch of the picture I am going to Paint directly on the
  respective canvas or panel" [VW, quoting a statement of 1895; original
  publication not identified, *Uncertain*]. A biography of 1902 says he did
  little preparatory sketching: he "may be said not to sketch at all"
  [ZIM02].
- **Pencil.** In 1899 an interviewer saw a half-painted small canvas where
  "the paint and pencilled lines" both showed, and a large canvas "filled
  with pencilled outlines" for the next year's exhibition [STRAND p.613].
- **One early panel.** IRR of the 1860 panel revealed carbon-based
  underdrawing as well as the older painting beneath [NGC].
- **Brush lay-in.** An unfinished canvas of about 1873–75 records a rough
  brush lay-in, thin subsequent paint layers and passages at different
  stages of completion [HEIJ p.169; LABEL].
- **Working from life.** He reported painting from live models and
  checking details against his collection of photographs, which he
  arranged by subject [VW; STRAND p.613; HEIJ p.171].

- **Source and date of the palette.** The manual (2nd edition, 1887) is by
  a pupil, who attributes the listed palette to Alma-Tadema. The printed
  arrangement is the author's own; Alma-Tadema's arrangement is unstated
  [MANUAL pp.13–14].
- **Suppliers.** In 1885 a contemporary wrote that he himself used "one or
  two colours (Tadema I think _all_) from Mommen's in Brussels" [BARR
  vol.2 p.292]. *Uncertain* (hearsay). Blockx (a Belgian maker) sold him
  colors in 1894–1904 [NPG-B]. *Madderton's Notes for Artists* printed his
  palette, colors and medium in a series of 1902–04 [NPG-M]; that text was
  not found. *Gap.*
- **Other blues and greens.** No additional blue or green pigment was
  documented in the consulted sources.

## 5. Media and varnish

- **Boiled oil, then oil copal.** In a letter of 1902 he wrote that a
  painting of 1864 was the last in which he used "boiled oil." A painting
  of 1862 made with "the same colours and oils" had been "full of cracks"
  [HEIJ p.171 and n.25]. On a visit to Paris in 1865 he asked a French
  painter what preparations he used and learned "to paint with Copal à
  l'huile: the varnish protects the colours against the influence of the
  atmosphere but when mixed with oil does not prevent the oil from
  evaporating. Since then I have always used this Copal à l'huile de
  Durosin" [HEIJ p.171 and n.26]. "Durosin" is unexplained, possibly a
  maker's name [HEIJ n.26].
- **What copal à l'huile is.** An oil-based copal resin varnish [HEIJ
  n.26]. The proportions of his medium are undocumented.
- **Final varnish.** A panel of 1883 has "a thin clear varnish"; whether it
  is original is not stated [SOTH11].
- **Analysis.** None published. *Gap.*

## 6. Layers and application

- **Thin layers.** An unfinished work has thin paint layers [LABEL]. The
  early panel has thin wet-in-wet brush application and lead-white-rich
  paint of varying radiopacity; its density in X-rays suggests
  lead-white-rich paint, an inference, not a pigment analysis [NGC].
- **Order on the early panel.** Examination establishes successive
  applications, with accents added last [NGC]. *Single early object.*
- **Passages at different stages.** In the unfinished canvas some
  passages are carried to finish while others remain lay-in [LABEL; HEIJ
  p.169].
- **Thick paint.** A panel of 1867 has localized thick impasto with
  craquelure [SOTH07]. *Single object.* Film thicknesses are not
  published. *Gap.*
- **Brushwork.** A museum text describes fine brushstrokes [RIJKS].
- **Brushes.** He washed his own brushes [STAND p.120]. Brush types are
  not documented for him. *Gap.*

## 7. Working sequence and reworking

- **Painting out and scraping down.** In 1899: "For days I make no
  progress with a picture—there is so much painting-out" [STRAND p.604].
  "Some detail in a picture may be painted and repainted before it
  satisfies him" [STRAND p.613]. Witnesses describe repeated painting out
  and scraping down, including substantial changes to finished passages;
  a close friend wrote that one large canvas "represents the work of two
  or three pictures" [ZIM02; ZIM83; STAND p.100]. One large canvas was
  probably altered after it was first exhibited in 1894 [GETTY-SP
  pp.32–33].
- **Scraping to reuse.** The reused early panel was selectively scraped
  before repainting, "presumably to prevent the paint texture" of the
  earlier painting "from interfering" [NGC].
- **Time per picture.** Reported working times range from nearly two
  years for one small canvas to one to three years for some works [STRAND
  p.613].
- **Several at once.** "I always have two or three pictures in hand at the
  same time… I must turn from one picture to another for quickness and
  freshness of interest" [STRAND p.613]. He left a picture upside down on
  the easel after a day's work on it [STRAND p.613].
- **Photographs of the work in progress.** From 1866 a photographer
  photographed his paintings in progress. He used the prints "to check the
  tonal balances," adjusted the painting and had it photographed again
  [UMMA]. *Uncertain* (seen in a secondary summary only).
- **Working hours.** He worked every hour of daylight when at work on a
  picture [STAND p.120].

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Cracking.** "Because his canvases were regularly painted over and worked
  up, they often exhibit a great deal of craquelure" [HEIJ p.171]. He
  blamed the cracks in a work of 1862 on boiled oil and on its display
  conditions [HEIJ n.25]. On a panel of 1883 "deeper vertical craquelure"
  opens down to the white ground [SOTH11]. On a panel of 1867 craquelure is
  in the thick impasto [SOTH07].
- **Panels.** The 1883 panel is "very stable with no warping" [SOTH11]; the
  1867 panel "has not bowed" [SOTH07].
- **Canvas.** An unlined canvas developed tears "along the fold line" after
  repeated loans [GETTY-CC].

## 9. The tubes here

This studio's box follows the palette the manual attributes to him.
Names map as follows: flake white → lead white; light red → red earth;
cobalt → cobalt blue; ivory black → bone black. Naples yellow is supplied
as lead antimonate; what his tube contained is unverified. Pale cadmium
and viridian are supplied although his use of them is uncertain (§4).
Not supplied: raw umber and deep cadmium, which the manual says he used
"very little, if at all," and chrome yellow, which the manual rejects.

## 10. At this easel

- **Support.** The easel's support is plain-weave linen with ground
  layers mixed from the tube box. There are no panels, boards or other
  rigid supports.
- **Medium.** Paint has one control for added medium: an oil-medium
  share. There are no solvents, driers, resins, copal, megilp or varnish
  controls.
- **Removing paint.** There is no tool for scraping or abrading paint on
  the canvas.
- **This note.** The supports, media and ways of removing paint named
  above that the easel lacks are historical context; the easel guide
  documents what is available. Wiping a brush unloads the brush; it does
  not lift paint off the canvas.
