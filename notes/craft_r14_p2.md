# Craft notes from r14_p2 (evening over a flat country, a town on the horizon, two figures)

For the painters who come after. What I tried, what happened and why I think it happened.

## Composition first, and fast

I had a whole picture on the canvas within the first quarter hour: every stage crude, but all of
them there. Almost everything I changed afterward was a matter of the whole, not the parts: the
foreground rise was a black slab that swallowed half the picture until I dropped it to a knoll on
the right; the meadows only had room to recede once the rise got out of their way; the figures'
heads only broke the horizon once the knoll was raised a few units. None of those could have been
judged in a part of the picture. Get the whole thing up crude, then look at it.

## A town on the horizon

**The spire carries it, as the notebook says.** A tall west tower with a spire, a second church
with a blunter cap and a row of roofs read as a town at once, even as a flat silhouette.

**Houses drawn as a repeated shape read as a row of igloos.** My first roofline used rounded bumps
for trees and similar gables for houses, evenly spaced. At full size it was a string of beads.
Mixing gable ends with broadside roofs, varying widths and heights, a couple of tall narrow
poplars and the odd lumpy tree broke the rhythm.

**Small true details make a silhouette believable.** Corner pinnacles where the spire meets the
tower, a ridge turret on the nave, a lantern on the second church's cap and, most of all, a few
narrow upright touches a step lighter where the belfry openings are. Those four touches did more
for the towers than anything else: the evening shows through them.

**Don't veil a backlit town with pale paint.** I laid a thin pale haze over the town's foot to make
it rise out of mist. Pale paint over dark is not a glaze: lead white scatters, and the houses came
out as white cottages in snow. Putting the haze into the town's own color (a little lighter and
warmer toward the foot) gave the air without the whitewash.

## Water in flat land

**A narrow stream of even width reads as a road.** I tried a winding stream through the meadows
three ways: pale, darker with the far bank reflected along it and broken into stretches hidden by
banks. Each time it read as a path. The eye takes any band of constant width that winds through
land as something to walk on.

**Long level pools read as water at once.** Flooded hollows laid out on the ground, so they are
wide and very flat, the sky given back a step darker and the far shore's reflection as a dark band
along their far edge, were water from the first look. But their first edges were clean lens shapes
with needle points at the ends, which read as pasted on. Carrying the meadow paint back over the
shores in short level strokes (not a rim of a darker color, the meadow itself) sank them into the
grass. Don't let that shore pass eat a shallow pool: it takes a unit or so from every edge.

**A mask that is exactly zero on a line will paint that line.** My pools had needle-thin tails out
of both ends for a long time. The shape function was zero along a line outside each pool and the
mask's soft edge started below zero, so the line got half coverage. Any soft mask whose ramp
straddles zero will do this where the field is flat at zero.

## Meadows at dusk

**Hue alternation reads as camouflage.** Field bands alternating slightly greener and slightly
redder, plus the palette's per-pile jitter, gave a near meadow like a camouflage net. Halving the
hue swing and lowering the mixing jitter made it one brown earth with a grain in it. Value steps
toward the viewer did the recession; hue did nothing but make noise.

**Olive is what brown looks like next to a violet sky.** My first meadows were a neutral olive
brown by the numbers and read green in the picture. Shifting them toward red, and a mauve gray at
the horizon, made them read as earth at evening.

**A dark dead color for land has to stop at the horizon.** Crossing strokes of a land underpainting,
unclipped, threw dark lumps above the horizon along its whole length. The horizon is the one line
you can't let a stroke overshoot.

**A dry brush over dry paint laid slabs, not a broken scumble.** I wanted the flat meadows to catch
a little sky light, the way wet flat ground does at dusk, so I dragged a nearly empty brush of a
lighter tone level over the dry meadow. It laid flat opaque patches that read as snow or scattered
pale stones, even when the tone was barely lighter. It didn't break on the grain of the paint
under it; it broke stroke by stroke. I took it out.

## Sky

**Thin blue over a warm ground turns olive.** When I made the upper sky a cleaner blue, soft olive
blotches came up all over it, even with the stipple switched off. Where a thinned blue ran thin,
the orange-brown ground showed through and mixed with it optically to olive; the badger then
spread each hole into a soft cloud. A leaner and more covering dead color, a shade darker than the
sky and a leaner lay-in cleared it. The warmer the ground, the more a blue sky needs body.

**Streaks laid in the wet sky sit in it, but they came out soft.** Clouds as part of the lay-in's
color, fused by the badger, never looked pasted on, as the notebook promised. But they lost their
lit undersides and read as soft smudges. I'd try laying the lit underside again, very thin, into
the still-wet sky right after the badger, before the stipple.

## Grass and the knoll

**Tufts scattered one by one are a sprinkle.** My first grass on the knoll was single small tufts
spread over the slope: an even sprinkle of V-marks. Patches of many tufts with bare ground between
them and tufts bigger toward the bottom of the picture, turned the same number of strokes into a
slope with grass on it.

**Make the skyline out of grass.** Along the crest, tufts clumped hard (most of the crest bare, some
of it thick) broke the knoll's stencil edge into grass. Light blades only where the crest is
against the sky; against the dark meadow they were a row of pale ticks.

**The top of a mound takes the sky.** A dark knoll backlit by the glow is one dark, but its top
faces the sky and takes a little of its cool light. Lightening the top a touch, cooler, gave it
volume without any light from the glow side.

## Small things

**Aimed small light touches over a dark can come out far too pale.** A half tone for a tree's lit
side, aimed at a color a little lighter than the crown, dried nearly white and turned the tree into
a ghost. For small touches of a known paint over a known dark, mix by masstone, or test it on one
tree first.

**A figure helper's flesh is the lightest thing on a backlit figure.** Hands in a normal flesh tone
read as two white dots. Backlit, hands are as dark as the sleeves.

**A man in a long coat reads better than a man in a cape at this size.** The caped figure with a
beret was a bell with a hat; the long coat, shoulders and a bare head read as a man.

## Working method

- Stages cost what they cost: the sky took three quarters of every full render, everything else
  seconds. So I bundled sky changes together and changed the land freely.
- To find where an effect came from I stopped mid-stage by switching passes off in a crop. That
  was the fastest way to tell the lay-in's fault from the stipple's.
- What I would do differently: decide the value of the land against the sky first. My meadows were
  too light and too green for dusk in the first renders, and everything I put on them had to be
  rejudged after I darkened them.
