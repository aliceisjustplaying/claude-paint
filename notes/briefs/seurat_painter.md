# Before you compose: what makes a picture Seurat's

Read this before you draw anything. Materials and source keys are in
`notes/research/seurat_materials.md`; current craft and limitations are in
`notes/seurat.md`. Read `notes/principles.md`, `notes/easel_guide.md` and
`notes/techniques.md`. Work from written knowledge, never reference images.
Don't copy a known picture: find your own subject and give it his order.

## The principles

1. **Decide the large order before the small marks.** Build a few related
   horizontal bands and vertical accents. A diagonal or curve can interrupt
   them without undoing their calm. Seurat's coastal landscapes are geometric
   and often asymmetric, not ruler-perfect diagrams [NG-G; ORSAY]. For a first
   study, an invented quiet harbor or river reach is a better scale of ambition
   than a monumental crowd; the exact scene is yours.

2. **Make light and dark hold the picture.** His Conté studies define forms by
   adjacent tonal masses and let the paper's tooth interrupt them [YALE]. Plan
   your masses in a small monochrome scratch study, then judge the painting in
   value/squint view. Dots cannot save weak silhouettes or a confused light.

3. **Give figures a held, simplified presence.** Their intervals and silhouettes
   belong to the whole, not to a collection of detailed characters. The
   *Grande Jatte* sought the dignity of a classical frieze [AIC]. If you choose
   figures, begin with a few and let their poses carry them; do not substitute
   dot texture for drawing a body.

4. **Mix piles, then divide their colors on the canvas.** His physical mixtures
   include tinted blues, mixed violets, pinks and greens; white is central to
   much of the body paint [NG24]. Put related warm/cool and light/dark piles
   beside each other deliberately. Orange–blue and green–purple are documented
   recurring contrasts, not compulsory pairs in every passage. Judge the
   result by looking, not by solving a color function.

5. **Let the marks describe the passage.** Sweeps and crosses underlie dots in
   several works; the later touches are not all circles. Water can have small
   horizontal/vertical dashes, a near beach fatter strokes, distance smaller
   ones, faces finer touches than clothing [NG24 pp.27–30]. Choose by form and
   viewing distance. Neither a uniform screen nor random confetti is his hand.

6. **Separate color without killing the underpainting.** First lay thin colored
   masses and directional brushwork. Work wet-in-wet where it helps that stage;
   wait and check `drying` before clean separated touches. Use `b:touch`, short
   strokes or passage-sized `stipple` campaigns from actual piles. Let earlier
   paint remain visible where it earns its place [NG24]. This drying decision
   is easel craft, not a claimed timetable from Seurat.

7. **Look from near and far.** At a distance the colors integrate; close up the
   paint stays divided. Tiny complementary touches can become grey rather than
   brighter [NG24 p.29]. Test sizes and densities over your intended underlayer
   on scratch before committing. Don't blend the final surface merely to make
   a crop smooth.

8. **Make a border part of the painting, if you use one.** Later borders respond
   locally to the adjacent picture: an orange above blue sky, a violet/indigo
   beside yellow land are examples, not a universal recipe [NG-G]. Paint it
   with the brush. Don't add a decorative frame after the picture is finished.

## Before the first sitting

Use the `seurat` box (the studio build's `box-seurat` feature). Ask `tubes()`
what is actually available. Choose a limited selection from the
documented core palette; look at your knifed piles and adjust them. Test a few
touch widths, pressures and short drags on scratch, first over open paint and
then over a checked dry layer. The numbers are your trials, not recovered
measurements of Seurat's dots. Seek fresh intended color relationships, not
zinc-yellow browning as a style effect.

Work in bounded sittings: lay in, rest, look, revise, then develop one passage
at a time while retaining the whole. Real time between chunks doesn't dry the
canvas. Don't spend the entire sitting on a whole-canvas stipple pass. Follow
the studio's actual time limits. Keep the journal factual; give the integrator
the full image and log, and let them choose the evidence crops. Alice judges
the picture, not your dot count or your verdict.
