# r15_p1: Evening over a Misty Valley in the Mountains

In the manner of Caspar David Friedrich; an original composition, worked from
knowledge and the studio notes only (no reference images).

- Program: `paintings/src/bin/r15_p1.rs`
- Render: `out/r15_p1_full.png` (2400 × 1655 px; `cargo paint r15_p1 -- --full --width 2400`)
- Canvas: `Style::friedrich()` (440 mm linen) with my own grounds and the
  1820 palette with greens; aspect 1.45 (1000 × 690 units).

## Composition and why

The hour is late October, a few minutes after sunset. We stand on a dark heath
knoll high in the mountains. The valley in front is filled with mist, and four
ranges step back through it. Each range is paler and closer to the colour of the
air than the one in front, until the farthest chain, with one broad rounded
summit, lies against the afterglow. The sky takes up about three-fifths of the
height. It runs from a dusky smalt-cobalt blue at the top, through a pale greenish
band and lemon, to a warm apricot glow where the sun went down, left of centre.
Two thin level streaks of violet-grey cloud lie low in that glow. A thin crescent
moon hangs in the cooling blue upper right, lit on the side toward the set sun.

- **Foreground as a barrier.** The dark foreground is Friedrich's device: a
  near ground we stand on, cut off from the far distance with no road between. The
  eye has to cross the valley through the air, not along a path.
- **Two verticals and one small figure.** On the knoll at the left stand two tall
  spruces and two young ones. The taller breaks the horizon and rises into the
  glowing sky: the strongest vertical, and the darkest shape against the lightest
  passage. On a slight rise at the right, between two low rocks, a man seen from
  behind (a Rückenfigur) stands with a staff and looks out over the mist toward the
  glow. He is small, so the space stays large. He stands where the mist is palest,
  so a small dark shape carries the most contrast in the picture.
- **Balance.** The firs (left) and the figure (right) balance across the empty
  centre. The far summit sits between them, a little right of centre, and the moon
  hangs almost straight above the figure. That vertical link from man to moon
  replaces any gesture.
- **Values.** The values run in five bands, near to far: nearly black
  foreground, dark near ridge, then mid, pale and palest ranges, with the lightest
  values in the mist and the low sky. Mist separates every range, as layered
  planes do in his Riesengebirge pictures.
- **Colour.** The palette is restrained. The only warm passage is the afterglow
  (with the rust of the heath answering it faintly). The ranges are violet-greys
  mixed from pale smalt, cobalt, white and a little red. The firs are Prussian
  blue with black and ochre, close to black.

## Working method, stage by stage

1. **ground.** `Style::friedrich()`'s grounds, but with a lighter top layer. Two
   knifed layers (red-brown 110 µm, light brown 70 µm) level the weave. The top is
   a brushed, patchy whitish lead-white layer (#d9ccb2, 55 µm), because his later
   grounds were light on top [KÖR p.284] and a luminous twilight sky in thin paint
   needs a light ground under it.
2. **drawing.** A 2H hand line along each crest; HB for the firs' axes and
   outer triangles and for the figure. The drawing shimmered through the thin sky
   paint until the firs covered it. The figure's pencil line is now hidden.
3. **underpainting.** A very thin warm-brown glaze (medium 0.75) over the
   foreground only, laid in broad strokes along the fall of the ground, then dried.
4. **sky.** The sky runs down under every range to the foreground crest. It is
   laid with the broad filbert in lean paint (medium 0.3, coverage 4), in long,
   nearly level strokes swept from the top down, each stroke aimed at the
   gradient. Two badger passes follow (level, then crossing ±0.35). Then, while the
   layer is still wet, a fine stippler goes over the whole sky (coverage 1.4, no
   clustering, a new dip every 8 touches) aimed at the same gradient. The touches
   fuse into the wet paint and leave a fine, live grain.
5. **sky stipple.** Drying only (the stipple moved into stage 4; see below).
6. **far chain, third range, second range, near ridge.** Each range goes in the
   same way. The body filbert follows the crest's slope, clipped to a band under
   the crest line. Its colour field runs from the range's crest colour down into
   the mist colour. Mist is then stippled into the range's foot *while it is wet*,
   with the aim going toward the mist colour, increasingly with depth. A
   horizontally stretched fBm modulates the mist into wisps. A badger along the
   slope fuses it, the range dries, and a faint semi-opaque veil goes over the foot.
   Crest colours: #a09aae, #8a889f, #525369, #474d5c. The second range crest dives
   into the mist on the left and the near ridge on the right, so both ranges end
   as spurs sinking into the fog.
7. **clouds.** Three tapered streak masks. The soft glaze filbert (medium 0.7)
   lays a greyish violet, warmer near the glow. A light unclipped badger pass fuses
   them into the sky.
8. **afterglow.** Once the sky is dry, a transparent glaze of vermilion and
   chrome yellow (#e9a265, up to 0.4 coat) goes over the lowest sky, strongest
   above where the sun set. Thin transparent violet glazes then set back the far
   chain (0.3) and the third range (0.45) along their crests, so the ranges keep
   their order of darkness against the deepened glow. Neighbouring glazes meet on
   complementary masks.
9. **foreground.** Body filbert along the fall of the ground, clipped at the
   crest. Umber, black and green earth are warmed where the heather grows and
   lighten a little just below the crest, where the ground turns up to the sky. A
   sable then draws the crest edge. Short upright hatched touches, lighter and
   warmer, make the heather, dense under the crest and thinning downhill.
10. **rocks.** Five rough, ridged solids in a `Form` (two angular blocks beside
    the figure, boulders on the knoll, one low in the right foreground). They are
    lit only by the sky from above and behind. The body filbert paints them from
    their lit value (#221e1b to #5d5852). The heath is then brushed back over the
    lower half of each rock, up to a wavering line found by scanning each column
    of the rock mask, so the rocks sink into the ground.
11. **firs.** A narrow hatched spine goes in first. Each tree then gets a trunk
    tapering to a hairline, with the trunk width scaled to the tree. Branches go
    from the top down, alternating sides, each a single stroke of a pointed round:
    pressed at the trunk, sagging, lifting at the tip, lifted off to a point. Branch
    length follows a tiered envelope that varies randomly per tier and per side,
    with occasional gaps. Short twigs hang from the outer two-thirds of each branch.
12. **figure.** Polygon masks for the coat (leaning slightly toward the
    staff), the legs and a bare head. They are painted with the sable in dark
    green-grey and near-black. A rigger draws the staff, and a faint cool
    sky-light touches the left shoulder and skull.
13. **grass.** Fine upturning strokes of a pointed sable: along the whole crest
    (not on the rocks or at the figure's feet), along the rocks' sink lines, and in
    sparse tufts down the slope, darker as the ground falls into shadow. Grass comes
    last, as in his pictures [NG p.56].
14. **moon.** A faint pale halo glaze, then the crescent (a disc minus an offset
    disc, lit toward the sun) touched in with the sable.
15. **last glaze.** A transparent violet-umber glaze deepening toward the edges
    and corners and sparing the moon. This is the advice Friedrich gave Carus
    [MET p.35].
16. **finish.** A thin pale varnish (0.22 ± 0.06 coat, not the yellowed later
    layers), restrained craquelure (narrow, clean cracks with a weaker hierarchy)
    and the style's raking relief.

The whole program renders in about 3 min 40 s. A from-scratch render and the
render assembled from resumed checkpoints are byte-identical.

## Tool limitations met, and workarounds

1. **Poured glazes print the weave.** `Canvas::glaze` levels and pools in the
   hollows of the surface. A deep veil (semi-opaque, ~1.2–1.6 coats) over dark
   range paint printed the warp threads as regular vertical streaks, like rain. So
   did a transparent 0.6-coat deepening glaze.
   *Workaround:* the mist became wet-into-wet stippling, with only a faint glaze
   (≤ 0.3 × amount) over it. Deepening glazes stay at ≤ 0.45 coat. Where a range
   needed to be much darker, I repainted it darker in its own stage rather than
   glazing it.
2. **Stipple over dry paint makes lace.** Touches on a dry layer stay separate,
   and each dip serves ~20 touches with one pile aimed at their centroid. Where the
   first layer was uneven, the sky dried as a patchwork of lighter lacy clumps, and
   mist stippled onto dry ranges looked like snow.
   *Workaround:* stipple into the wet layer (touches lift and fuse), with
   `cluster(0.0, None)` and a dip every 8 touches.
3. **A brushed glaze over a dry pale passage** (`st.glaze(0.85–0.9)`) laid its
   thin, medium-rich film mostly on the weave crowns. It speckled and, after the
   badger, read lighter rather than darker.
   *Workaround:* abandoned; the range was repainted darker (item 1).
4. **Clipped fills take the mask's outline exactly.** A hatch fill clipped to a
   tiered fir mask made firs of stacked flat slabs. Branch strokes alone over a
   wide core gave a solid cone with spidery arms.
   *Workaround:* the branch strokes make the silhouette. Each is one pressed,
   tapering stroke of a pointed round (`point` 0.6, 2.4–4.6 units wide) with
   hanging twig strokes; the clipped core is only a thin spine.
5. **Unclipped body strokes overrun a crest.** One stroke made a sliver standing
   up out of the knoll.
   *Workaround:* `clip(true)` on the foreground plus a sable pass along the edge.
6. **Two soft masks meeting leave a seam.** Separately softened masks for
   adjacent glazes left a light gap line, or, if overlapped, a dark rim along the
   crests.
   *Workaround:* complementary masks (`m` and `1 − m` from the same smoothstep).
7. **Form-lit rocks look pasted on.** Lit ellipsoids and blocks read as pillows
   or a plinth sitting on the heath. The API has no "embed in ground" operation.
   *Workaround:* angular, ridged roughness; a darker value range; each rock's
   vertical extent per column found by scanning its mask (`f.per_column`); heath
   brushed back over its lower half, plus grass along the line.
8. **No figure tool.** Figures come from `Shape` polygons. A head with a flat
   cap read as a chimney or kepi at 140 px tall.
   *Workaround:* a bare head, rounded shoulders and a slight lean.
9. **Thin lit edges with the detail handling.** A 1-unit band for the clouds'
   lit undersides, filled with short `detail()` strokes, came out as dashed white
   lines, because the planner lays separate short strokes with gaps along a thin
   band.
   *Workaround:* dropped; the clouds are one fused glaze colour.
10. **`Cracks::aged` defaults** made a dominant dark network on this small,
    thin-ground canvas.
    *Workaround:* `width_um` 4, `dirt` 0.12, `veil` 0.15, `hierarchy` 0.5,
    `patchy` 0.6, shallower grooves.
11. **Checkpoint staleness counts everything above a stage.** Removing an unused
    import at the top of the file staled every checkpoint, and so does any edit to
    the crest functions at the top.
    *Workaround:* `--stale-ok --ckpt` once for the harmless import change. I put
    stage-specific helpers (as nested `fn`s) and masks between the stages that use
    them, so edits there only stale later stages.
12. **Blender at a spur's tip.** Brushing along the slope at the steep end of a
    ridge band swirled wet paint and left a light fleck.
    *Workaround:* the ridge was made to descend gradually into the mist, so the
    tip is a long taper, not a cut.

## Critique

What works:
- The air: the ranges recede by value and hue, and the mist lies between them
  softly, with no visible technique at normal distance.
- The afterglow glaze gives the low sky depth. Against it, the far chain's violet
  reads as distance rather than colour.
- The firs are recognizably spruces, with sagging, lifting branches and ragged,
  asymmetric tiers, and they hold the left side.
- The figure is small, dark and in the right place against the palest mist, and
  the moon above him ties the right half together.
- The surface, a light stippled grain in the sky, pooling in the ground and faint
  craquelure, reads as thin oil on a fine ground.

What is weaker:
- The upper sky is a little blotchy. Faint cirrus-like movements, left over from
  the broad lay-in, survive the stipple. They read as high cloud, but Friedrich's
  upper skies are usually more even.
- The two cloud streaks are schematic lenses with tapered ends. They add
  interest to the glow but are less observed than the rest.
- The foreground is dark and fairly uniform below the lit band. It is truthful to
  the hour and to his barrier foregrounds, but it lacks the minute botanical
  precision of his foregrounds; the rocks are still somewhat generic lumps.
- The figure is a simple silhouette, a coat, legs and a head, without the
  costume detail Friedrich gave his figures.
- The palette stays within his pigments. The afterglow's orange sits near the
  edge of what his chrome yellow and vermilion would give as a glaze, and it is a
  touch sweeter than his more austere evenings.
