# Lawrence Alma-Tadema: materials and method

*A sourced technical report. Every claim cites a source key; the sources
are listed at the end. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified,
disputed or taken from a single object.*

## Summary

Alma-Tadema painted in oil on wooden panels and on canvas in roughly equal
numbers. Most of the panels are small, and the largest works are on canvas
[RA13]. He bought ready-prepared supports from London colormen and at
least once from a Brussels firm [NPG-W; NPG-M; NPG-R; NPG-W11]. The one
condition report that mentions his ground says it is white [SOTH11]. He
drew the composition slightly, in pencil, straight onto the support; then
he laid it in with rough brushstrokes and built the figures up in thin
layers against a light background. In his one well-described unfinished
canvas, one figure is finished while the others are still laid in [HEIJ
p.169; VGM-label; STRAND p.613]. From 1865 he painted with an oil
copal varnish ("copal à l'huile") mixed into the paint, having used boiled
oil before that [HEIJ p.171 and nn.25–26]. A pupil published his palette
around 1886: brown ochre, yellow ochre, Naples yellow, flake white, orange
vermilion, light red, Chinese vermilion, rose madder, burnt sienna, cobalt
and ivory black [MANUAL pp.12–14]. He reworked constantly, painting out
and scraping down finished passages, and this repainting is linked to
cracking in his paint [ZIM02; STAND p.100; STRAND p.613; HEIJ p.171]. A
2004 survey found no technical studies of his work [TOWN]. The only
published instrumental examination found is of a single early panel
[NGC]. No cross-sections, medium analyses or pigment identifications have
been published.

**Terms.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layers between the support and
the paint. *Lay-in*: the first broad placing of shapes and colors.
*Painting out*: covering a finished passage with new paint. *Scraping
down*: removing paint with a knife or blade before repainting. *IRR
(infrared reflectography)*: imaging that shows carbon-based drawing under
paint. *Craquelure*: the network of cracks in paint or varnish. *Opus
number*: a serial number the artist gave each work.

## 1. Supports

- **Panel and canvas.** The 1913 memorial exhibition catalogue lists the
  support for about 140 works: about 73 panels and 70 canvases
  [RA13]. The wood species is rarely stated (see below).
- **Sizes.** Panels are mostly small; several measure about 30 × 23 cm
  [RA13; TATE-N01523]. A few are large: one of 1869 measures
  28½ × 48 in. (72 × 122 cm), one of 1874 measures 26½ × 87½ in. (67 ×
  222 cm) and one of 1882 measures 86 × 26 in. (218 × 66 cm) [RA13]. The
  largest works are on canvas, for example 50 × 84 in. (127 × 213 cm),
  painted in 1888 [RA13].
- **Bought prepared.** In answers to the Royal Academy's "Technical forms of
  procedure" questionnaires he recorded Winsor & Newton panels and canvas in
  1873, 1877 and 1881, and a panel from the Brussels firm Mommen in 1877–78
  [NPG-W; NPG-M, both citing Don and Woodcock 2020]. A work of 1878 and a
  small panel of 1889 bear Winsor & Newton marks; the 1889 panel carries a
  "PREPARED PANEL" label [NPG-W; NPG-W11]. A panel of 1909 and a work of
  1902 carry Roberson labels [NPG-R8; NPG-R]. He had an account with
  Roberson [HKI; NPG-R].
- **Wood species.** A panel of 1868–69 (72.5 × 109 cm) is catalogued as
  "oil on mahogany panel" [BMAG]. The one panel examined instrumentally is
  radially cut oak. It is from his student years (1860), and he reused it:
  IRR shows an older genre scene underneath [NGC]. How many of his panels
  are mahogany is *unverified*.
- **Panel construction.** A panel of 1867 has a join; a crack follows the
  join along its upper edge [SOTH07]. *Single object.*
- **Canvas.** Weave, thread count and fiber are not documented in the
  sources found. *Gap.* One of his canvases in a London museum is still
  unlined [GETTY-CC].

## 2. Grounds

- **White.** A conservator's condition report on a panel of 1883 describes
  "the white ground revealed by the craquelure" and retouching laid in the
  cracks to hide it [SOTH11]. *Single object.* Nothing is published on
  the composition or number of layers of his grounds. *Gap.*
- **Commercial preparation.** Because he recorded ready-made colorman
  panels and canvases (§1), his grounds were usually commercial primings,
  not his own [NPG-W; NPG-M]. *Inferred.* The Royal Academy forms asked
  whether the ground was absorbent or not and white or tinted [RA-arch].
  His answers were not found in accessible sources. *Gap.*
- **Light under the image.** An article in a museum journal says he
  "built up his figures against a light background" and calls this "his
  preferred manner" [HEIJ p.169]. A reviewer in 2017 describes his "habit
  of painting darker colours on to a pale background (as opposed to the
  conventional inverse)" [SI]. Both describe the visible build-up, not an
  analyzed ground layer.

## 3. Underdrawing and lay-in

- **On the support, not on paper.** In 1895 he wrote: "I generally make a
  slight sketch of the picture I am going to Paint directly on the
  respective canvas or panel" [VW, quoting a statement of 1895; original
  publication not identified, *Uncertain*]. A biography of 1902 says he did
  little preparatory sketching: he "may be said not to sketch at all"
  [ZIM02].
- **Pencil.** In 1899 an interviewer saw a half-painted small canvas where
  "the paint and pencilled lines" showed the subject. He also saw "a large
  canvas filled with pencilled outlines" for the next year's exhibition
  [STRAND p.613].
- **One early panel.** IRR of the 1860 panel revealed underdrawing
  (carbon-based) as well as the older scene beneath [NGC].
- **Brush lay-in.** In an unfinished canvas of about 1873–75 "he set out the
  composition with rough brushstrokes, and then began working out the
  bodies of the first two figures in the foreground"; the objects are "still
  only a suggestion of their ultimate form" [HEIJ p.169]. The owning
  museum's label describes three stages visible side by side: "the rapidly
  sketched underpainting, through the thin layers of paint in which
  elements such as the flesh colour have been built up, to the careful
  finish" of one figure [VGM-label].
- **Figures from life.** In the same 1895 statement: "I paint my figures
  direct from life in every instance," and insisted the model be "dressed
  and coiffured as completely as though [she] were sitting for the
  finishing touches" [VW]. Architecture and objects were checked
  against his collection of photographs, which he arranged by subject
  [STRAND p.613; HEIJ p.171].

accessible source. The palette below is documentary.

- **Source and date of the palette.** The pupil's manual (2nd edition,
  1887) says: "my palette has the great recommendation of being the one used
  by Mr. Alma Tadema, from whom I have taken it" [MANUAL p.13]. The colors
  are listed "in the order in which I set them on the palette" [MANUAL
  p.14]. Whether he kept this order is not stated.
- **Suppliers.** In 1885 a contemporary wrote that he himself used "one or
  two colours (Tadema I think _all_) from Mommen's in Brussels" [BARR
  vol.2 p.292]. *Uncertain* (hearsay). Blockx (a Belgian maker) sold him
  colors in 1894–1904 [NPG-B]. *Madderton's Notes for Artists* printed his palette, colors and
  medium in a series of 1902–04 [NPG-M]; that text was not found. *Gap.*
- **Greens and blues.** No other green or blue is documented. Whether he
  mixed greens from cobalt and yellow or used viridian is *unverified*.

## 5. Binding media and varnish

- **Boiled oil, then oil copal.** In a letter of 1902 he wrote that a
  painting of 1864 was the last in which he used "boiled oil." A painting
  of 1862 made with "the same colours and oils" had been "full of cracks"
  [HEIJ p.171 and n.25]. On a visit to Paris in 1865 he asked a French
  painter what preparations he used and learned "to paint with Copal à
  l'huile: the varnish protects the colours against the influence of the
  atmosphere but when mixed with oil does not prevent the oil from
  evaporating. Since then I have always used this Copal à l'huile de
  Durosin" [HEIJ p.171 and n.26]. "Durosin" is unexplained, possibly a
  maker's name [HEIJ n.26].
- **What copal à l'huile is.** A resin-based (copal) varnish made with oil
  [HEIJ n.26]. His pupil's manual recommends a medium of copal, linseed oil
  and turpentine in about equal parts, reducing the oil when quick drying is
  wanted [MANUAL pp.14–15]. The manual does not say this is his medium.
- **Final varnish.** A panel of 1883 has "a thin clear varnish"; whether it
  is original is not stated [SOTH11].
- **Analysis.** None published. *Gap.*

## 6. Layers and application

- **Thin build-up.** Flesh is built up in "thin layers of paint" [VGM-label].
  On the 1860 panel the face is "applied thinly by brush in a wet-in-wet
  technique that suggests direct painting from life" [NGC]. The X-ray there
  shows the flesh and collar paint as thin but lead-white rich; the edges
  are thin or less radio-opaque [NGC].
- **Order on the 1860 panel.** The bluish-green background went on before
  the face, and brown accents were added afterward "in a loose, animated
  manner" [NGC]. *Single early object.*
- **Figures against light.** He "built up his figures against a light
  background" [HEIJ p.169].
- **One passage at a time.** In the unfinished canvas one figure is carried
  to "careful finish" while the others remain lay-in [VGM-label; HEIJ
  p.169].
- **Thick paint.** A panel of 1867 has "thick impasto in the upper half of
  the work and in the foreground," with some craquelure [SOTH07]. *Single
  object.* Film thicknesses are not published. *Gap.*
- **Fine brushwork.** The Rijksmuseum describes texture rendered "by using
  fine brushstrokes" [RIJKS]. In 1883 a friend described watching him
  "handle his brush and place his rare strokes, none of which are idly
  bestowed" [ZIM83]. Another friend notes that painted flowers were not
  made with "a wearisome number of strokes of the brush" [EBERS pp.56–57].
- **Stone and marble.** No technical source describes the layer structure
  of his painted marble. *Gap.* He told an interviewer in 1899 that he could
  paint "very blue skies and very light marbles better and more easily than
  anything else" [STRAND p.604]. This is not a description of layers.
- **Brushes.** He washed his own brushes [STAND p.120]. Brush types are not
  documented for him. *Gap.*

## 7. Reworking and working practice

- **Painting out and scraping down.** In 1899: "For days I make no progress
  with a picture—there is so much painting-out" [STRAND p.604]. "Some detail
  in a picture may be painted and repainted before it satisfies him"
  [STRAND p.613]. A close friend wrote that "again and again did he scrape
  down his work, erasing recklessly the most exquisite little figures," so
  that one large canvas "represents the work of two or three pictures," and
  "under each picture is hidden some exquisite detail, painted over" [ZIM02].
  In 1883 the same writer: "Calmly, remorselessly will he paint over figures
  and details" [ZIM83].
  His later biographer describes "innumerable alterations, erasures,
  seemingly reckless scrapings down of canvas" [STAND p.100]. Figures were
  probably added to one large canvas after it was first exhibited in 1894
  [GETTY-SP pp.32–33].
- **Scraping to reuse.** On the reused 1860 panel he scraped down the older
  image selectively, around the mouth and chin, "presumably to prevent the
  paint texture of the genre scene from interfering" [NGC].
- **Time per picture.** One to three years for important pictures; a small
  half-painted canvas had been "in hand" for nearly two years [STRAND
  p.613].
- **Several at once.** "I always have two or three pictures in hand at the
  same time… I must turn from one picture to another for quickness and
  freshness of interest" [STRAND p.613]. He left a picture upside down on
  the easel after a day's work on it [STRAND p.613].
- **Photographs of the work in progress.** From 1866 a photographer
  photographed his paintings in progress. He used the prints "to check the
  tonal balances," adjusted the painting and had it photographed again
  [UMMA, citing Pohlmann]. *Uncertain* (seen in a secondary summary only).
- **Opus numbers.** He "numbers each work" like a composer, so that "there
  can never arise… any doubt as to their chronological order"; the latest
  in 1886 was Opus 275 [ZIM86 p.27]. Signatures read, for example, "L.
  Alma-Tadema, op. cxcviii," with the number in Roman numerals, as
  transcribed in the 1913 catalogue [RA13]. By 1899 he had painted "more than three hundred important
  pictures" [STRAND p.604].
- **Studio light and color.** "The light and colour in a studio had a great
  influence upon me in my work." He painted successively in a studio with
  black decoration, one of bright red (Brussels), one of light green
  (London) and finally one of "silvery white" [STRAND p.612].
- **Working hours.** He worked every hour of daylight when at work on a
  picture [STAND p.120].

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Cracking.** "Because his canvases were regularly painted over and worked
  up, they often exhibit a great deal of craquelure" [HEIJ p.171]. He
  blamed the cracks in a work of 1862 on boiled oil and on its display
  conditions [HEIJ n.25]. On a panel of 1883 "deeper vertical craquelure"
  opens down to the white ground [SOTH11]. On a panel of 1867 craquelure is
  in the thick impasto [SOTH07].
- **Panels.** The 1883 panel is "very stable with no warping" [SOTH11]; the
  1867 panel "has not bowed" [SOTH07].
- **Canvas.** An unlined canvas developed tears "along the fold line" after
  repeated loans [GETTY-CC].
