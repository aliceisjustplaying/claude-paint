# Lessons from batch A at the easel

The strongest repeated pattern is a repair that creates another repair: local patches leave seams, blending carries wet neighbors into them and glazes change more than their intended target. The clearest escapes were repainting a coherent passage, protecting neighbors and waiting before restating contrasting marks (paint-studio-3be9e8:3–25; paint-studio-c6b5dd:10–21; paint-studio-cfa19c:12–18). Some apparent material failures were design failures: converging banks made mud read flatter, shared crown lighting reduced the broccoli effect and an eye-height check forced a complete scale correction (paint-studio-75c237:5–8; paint-studio-cfa19c:8,16; paint-studio-358aea:14–15).

All 18 journals in [batch A](batch-A.txt) were read completely. They cover r16–r17, engine 1, the default tube box. These are journal observations, not independent visual verification or current-engine experiments. Each Lesson is my practical interpretation of its cited evidence; additional interpretations are labeled below. Exact old recipes, drying intervals and API assumptions need fresh trials; agreement with today's guide supports only the stated mechanism. The shortest still-life journal stops while work is pending, so its planned refinements are not counted as successful repairs (paint-studio-6399ad:5).

## Ground and drawing

### G1. Treat the ground as a starting condition, not a proven recipe

- **Lesson:** Choose and record the ground before the lay-in, then judge its effect through the actual paint placed over it.
- **Kind:** process.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; warm double grounds on linen, commonly a knifed lead-white base and a brushed ochre/umber or white top; no controlled ground comparison.
- **Evidence:** One journal calls an 80 µm white base plus a 30 µm ochre/umber top “luminous warmth”; another records linen 16×14 with a leveled white/umber base and warm brush ground (paint-studio-b5323f:1; paint-studio-6399ad:1). Those are choices and impressions, not proof that two grounds outperform one; neither later journal isolates the ground's contribution.
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: G:53–71 specifies preparation and ground behavior; T:21–30 covers ground choice.
- **Still valid?** The guide retains layered grounds and their visible influence. Engine-1 journals do not support an absorbency recommendation or a universal thickness recipe.

### G2. Give an object enough supporting plane before its details

- **Lesson:** Establish the surface beneath an object far enough forward to carry its base before modeling its small features.
- **Kind:** craft.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; still-life pitcher and fruit on a stone ledge; chalk layout followed by broad environmental masses.
- **Evidence:** Broad strokes crossed contours, and the shelf needed extension to about y620 “to give the pitcher and quinces a solid supporting plane”; the next entry reports the extended horizontal surface and vertical drop (paint-studio-6399ad:3–4). Close-up refinements remain planned at line 5, so this confirms the revised layout, not a finished still life.
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: T:53–56 addresses physical meetings between forms; B:37 asks for spatial relationships after the masses.
- **Still valid?** A design lesson rather than a specific engine rule; success here is the painter's reported assessment only.

### G3. Resolve seams in a continuous form by redrawing the form

- **Lesson:** If a trunk or body is broken by a construction seam, restate its continuous shape instead of disguising the join with texture.
- **Kind:** process.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; trunks assembled from separate masks or root passages; wet blends and later opaque restatement.
- **Evidence:** Blending a trunk/root join lightened it and left a step; restating the lower trunk in the same upper-trunk recipe finally made the column continuous (paint-studio-cd267e:10–11). A separate oak lost its fork seam after a new trunk mask ran “in one piece from the fork to flared roots” (paint-studio-cfa19c:8,11).
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: T:91–94 calls for one flowing mass; B:37 asks for a method change after a recurring repair.
- **Still valid?** The coherent-shape advice is transferable; exact old blend behavior is supported only at the mechanism level by S:38–42.

## Color mixing

### C1. Test white-bearing mixtures where their value must work

- **Lesson:** Inspect a white-bearing mix on its intended underlayer before repeating it across a sky, shadow or repair.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; lead-white mixtures with smalt, cobalt, earths or greens on pale grounds and previously painted passages; quantities often missing.
- **Evidence:** Bottom-edge swatches showed smalt 4:white 1 was still only “a mid slate”; the sky had been too pale (paint-studio-9aae10:2). White-bearing water repeatedly came out nearly white, and the painter later recommended planning darker masses first (paint-studio-96b5e4:5,8). This does not mean all opaque shadows are impossible: seven-pile opaque lemon modeling succeeded without blending (paint-studio-3be9e8:10,13).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: T:129–130 warns about white; S:8 explains hiding and lightening; B:39 requires a laid trial.
- **Still valid?** Current notes support white's hiding effect. Ratios and the old “lead white wins” diagnosis remain engine-1 observations, not calibrated current recipes.

### C2. Match the present painted surface, not its remembered recipe

- **Lesson:** For a color repair, trial the mix over the actual repair substrate and compare it with the surrounding passage.
- **Kind:** process.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; smooth skies and misty plains after layering or glazing; fresh paint over different light and dark underlayers.
- **Evidence:** A plain had “sunk under glazes,” so old-looking white-rich mixes failed; swatches found a closer match, but repainting them wet spoiled a pass (paint-studio-9aae10:11). A sky match initially seemed successful, then later patches still differed because underlayers differed; the painter shifted toward whole-passage repainting (paint-studio-cfa19c:12–14).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: G:97–101 describes substrate-dependent appearance; B:39 specifies intended substrate and treatment. The remembered-recipe trap is only partly explicit.
- **Still valid?** Current guide supports substrate dependence; the journals do not isolate aging from thickness, glazing or wet pickup as the cause.

### C3. Change pigment strength when repeated weak veils miss the target

- **Lesson:** If repeated glazes barely move a passage's value, trial a stronger mixture instead of accumulating the same ineffective veil.
- **Kind:** process.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; pale snowy foreground, smalt glazes followed by mixtures including Prussian blue and black; exact later ratios absent.
- **Evidence:** Two smalt glazes left the land pale; the painter called smalt “too weak a stainer,” then a third with Prussian blue and black finally lowered the foreground (paint-studio-7ff345:11–12). A deeper later mixture still proved necessary for the sky glow to stand clear (paint-studio-7ff345:16). This supports trialing a different mix, not a fixed three-coat prescription.
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: G:150,159 gives relative tint strengths; T:108–109 says to steer by eye; B:37 asks for a method rethink.
- **Still valid?** Today's table retains weak smalt and strong Prussian blue. Actual glazing strength still depends on current film and substrate conditions.

## Thin vs thick paint

### P1. Make a foreground form opaque enough to conceal what is behind it

- **Lesson:** If a horizon or river shows through a foreground tree, establish sufficient body coverage before adding foliage detail.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; thin pollard trunks and stippled crowns over horizon and river bands; no load or coverage recipe recorded.
- **Evidence:** The painter found the crowns and trunks “thin enough that the horizon line and the river show through”; later re-stippling covered the horizon and made the trees hold as a dark mass (paint-studio-358aea:17–19). A subsequent sitting still found a background band through crown level and rebuilt the structure again (paint-studio-358aea:22), so the first coverage repair was incomplete.
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: S:2 and S:20 distinguish thin coverage from hiding and gap filling; G:331 documents `fill`; B:35 requires inspection.
- **Still valid?** Current sources retain thin-film visibility and coverage gaps. This is not evidence that one extra coat always suffices.

### P2. Do not equate low brush load with a transparent pale film

- **Lesson:** Trial the laid effect of a pale low-load stroke before calling it a scumble or spreading it over a dark passage.
- **Kind:** failure-pattern.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; lead-white-heavy pile at load 0.25 for sheen on mud, with underlayer wetness not specified in that entry; scumble filbert on thin cloud ellipses was also reported too loaded.
- **Evidence:** Low-load mud sheen became “opaque cream blobs,” and blending spread the cream over the bank (paint-studio-1def1a:8). White cloud streaks became “white bricks,” although clipped crossing blends rescued those clouds (paint-studio-497bcf:2). Same apparent failure, different acceptable result.
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: S:8 explains pale veiling; S:12–13 ties dry-brush breakup to several conditions; T:175–179 covers scumbling; B:39 requires trials.
- **Still valid?** Today's notes support load being only one condition. The 0.25 failure is an old specific observation, not a current threshold.

### P3. Use material accents to support a modeled mass

- **Lesson:** Place crisp highlights and texture after the object's volume and attachment are legible.
- **Kind:** craft.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; glazed jug shoulder, citrus rind and stone ledge; opaque body modeling followed by bright impasto and contact shadows.
- **Evidence:** Close inspection identified handle negative space, lower-fruit shadow and specular accents as distinct needs; final opaque fruit light, handle articulation, impasto and contact shadows were followed by a favorable mirror/value assessment (paint-studio-b5323f:6–7). Another jug's strap and volume were reported clear before final sprig and specular work (paint-studio-7b31aa:8–10). The journals do not isolate impasto's contribution from the other changes.
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: T:138–155 reserves thickness for accents; B:37 puts large relationships before repeated detail.
- **Still valid?** The sequence remains useful as craft advice. Current impasto controls are described in G:261–266; these journals provide no measured thickness recipe.

## Blending and edges

### E1. Blend a coherent field when a local blend creates a seam

- **Lesson:** When blending a local smooth patch leaves its boundary visible, reconsider the whole connected passage while protecting neighboring forms.
- **Kind:** process.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; wet wall, sky, meadow or water paint inside rectangular or limited blend masks; some smooth underlayers dry.
- **Evidence:** A local wall shadow blend left an outline, while a whole-wall blend made it smoky (paint-studio-3be9e8:7). A rectangular reflection repair became a gray block; later blending inside the reflection's own shape worked (paint-studio-75c237:3,13). Thus “blend whole fields” is conditional advice, not a command to blend the whole picture.
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: S:39 explicitly records local seams; B:37 asks for a different method after recurring repairs. The alternatives of coherent field versus shape-following region are not developed there.
- **Still valid?** S:39 supports the seam mechanism. Exact old mask behavior needs retesting; current blend clipping differs from old assumptions (G:340–347).

### E2. Keep the blender's scale below the form's tonal structure

- **Lesson:** On a small modeled object, trial narrow transition work or overlapping graded strokes before blending its whole body.
- **Kind:** failure-pattern.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; 40-unit badger over jug and lemons; later 20-unit vertical blend; seven graded lemon piles, strokes recorded as 0.075 apart without specifying the units.
- **Evidence:** Whole-object blending flattened jug and lemons to mid value; a smaller vertical blend still pulled the jug pale (paint-studio-3be9e8:3,5). Curved lemon strokes turned the form, diagonal blending ruined it and repainting with seven piles and no blend restored it (paint-studio-3be9e8:11,13). A whole-sail blend similarly spread orange and damaged rigging (paint-studio-1def1a:6).
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: S:39–40 explains wet mixing; T:68–69 covers form-following marks. Blender size versus small-form modeling is not explicit.
- **Still valid?** Current blender width and lifting are documented (G:314), but “never blend small forms” is too broad: small clipped blends helped distant house fronts (paint-studio-497bcf:29).

### E3. Treat blending as paint removal as well as smoothing

- **Lesson:** If a dark passage lightens or exposes a lattice during blending, stop adding blend passes and restate it after the underlayer sets.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; clean badger through thin wet dark paint over lighter layers; repeated passes on trunks, water and mud.
- **Evidence:** “each blend over the trunk lightened it”; matching dark body later restored continuity (paint-studio-cd267e:10–11). A dark creek-edge blend left exposed lattice (paint-studio-96b5e4:5). This is not evidence that dry water itself was lifted: the new wet marks over it were the workable layer.
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: G:314 explicitly quantifies approximate film lifting; S:38–42 records lifting, lattice and thin-spot spreading.
- **Still valid?** The mechanism is explicitly current. Journal outcomes and the guide's approximate percentages are not measurements of these historical passes.

### E4. Protect wet neighbors during blending and late background work

- **Lesson:** Keep fresh darks and finished narrow accents outside a blend's effective region, or let them set before nearby smoothing.
- **Kind:** failure-pattern.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; water beside wet boat hull, sky near tree crowns and clouds near towers; explicitly unclipped or broad badger passes.
- **Evidence:** Two water/mud blends smeared a boat bow; the painter then stopped blending near the boat (paint-studio-4fb403:7–9). A sky repair cut an oak into balls and nearly erased a cloud, then clipped repainting and blends enabled recovery (paint-studio-cfa19c:14). Warm cloud rims also dragged white across tacky towers (paint-studio-cfa19c:5).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: T:183–185; S:25–31 and S:39; G:340–347.
- **Still valid?** Current blending is clipped by default, so old “always unclipped” beliefs are obsolete. A clipped blend can still mix wet colors inside its mask; clipping does not protect included neighbors (S:39).

## Glazing/scumbling

### V1. Select the glaze by its laid film, not its name or medium alone

- **Lesson:** Trial a glaze's pigment mix, medium and handling on the intended dry passage before committing it broadly.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; smalt land glazes on dry snow, then reed glazes; medium 0.75, 0.92 and 0.85 in the snow trials; reed ratios absent.
- **Evidence:** A 0.75-medium snow test was an opaque lavender block; 0.92 with low load was starved streaks; pale smalt with a trace of umber and white, medium 0.85 and load 0.5, then blending gave a useful veil, later successful in two passes (paint-studio-9cf69b:9–11). Umber reed glaze hid too much, but replacing it with lower-hiding pigments still produced bright blue detail strokes (paint-studio-7ff345:14–15).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: G:97–101; T:170–179; B:39. The paired failures particularly qualify pigment-only advice.
- **Still valid?** Current guide supports variable transparency, fluidity and substrate effects. None of these old settings is a safe universal recipe.

### V2. Keep white-bearing veils distinct from darkening glazes

- **Lesson:** If the aim is to lower value without veiling a passage, first test a mixture without lead white.
- **Kind:** failure-pattern.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; white-bearing glazes over pale jug shadows and red cattle; also a successful pale-smalt snow veil with a trace of white.
- **Evidence:** Jug glazes containing white made opaque blotches; no-white glazes were eventually useful, although initially too dark (paint-studio-3be9e8:6,8–9,18–19). A white-bearing cattle glaze made “pinkish ghosts with red rims”; solid deeper repainting followed (paint-studio-497bcf:31–32). The successful snow veil at paint-studio-9cf69b:10–11 prevents turning this into “no white in any glaze.”
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: S:8 explicitly distinguishes lightening veil from tinting; T:129–130 and T:170–176 cover white and glaze/scumble roles.
- **Still valid?** Current notes support the distinction. The old final claim that every pale-object shadow must be glazed is overgeneralized by its own successful opaque lemons (paint-studio-3be9e8:13,25).

### V3. Constrain glaze strokes explicitly near finished shapes

- **Lesson:** Clip a glaze near shapes it must preserve rather than expecting its planning mask to contain every bristle.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; long horizontal glaze strokes around stones, paths, tree trunks and finished figures; glaze hand described as a 26-unit brush in one journal.
- **Evidence:** A stone glaze filled openings and dirtied the plain (paint-studio-9aae10:11). Mist whitened a trunk until the painter used `clip=true` for the remaining width (paint-studio-cd267e:9). A path glaze sent brown smears into hay fields; repainting those whole passages removed them (paint-studio-497bcf:16–17).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: G:340–347 states glaze overrun may reach a stroke's length; S:43; T:183–185.
- **Still valid?** Explicitly supported today. The old phrase “hand=glaze is never clipped” means its default, not inability to request a clip; the journal's successful `clip=true` and current guide both qualify it.

### V4. Use a glaze only when its direction serves the form

- **Lesson:** For a narrow backlit edge or wet sheen, trial a few constrained strokes instead of a whole-shape veil and blend.
- **Kind:** craft.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; sail leech, mast and sprit; later light clipped to a 2-unit band and left unblended; mud sheen on a dark bank.
- **Evidence:** Whole-sail blending spread hot orange and dragged the mast; a later narrow broken light band read as backlight through cloth, with an unwanted foot patch covered separately (paint-studio-1def1a:6,11). On mud, low-load white blobs and broad blending destroyed darkness, prompting a plan for thinner lower-white strokes (paint-studio-1def1a:8); that plan is not separately confirmed as a sheen success.
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: T:68–75 covers directional marks and quiet light; B:39 calls for trials. The narrow-band alternative is not specified.
- **Still valid?** This is composition and handling advice supported by one reported sail improvement; exact old band width is not a current prescription.

## Drying and timing

### D1. Wait for separation when clean contrasting marks are needed

- **Lesson:** Let the supporting layer set before placing clean snow, pale cloth or dark detail that must remain separate.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; snow on wet dark stones, cream shawls over red dresses, dark roots over pale snow and foliage stipples into wet dark crowns.
- **Evidence:** Snow on wet stones went gray; clean snow over dry stone later worked (paint-studio-9cf69b:5,7). A shawl repeatedly picked up red and was finally restated clean over dry red (paint-studio-774267:7,11–12). Foliage stipples fused wet but modeled the dry base later (paint-studio-96b5e4:3–4). Some pickup was accepted as warm light rather than repaired (paint-studio-7ace8e:18).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: S:25–32; T:125–128 and T:178–179; G:601–615.
- **Still valid?** Current guide retains pickup in open paint and separation over dry paint. “Wait three or four days” from paint-studio-9aae10:8 is historical, not a universal schedule.

### D2. Do not treat tacky paint as a smooth glazing surface

- **Lesson:** If a thin stroke grabs into streaks on tacky paint, wait for a dry support or rebuild the passage with a fresh body layer.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; path and horse glazes on tacky paint; scratchy cloud cores on tacky cloud paint.
- **Evidence:** A path glaze became bark-like blotches and a horse glaze became streaks when impatiently chained onto tacky paint (paint-studio-7ace8e:10,22). Thin cloud cores produced “stick-and-slip scribbles”; fresh fuller cloud bodies with wet internal modeling and clipped blending then read as a cloud bank (paint-studio-c6b5dd:8–9).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes for the warning: S:31; G:601–603; T:178–179. Partly for the two repair choices.
- **Still valid?** Current sources describe tacky grab and dry glaze support. The fuller-layer rescue is an engine-1 observation, not proof that tacky substrate is harmless beneath any body pass.

### D3. Inspect the earliest strokes before the whole campaign sets

- **Lesson:** For a slow gradation, work and inspect manageable sections while their fresh paint remains blendable.
- **Kind:** process.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; a sky graded by stippling, which took hours; later eight narrow overlapping bands blended section by section while wet.
- **Evidence:** The first sky remained stepped because “the paint set before blending”; the next layer used overlapping bands and blends “as I go while wet” (paint-studio-358aea:2). No later entry explicitly isolates the second sky's improvement, so the revised approach is documented, not independently confirmed as the sole cure.
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: G:590–596 and S:51 say a long pass ages in slices; B:35 requires campaign inspection. Section-wise planning for a slow passage is not explicit.
- **Still valid?** The current clock mechanism supports checking elapsed painting time. Old hours-to-setting cannot be carried forward because today's drying depends on film and pigment (G:605–615).

## Corrections and lifting

### R1. Lift an unwanted wet trial before it becomes another layer

- **Lesson:** When a trial misses its target, lift it while open with a clean brush wiped between strokes and inspect what remains.
- **Kind:** easel.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; clean flat brush, crossing strokes, wiping after every stroke; unwanted glaze over snow or reeds. The reed support is explicitly dry; the snow trial entry does not report a wetness reading.
- **Evidence:** Both failed snow tests came away almost entirely; the useful third test was also lifted so the corner would not receive it twice (paint-studio-9cf69b:9–10). Three passes removed an opaque reed glaze cleanly to the dry reeds; a later blue reed attempt was lifted too (paint-studio-7ff345:14–15). Lifting a different bark trial left a light patch requiring dry repainting (paint-studio-9cf69b:11).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: S:25 explains clean-brush lifting; B:13–14 lists brush/rag corrections; T:192–193.
- **Still valid?** Current guide retains open-paint lifting and set-film protection (G:509–512). Complete removal is not guaranteed; these journals do not test today's rag or knife.

### R2. Replace a failed smooth patch with a coherent passage

- **Lesson:** If successive patches keep showing on a smooth gradient, repaint the connected passage as a new controlled layer instead of matching smaller rectangles.
- **Kind:** process.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; water patched after a stray dark column; sky patched around crowns or old tower masks; dry support, new graded wet layer.
- **Evidence:** A water patch covered the column but left a pale vertical strip; a full open-water layer removed it (paint-studio-c6b5dd:10–12). Old tower masks left cream halos; repainting the cumulus cut only around the current town removed them (paint-studio-cfa19c:18). An earlier whole-sky repaint damaged protected forms when unclipped, so this cure needs boundary control (paint-studio-cfa19c:13–14).
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: S:39 and S:48 explain visible boundaries; B:37 asks for reconsideration after repeat failure.
- **Still valid?** The repair strategy is journal-supported, not guaranteed. Current clipping supports controlling neighbors; exact color matching and pass effects need trials.

### R3. Attach the object to its support, not to a detached shadow patch

- **Lesson:** If an object floats, bring the support up to its base and place the contact shadow against that base.
- **Kind:** craft.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; lemons on a ledge and a boat on mud; contact shadows initially too low, hard or separated from the object.
- **Evidence:** Lemon shadows floated below the fruit, were wiped out, then table paint around the undersides plus a thin hugging dark line made the lemons sit on the ledge (paint-studio-3be9e8:23–24). A boat floated over a gray slab; a darkened ragged mud lip over its lower edge and warmed shadow made it sit in mud (paint-studio-4fb403:11).
- **Already in the prompts?** partly: T:53–56 covers physical contact and edges; G:569–572 offers contact-shadow scaffolds, not this diagnosis.
- **Still valid?** A craft relationship observed in two paintings; the exact wet/tacky correction sequence is engine-specific and need not be prescribed.

## Detail and texture

### X1. Keep distant ground quieter than nearby ground

- **Lesson:** When small far-field marks read as cobbles or bricks, reduce their contrast and scale or replace them with a quieter passage.
- **Kind:** craft.
- **Conditions:** Engine 1; short meadow hatch touches, noise islands and far mud dabs; wet horizontal blends used for rescue.
- **Evidence:** Meadow touches became “cobbles”; level blending changed them into a smooth field whose interest could be carried by particular objects (paint-studio-cd267e:5). Mud dabs also became cobbles until blending made streaky mud (paint-studio-75c237:6). The rescue did not resolve the latter painting's perspective, which was diagnosed separately (paint-studio-75c237:7).
- **Already in the prompts?** yes: T:62–69 covers mark hierarchy, distance and direction; B:37 puts large relationships before repeated detail.
- **Still valid?** A visual design lesson. Today's `scale_at` is documented (G:330); these journals do not establish a current numeric threshold for distant texture.

### X2. Avoid equal, bright scatter across the foreground

### Across the batch: time sinks, escapes and limits

These are qualitative rankings from recurrence and journal narratives, not measured wall-clock costs.

- **Repeated blend/rescue cycles were a major sink.** Jug modeling ran through flattening, strips, pale blends, glazes and another band repair before acceptance on day 48; the clear lemon escape was graded curved paint without a blender (paint-studio-3be9e8:3–24). A water/sky repair sequence ran from an accidental column through a visible patch, whole-water repaint, glow overrun and another whole-sky band (paint-studio-c6b5dd:10–21).
- **Patch matching and boundary damage consumed repeated campaigns.** The dolmen's grain glaze, dirty openings and plain matching extended through day 114; the cloud/town/crown cycle returned again on day 133 to remove halos left by old masks (paint-studio-9aae10:10–12; paint-studio-cfa19c:12–18). The exits were controlled coherent repainting and current shape masks, not simply more patience with the same local patch.
- **Subject structure and scale drove wholesale restarts.** Pollards cycled from broccoli to lollipops to palms, then a scale check required repainting the row; later structural work finally made them read as pollards (paint-studio-358aea:6–15,22). Darkening a boulder repeatedly cost layers until dry thin passes replaced wet scumble/blend repairs (paint-studio-774267:8–11).
- **Long stippling could miss the working window.** The sky painter explicitly said stippling took hours and paint set before blending; the next layer changed to bands blended in sections (paint-studio-358aea:2). Thousands of twigs or blades are recorded without real-time duration, so stroke counts alone cannot establish the largest time sink (paint-studio-9cf69b:3–4; paint-studio-774267:9).
- **Clear reported improvements followed changes in approach.** Converging channel banks improved flatness; one crown light improved separately lit balls; no-blend lemons restored turning; a dry matched snow veil put the glow above the land; coherent current-mask repainting removed tower halos (paint-studio-75c237:7–8; paint-studio-cfa19c:8,18; paint-studio-3be9e8:13; paint-studio-9cf69b:10–11).
- **Trials were on the painting, with their own cleanup costs.** Bottom-edge color swatches and corner glaze tests were painted over or lifted; one wet swatch spoiled a repair pass (paint-studio-9aae10:2,11; paint-studio-9cf69b:9–10). These journals contain no r31 smoke scratch tests. They also contain no explicit painter-to-painter handoff, so they cannot establish a handoff protocol; repeated returns and reflections are the available sitting evidence (paint-studio-1def1a:10–13; paint-studio-cfa19c:15–19; paint-studio-358aea:21–26).
- **Stopping was sometimes acceptance, sometimes an inspection-only return.** Busy foliage, faint ghosts or seams were explicitly left where the whole worked; one painter's final return inspected crops and saved unchanged (paint-studio-cd267e:11; paint-studio-7ace8e:26; paint-studio-497bcf:35–36). The 6399ad journal has no completed verdict, unlike the other short still-life accounts (paint-studio-6399ad:5; paint-studio-7b31aa:10; paint-studio-b5323f:7).
