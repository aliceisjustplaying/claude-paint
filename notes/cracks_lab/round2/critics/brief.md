# Brief: craquelure on one painting, five ways

You are a paintings conservator and painter who has looked closely at many
aged 19th-century oil paintings on canvas (Caspar David Friedrich's among
them). A, B, C, D and E are the same painting (a winter dusk, made with a
simulated oil-paint engine) finished five ways: everything is identical
except the simulated craquelure (age cracks). Files: `X_whole.png` (the
whole, scaled down), `X_sky.png` (a 1:1 crop of thin, pale sky) and
`X_tree.png` (a 1:1 crop of a dark tree against warm sky), for X = A..E.
Look at the crops closely; the cracks are fine lines.

IMPORTANT: look only at the image files in this folder. Do not open, list
or search any other file or folder on this machine: the judgment must be
blind.

The owner looked at an earlier set: she likes a slight dominant direction
(as in *Monk by the Sea*, where the cracks "almost flow, running down a
little to the right"), but a strongly directional version looked digital.
She also sees real craquelure as clustered, "random in a sort of human
random way", and varying a lot in amount from painting to painting.

Write:
1. Rank A-E by how much they look like real aged craquelure, a sentence
   each.
2. What, specifically, makes any of them look digital or fake (pattern,
   spacing, straightness, parallelism, width, contrast, where the cracks
   appear and where they don't)?
3. How real craquelure on a Friedrich-era canvas differs from all five:
   where it shows (lights vs darks, thin vs thick paint), how it clusters,
   how direction shows, what the cracks look like up close. Be concrete,
   and say how sure you are of each claim.
4. What the simulation should change, most important first.
Keep it under 800 words. US English, no Oxford comma.
