# John Singer Sargent: materials and method (oil portraits)

*A sourced technical report. Every claim cites a source key; the sources
are listed at the end. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified,
disputed or taken from a single object.*

## Summary

Sargent painted his oil portraits on fine or medium plain-weave canvas
bought already primed, most often with a cool gray ground of lead white
and black in oil; some grounds are white, cream or pale pink [APOLLO
"Canvases and Priming"; NGA pp.108–125; MMX p.129 n.17]. He drew little
on the canvas: a few charcoal lines, rubbed back with a rag, then a thin
turpentine wash for the background and the big masses [CH p.181;
APOLLO "Limited Working-out"]. After that he worked direct: large, loaded
bristle brushes, stiff tube paint with little or no medium in the lights
and half tones and thin, oily paint in the darks, one passage worked
into the next while wet [CH pp.182, 184; APOLLO "Application of Paint", "The
Painter's Palette"]. When a passage failed he scraped it down or brushed
it together and began again, sitting after sitting [CH pp.184–187; BAS
"Pentimenti"]. His palette was wide and of high quality: lead white,
vermilion, madder lakes, bone or ivory black, iron-oxide yellows, reds
and browns, cadmium and chrome yellows, viridian, emerald green, cobalt
and ultramarine blues, with cerulean, cobalt violet and red lead at times
[APOLLO "The Painter's Palette"; BAS "Materials"; HAM]. No accessible
source gives thread counts or paint-film thicknesses in microns for his
portraits.

**Terms.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layers between canvas and
paint. *Cross-section*: a microscopic sample cut through all layers.
*X-radiograph*: an image made with X-rays, which shows lead-rich paint.
*IRR (infrared reflectography)*: imaging that shows carbon-based drawing
under paint. *Wet-in-wet*: paint laid into paint that is still wet.
*Scumble*: a thin, semi-opaque layer dragged over dry paint. *Glaze*: a
transparent layer. *Impasto*: thick, raised paint. *Megilp*: a gelled
medium of mastic varnish and oil cooked with a lead drier. *Tacking
margin*: the canvas folded over the side of the stretcher.

## 1. Supports

- **Fabric.** "His portrait canvases all have a plain weave… generally
  very fine, though a few are of medium weight" [APOLLO "Canvases and
  Priming"]. Seven portraits of 1888–1911 catalogued in Washington are
  on medium-weight (three), fine or very fine (two), tightly woven (one)
  and medium- to heavy-weight (one) plain weave; the tightly woven one is
  hemp mixed with linen [NGA pp.108, 111, 113, 115, 117, 120, 125].
- **French canvas.** He took "his taste for French canvas" from his
  student years in Paris [NPG "Introduction"]. Two stamped portrait
  canvases come from Paris canvas makers or their London importers
  [NPG "France and Italy", "London"]. His restorer wrote in 1926 that he
  had "an affection for a particular sort of French canvas… not primed
  and kept in stock. Only got ready for him when he wanted it" [APOLLO;
  NPG].
- **Thread count.** No count for any of his canvases appeared in
  accessible sources. *Gap.*
- **Cut from the roll, generously stretched.** "In every case, the
  generously stretched canvas is primed right to the edge, demonstrating
  that it was cut from a larger length" [APOLLO]. He often left "an
  excess of primed canvas, in some cases several inches, lapped round the
  back of the stretcher," which let him enlarge or shrink the picture
  later; one portrait was cut down by at least four inches, and others
  had a folded margin brought into the picture [APOLLO; NGA p.115].
- **Working stretchers.** At least one full-length was begun on a working
  stretcher, which "frequently results in passages along the edges that
  are not fully painted out" [MMX p.125].
- **Sizes.** Portraits range from about 64 × 76 cm [NGA p.125] to
  full-lengths of about 209 × 110 cm [MMX p.122] and 258 × 144 cm
  [APOLLO n.23].

## 2. Grounds

- **Commercial and mostly gray.** The priming is gray on nearly all the
  Tate's portraits and on most later portraits the authors saw; "the rest
  are white" [APOLLO]. The primings are "typical
  commercially-applied ones," varying from light to mid gray, all "made
  by mixing ivory black and lead white in linseed oil" [APOLLO]. A pupil
  wrote that he began "on a perfectly clean greyish coloured canvas (which
  he preferred)" [CH p.181].
- **Glue size.** Several canvases carry "a thick layer of glue size
  applied over the canvas, a feature not often seen in British primed
  canvases of this date, which suggests purchase abroad" [APOLLO].
- **Other grounds.** A thin, even, preprimed gray ground (1888); a
  thick, uniform, preprimed gray-white ground that hides the weave
  (1890); a thin white ground (1901); a thin gray ground (1902); a white
  or cream commercial ground (1902); a light gray ground under a slightly
  darker gray imprimatura (1907); a white ground (1911) [NGA pp.108, 111,
  113, 115, 117, 120, 125]. A portrait of
  1892: "a light gray ground preparation composed mainly of lead white
  mixed with some calcite and a little carbon-based black" [MMJ53
  p.175].
- **A warm exception.** A full-length of 1883–84 has a ground that is
  "a medium light, dull pink color composed of lead white tinted with
  particles of red iron oxide," apparently commercial and "very similar"
  to the ground of another portrait of 1882 [MMX p.129 n.17]. *Two
  objects.*
- **The ground as a middle tone.** The gray primings "provide a mid-tone
  which also intensified the colour of the brown, thinly-painted
  backgrounds" [APOLLO]. In one portrait the priming, "simply washed over
  a warm tone," stands as the half tones of a dress, and the gray priming
  shows through in places even after heavy reworking [APOLLO
  "Conclusion", "Application of Paint"]. In one unfinished portrait a
  hand is left as a near-bare "reserve" of primed canvas [APOLLO].
- **Thickness.** No micron values found. *Gap.*

## 3. Drawing and lay-in

- **Little drawing on the canvas.** "There is little evidence to support
  the idea that Sargent habitually drew or painted an outline directly
  onto the canvas, though he did do so on occasion" [APOLLO "Limited
  Working-out"]. One sitter reported a pencil outline still visible along
  the jaw of an hour-long sketch; in one unfinished portrait there is "no
  drawing" at all [APOLLO]. Drawing was not detected by infrared in one
  canvas, so his drawing material may not show under IRR [APOLLO n.10].
- **Charcoal, then a rag.** A pupil watched him place a head "with a bit
  of charcoal… with no more than a few careful lines over which he passed
  a rag," so that only faint traces remained [CH p.181]. A Royal Academy
  student recalled charcoal used to map "the proportions of the large
  masses," with a plumb line held up to check verticals [CH p.186].
- **Turpentine lay-in.** "At the start he used sparingly a little
  turpentine to rub in a general tone over the background and to outline
  the head (the real outline where the light and shadow meet…)," with the
  masses of hair and dress; "this was a matter of a few moments" [CH
  pp.181–182].
- **Masses, not contours.** A fellow artist described him drawing "with
  his brush, beginning with the shadows, and gradually evolving his
  figure from the background by means of large, loose volumes of shadow,
  half tones and light" [quoted in APOLLO]. In an unfinished portrait
  "the background… was blocked in, leaving space for the figure, whose
  bulk was then suggested with broad strokes and planes of colour which
  nudged the background back into place" [APOLLO]. X-radiographs of
  finished portraits show "an almost complete absence of actual contours"
  [APOLLO "Conclusion"].
- **Studies.** Loose pencil sketches on paper fix the pose for some
  portraits, not a traced outline [APOLLO].

- **Extenders.** His tube paints hold "easily detectable quantities of
  extenders such as kaolin, chalk, talc and barytes" [APOLLO]. In
  1885–86 his pigments were "of very good quality, with few extenders"
  [BAS]. *The two sources describe different periods.*
- **Range over time.** The later portraits use "a more limited selection
  of blue and yellow pigments" than the earlier ones [APOLLO]. The range
  was "quite wide but does not include every pigment available" [APOLLO],
  and he used "a wide range of high-quality paints consistently over his
  lifetime" [VCB abstract].
- **Suppliers.** Tubes from well-known British colormen, some bought
  locally while traveling [BAS "Materials"]; oil colors, "Magenta in
  tubes" (1888), Flake White (1893) and Bone Brown (1899) from one London
  colorman [NPG "Paints and paint medium"].

## 5. Media and varnish

- **Tube oils.** Paint samples showed "linseed oil in the darker colours
  and poppyseed oil in white paint: this is typical of manufactured tube
  paints at this time" [APOLLO "The Painter's Palette"].
- **Little medium in the lights.** Apart from the turpentine lay-in "he
  used his colour without a medium of any kind, neither oil, turpentine
  or any admixture. 'The thicker you paint, the more your colour flows'"
  [CH p.182]. He "used paints straight from the tube, or else thinned
  them" [APOLLO].
- **Much medium in the darks.** A fellow artist saw him use "oil and
  turpentine freely as a medium" [quoted in APOLLO]. "Liberal use of oil
  and turpentine to thin his darker paint is certainly confirmed by the
  dribbles seen on tacking margins" [APOLLO]. The dark backgrounds of many
  portraits are ivory black and Mars brown with "a generous quantity of
  paint medium" [APOLLO].
- **Megilp.** Poppy and linseed oil and "a medium modifier such as
  Megilp" were found in a painting of 1885–86, and "tubes of Megilp were
  also found in Sargent's paint box"; a deep madder mixed with megilp was
  laid thickly over an opaque pink [BAS "Materials"]. Spilled oil in one
  paint box was heat-bodied linseed oil with thinner [VCB, via summary].
  *Uncertain* (not read in the original).
- **A matting medium.** From 1902 to 1913 he bought, "in greater
  quantities," a medium sold "for mixing with oil colours to achieve a
  Matt finish" [NPG "Paints and paint medium"]. *How he used it is
  undocumented.*
- **Varnish.** Little evidence of varnish between layers in the later
  portraits [APOLLO]. A thin "retouching varnish" lies between the
  campaigns of one 1885–86 painting [BAS "Varnishing"], and a varnish
  layer lies under the repainted background of one full-length [MMX
  p.128]. Final varnishes were put on after framing; the original ones on
  a family series are consistent with mastic spirit varnish [APOLLO].
  He "didn't like a glossy varnish" [BAS "Varnishing"].

## 6. Layers and application

- **Brushes.** "Numerous hog-hair bristles found trapped deep in the
  paint" [APOLLO]. "Unfinished portraits show that initial paint-layers
  have brushstrokes from quarter-inch and half-inch brushes: the boldest,
  broadest strokes were used for finishing," with strokes "up to an inch"
  wide in fabrics and "fine points" for small details of faces [APOLLO].
  He wanted "good thick brushes that will hold the paint" [CH p.181]: "Use
  yourself to a large brush" [CH p.187]. Brush shapes (flat, filbert,
  round) are *undocumented* in the sources read.
- **A full palette.** "You do not want dabs of colour… you want plenty
  of paint to paint with"; he put out "enough paint so it seemed for a
  dozen pictures" [CH p.181]; "Do not starve your palette" [CH p.187]. On
  surviving palettes the colors run white, yellow, red, brown,
  green/blue and sometimes black [HAM]. Flesh mixtures were lead white
  with vermilion plus bone black, sometimes rose madder or viridian,
  "mixed together roughly on the palette" and finished by working them
  "into and onto adjacent brushstrokes on the canvas" [APOLLO].
- **Wet-in-wet, thick in the lights.** He wrote to a pupil: "paint all
  the half tones and general passages quite thick — and always paint one
  thing into another and not side by side until they touch" [CH p.184].
  "The finishing touches to faces were applied very much wet-in-wet,"
  confirmed in cross-section; a white dress holds "a great number of
  colours, each applied as a separate brushstroke to paint which was
  wet" [APOLLO]. Catalogued portraits are "mainly wet-into-wet, very
  thickly in the drapery folds… noticeably thinner in the sitter's face"
  (1890) or have faces in "broad, impasted strokes" (1901) [NGA pp.111,
  113].
- **Thickness varies across the canvas.** "The thickness of paint varies
  widely over the canvas in every portrait. Heavy impasto was used to
  sculpt jeweled details and hard highlights" [APOLLO]. Backgrounds are
  often thin: "a thin wash" (1888), "very thinly with liquid paint"
  (1901) [NGA pp.108, 113]. Drapery impasto reaches "almost 0.5 cm" in
  one portrait of 1907 [NGA p.120]; in another "the last touches of
  highlights are the most highly impasted" [NGA p.125].
- **Transparency without staining.** He told a pupil: "If you see a
  thing transparent, paint it transparent; don't get the effect by a thin
  stain showing the canvas through"; the shadow "was painted as heavily
  as the light" [CH p.183].
- **Soft edges made in the paint.** He laid a head in "slightly larger
  than he intended to leave it, so that he could model the edges with and
  into the background" [CH p.182]. He called it "slovenly" to soften a
  hard edge afterward by drawing a brush lightly across it [CH p.185].
- **Glazes and scumbles.** Mostly direct, with occasional glazing in
  reds and purples [BAS "Materials"]. Over a gray ground
  he laid a pure red lake on top of "toning layers" of vermilion, warm
  ochers and a little green, finishing with "scumbles of lead white"
  [MMJ53 p.175]. A repainted background is a loosely brushed mixture of
  yellow ochre, white and black, thin enough in places to mix optically
  with the rose layer beneath [MMX p.127]. A pure, unmixed stroke of rose
  madder appears within pale flesh [MMX p.126].
- **Darks.** Dark cobalt blue and emerald green were used "in place of
  blacks" in one painting of 1885–86 [BAS]; large amounts of dark,
  transparent viridian were found in the black and dark brown passages of
  one full-length [MET20].

## 7. Working sequence and sittings

- **Easel beside the sitter.** He "put his easel directly next to the
  sitter so that when he walked back from it he saw the canvas and the
  original in the same light, at the same distance" [CH p.182]. A
  fellow artist: he "placed his canvas on a level with the model, walked
  back until canvas and sitter were equal before his eye" [quoted in
  APOLLO]. He stepped back "after almost every stroke," wearing a track in
  the studio carpet [CH p.28].
- **Order within a sitting.** The two pupils' accounts agree: a general
  tone of background first, laid about half an inch over the neighboring
  tones; then the mass of hair and the main flesh tone, "painting flesh
  into background and background into flesh"; "at first he worked only
  for the middle tones, to model in large planes"; then the strongest
  accents, "painting deliberately into wet ground"; then subtler tones;
  "finally with deliberate touch the high lights are laid in" [CH
  pp.182–183, 187]. His teacher's formula was the same order: "Cherchez la
  demi-teinte, mettez quelques accents, et puis les lumières" (find the
  half tone, add a few accents and then the lights) [CH p.28].
- **One process over several sittings.** "He painted a head always in one
  process, but that could be carried over several sittings" [CH p.185].
  A student recalled the rule "to lay in the drawing afresh at every
  sitting" [CH p.186].
- **Scraping down and starting over.** Where the underlying structure was
  wrong "he ruthlessly scraped and repainted the head from the beginning"
  [CH p.185]; a pupil reported he "often repainted the head a dozen
  times," once sixteen times [CH p.184]. If a sitting was unsatisfactory
  "the whole is ruthlessly fogged by brushing together… The process is
  repeated until the canvas is completed" [CH p.187]. A fellow artist saw
  a day's work "scraped down to the quick" every morning for many days
  [BAS "Pentimenti"]; a fellow artist saw him "smudge and efface the part
  he wished to reconstruct, and begin again from a shapeless mass"
  [quoted in APOLLO].
- **New canvases.** He also abandoned canvases: after three weeks on one
  portrait he scraped it out and repainted it in three sittings; after
  twelve sittings on another he started a new canvas and finished in six
  [CH pp.184–185]. He urged keeping "any number of clean canvases ready,
  of all shapes and sizes" [CH p.185].
- **Number of sittings.** Usually "two to ten," with exceptions [APOLLO].
  One sitter held a pose for two hours while her hands were painted [CH
  pp.157–158].
- **Changes after the first campaign.** Profiles, arms, straps, a skirt
  and furniture were moved or repainted; one full-length shows "at least
  eight profiles" in the X-radiograph [MMX p.123; APOLLO]. Some later
  changes went over dry or varnished paint [MMX p.128; BAS "Pentimenti"].
  Many portraits were reworked after framing [APOLLO; MMX p.125].

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Drying cracks and wrinkles.** His changes "are now often visible
  through the development of a fine network of drying cracks, or as areas
  of wrinkled paint" [APOLLO]. Wide drying cracks in one full-length are
  "no doubt a consequence of the continuous reworking," made worse by
  painting over varnish [MMX pp.126, 128]. "Fine, linear, brittle
  cracking" suggests the primed canvas was rolled early [APOLLO]. Traction
  crackle is noted in two catalogued oils [NGA pp.103, 108].
- **Lead soaps.** In one portrait small raised bumps in the hair and a
  velvet bodice are lead soaps formed in the lead-white ground [MMX
  p.128].
- **Bloom in the darks.** Dark paint tends "to become milky or bloomed,"
  linked to glossy dark brown tube paints and possibly the thick glue size
  [APOLLO n.42].
- **Yellowed varnish.** Original mastic varnishes yellowed within a
  generation in daylit galleries [APOLLO].
- **Fading red lakes.** A purpurin-rich madder shows fading in
  cross-section, though not to the eye [MMJ53 p.175]; one of three lakes
  in a painting of 1885–86 has faded where the frame did not cover it
  [BAS "Varnishing"].
