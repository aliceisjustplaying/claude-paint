# Degas, c. 1880–1895: materials and process (pastel over peinture à l'essence)

Materials and method only; no artworks named. Tags like [AIC] refer to the source list at the end. "Uncertain" marks disagreement or thin evidence.

## 1. Supports

**Paper types.** Mostly cheap machine-made wood-pulp or mixed-furnish papers, chosen for colour and rough tooth [Met; N-A 618]. Recorded types:
- Wove papers are the most common. Laid papers (cream, ivory, pale blue) also occur [AIC; N-A 614; Thyssen].
- "Mottled" papers: a beige-to-brown or cream sheet with scattered light-blue, dark-blue, red, black and brown fibres. These were sold for charcoal and pastel work [N-A 618; NCAW].
- Pale-grey wove wrapping paper [Thyssen/search summary].
- Heavy rough papers dominate before c. 1885; from the mid-1880s, and especially the 1890s, **tracing paper** (sold in rolls from 1865). It has almost no tooth, so tooth came from fixative and the board beneath [NCAW citing Maheux; Kendall in MaM].

**Paper tones** recorded for 1875–1890 work:
- tan or mid-brown
- grey and grey-tan
- blue-grey
- buff
- pink or peach (one type has a Michallet watermark)
- light green
- cream

Mid-tones (tan, grey, blue-grey) are commonest; white is rare. **Many papers were aniline-dyed and have faded** (green to warm grey, pink to tan, blue to grey/buff) [Met; Getty; Norton Simon summary], so the original paper was more saturated than it looks now.

**Mounting.** Usually the sheet is wrapped over a 5–7 mm paperboard and taped only at the back edges, giving a firm, taut surface that tolerates wet work; board backs are sometimes blue-grey [Thyssen; N-A 618; AIC]. Also:
- sheets laid down over their whole surface onto board or millboard
- occasionally a sheet stretched on a wooden stretcher
- early essence works on paper mounted on canvas [AIC; search summary]

**Joined sheets.** He often butt-joined strips to one or more edges (from c. 2.5 cm to nearly a third of the image), sometimes mid-process; pastel runs across the seam and side-strokes leave pigment in the join. Late works combine several tracing-paper pieces on paper and board, the first pieces fixed before gluing [AIC; NCAW; N-A 616].

## 2. Pastel sticks

**What they are made of.** Ground pigment, a white filler (chalk, kaolin or similar) and a very small amount of gum binder (gum tragacanth or gum arabic). They were bought ready-made, either as sticks or as pointed sticks in paper wrappers [N-A glossary].

**Maker.** It is widely repeated that he used **Roché** (the Maison Macle firm, later La Maison du Pastel). The firm describes its sticks of that period as fairly hard and thin.
- Uncertain: this claim comes mainly from the maker's own history and from practitioners, not from technical analysis.
- Paris colourmen such as Lefranc also sold pastels and pastel boards [N-A 618].
- Treat the stick hardness as a range: firm enough for a sharp point and fine hatching, but soft enough to cover broad areas.

**Pigments identified in his pastels.**
- XRF/SEM-EDX on an 1882 pastel: bone black, vermilion, red ochre, chrome green, lead white among many; single points show many elements, so mixtures come from superimposed strokes, not blended sticks [Thyssen].
- Other analyses and summaries mention Prussian blue, viridian, cadmium yellow, yellow and red ochres, and at least two different red colourants in a single pastel (one fluoresces under UV, one does not) [N-A 616; Jackson's (secondary)].
- An oil-paint study found vermilion, emerald green, madder lake and black in his oils [Sci. Rep.].
- The Tate analysed loose pastel from eight works [Conservator 1993], but I could not get the results.
- Recurring hues in the late pastels: blue-green and salmon or orange-red, alongside muted greens and purples [MaM, exhibition notes].

## 3. Peinture à l'essence

**How it was made.** Squeeze tube oil paint onto blotting paper or rag and let it soak out as much oil as possible. This leaves a "short" paste. Thin that paste with turpentine (essence) [eclecticlight; Getty/Norton Simon summaries].

**What it does.**
- It dries quickly and completely matte, with a gouache-like look.
- It is applied with a hog-hair brush, as thin washes or as dry, dragged strokes.
- On paper it can leave oily halos or stains, and conservators sometimes keep these as original [Harvard].

**Supports and use.** It was used on tinted papers (brown, tan, pink), on board and on canvas. The paper colour served as the middle tone: dark lines drawn in essence, then white lights on top [Harvard].
- In the 1870s it was a full medium in its own right, sometimes mixed with watercolour or gouache.
- By the 1880s pastel was his main medium. Essence then mostly served as a fluid lay-in or underpainting under pastel, alongside gouache, distemper (détrempe: glue- or casein-bound paint) and monotype [AIC; Thyssen; MFA/search summary].
- Practical point: a lean, matte essence layer holds pastel well, while a glossy oil layer would not.

## 4. Other underlayers and mixed media

**Monotype under pastel** (most common late 1870s to mid-1880s). Oily printer's ink is rolled or brushed onto a copper or zinc plate (or similar) and printed on dampened paper.
- "Dark-field" method: cover the plate with ink and wipe out the lights with rags, brushes or fingers.
- "Light-field" method: brush the image onto a clean plate.
- He often pulled a second, paler impression (a "cognate") and worked pastel over it.
- The pastel can be thin enough for the print's tones to show through, or heavy enough to hide them [AIC; N-A 614; Buchberg & Neufeld via N-A].

**Gouache and pastel paste.** Opaque watercolour, alone or mixed with pastel paste (ground pastel with a little binder, applied wet), was used for broad back areas. He could drag a second colour through the wet paste with a brush. Thin water washes of dissolved pastel were used as underlayers. Gouache-heavy areas are opaque and raised when looked at under raking light [N-A 614, 616; AIC].

## 5. Wetting, steaming and fixing

**Wet pastel** (all seen in one c. 1882 pastel) [Getty]:
- dipping the sharpened tip in water and drawing with it, which gives a creamy, saturated line
- wetting sticks and applying the colour with a brush
- grinding pastel and mixing it with water into a paste

A dampened stick can be drawn over earlier layers without lifting them [N-A 618].

**Steam.** Steam from a boiling kettle, played over the surface, did one of two things:
- dissolved a layer into a soft "vaporous film"
- melted thick passages into a paste that he then reworked with a brush or knife as it dried

It can leave fine pitting. Accounts are mainly anecdotal (Rouart 1945; Boggs & Maheux) [Artist's Network; Dix-Neuf abstract].

**Fixative.** Commercial fixatives darkened and dulled colour, so his friend Luigi Chialiva (painter and chemist) supplied one said to leave colour and matteness unchanged.
- Sprayed **between layers, on selected areas only**, repeatedly (Rouart); later layers sit on top unmixed and earlier colour shows through gaps, like a glaze.
- The **final layer was most likely left unfixed** [Met; Thyssen].
- Fixing also builds up tooth and a crust on smooth tracing paper.
- Under the microscope the fixative shows as glistening spots or a thin film. Where heavily fixed, the colour reads slightly darker [AIC; Thyssen].

**What the fixative was (uncertain).**
- Thyssen found a bluish-fluorescing organic layer, 15–40 µm thick, between paper and pastel, plus an ultra-thin surface film, and refers to "moist casein".
- Modern commercial "Degas" fixatives use casein in alcohol and water, citing a Chialiva patent from the 1890s.
- Richard Kendall said dilute shellac [MaM].
- Some pastels show **no fixative at all** [N-A 616, 618]. Fixing between layers was a method, not a rule.

## 6. Strokes, contours and coverage

**Underdrawing.** Light charcoal or black pastel, sometimes graphite. Often sparse, as though traced or transferred from an earlier drawing. Tracing and counterproofs (pressing damp paper onto a drawing) let him reuse figures [N-A 616, 618; NCAW].
- Contours are drawn in firmly in some places and broken, blurred or left out in others [Thyssen].
- Contours are often restated late, sometimes in strong colour rather than black [Getty]; late charcoal lines stay visible through colour [MaM].

**Mark-making.**
- Fine pointed-end strokes for detail. Squiggles and zigzags for frills and feathery textures.
- Networks of fine parallel hatching for modelling. Hatching follows the form ("contour hatching") or runs diagonally in one direction, and is crossed by other colours.
- Broad side-of-stick strokes for skies, water and backgrounds. On a rough or board-backed surface these catch only the high points and give a scumbled, speckled effect [AIC; Getty; Wikipedia summary; MaM].
- Through the 1880s, less smudging and more separate directional strokes, fixed between layers. Blending persists locally (fingers, rag, brush, stump): blended dark areas recede, crisp hatched areas advance [Getty; N-A 618; NCAW].
- Very heavy working can burnish the pastel and roughen the paper surface [search summary].
- Small complementary touches are common: green in the shadows of flesh, blue against orange, blue shadows beside yellow reflected light [N-A 616, 618].

**How much paper shows.** This varies within a single picture.
- 1880s: important areas are worked up, secondary areas are only sketched, and the tinted paper is left bare as a middle tone, especially in thinly worked passages [Getty; N-A 618; Maheux].
- Some works are almost fully covered [N-A 616].
- 1890s work tends toward denser, more abstract all-over layering. The hatching still lets patchy areas look intentional.

**Scale.** Recorded sheet sizes for pastels of this period:
- about 49 × 72 cm, 65 × 50 cm, 65 × 58 cm, 73 × 57 cm and 81 × 62 cm
- late pieced works up to about 105 × 108 cm
- monotype plates from small (a few tens of cm) up to about 35 × 71 cm

He sometimes used standard commercial formats [AIC; N-A 618; MaM].

**Light.** Persistent interest in artificial light (gas, lamp, footlights), low or side lighting and strong value contrast; dark-field monotype suits this [general; AIC].
