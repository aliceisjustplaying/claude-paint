# Research: how painters paint trees (a sourced note)

Worktree: ~/src/a/claude-paint-research-trees2 (branch research-trees2).
Write ONE file: notes/research/painting_trees.md. Commit it; do not push.
Scratch: run `~/.local/bin/agent-tmp trees2` once and export TMPDIR=TMP=TEMP.

## Why
Painters in this project (AI models, working from knowledge only) paint
original pictures in the manner of Caspar David Friedrich. Given botany
(branch taper rules, how junctions form), they turned it into tree-growing
algorithms and drew thousands of twigs one by one; the result reads as a
computed fractal. What they lack is not botany but how a *painter* renders
a tree: how people who paint trees by hand see and simplify them.

## What the note should cover (knowledge, not recipes)
1. How painters, especially in Friedrich's time and circle (Dahl, Carus,
   the Dresden academy, the Romantic landscape painters) and in the oil
   landscape tradition generally, render trees: big masses before
   branches; the silhouette against the sky; the fine outer twigs of a
   bare crown as a tone or haze rather than lines; which few twigs and
   limbs get drawn singly; lost and found edges; how distance changes what
   is shown. From treatises, painters' letters and manuals, museum and
   conservation texts.
2. How Friedrich himself drew and painted trees, from text sources:
   carry over section 5 of notes/research/trees.md (read it; keep its
   sources) and add what you find on his handling: "small strokes and
   hooks", "jagged lines", the order of painting, conservators' notes on
   his tree brushwork, the dated studies from nature reused in paintings.
3. What Friedrich's contemporaries said good and bad tree painting was
   (e.g. Carus on painters who "confound all the specific forms of tree
   growth"), and what separates a tree that reads as seen from one that
   reads as made up.
4. Species only as far as a painter sees them at a distance: the
   characteristic silhouette and gesture of an oak, a birch, a spruce, a
   pine, a willow, a beech; no taper formulas or growth rules.
