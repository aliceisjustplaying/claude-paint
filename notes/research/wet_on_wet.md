# Wet-on-wet landscape painting: materials and technique for an oil-paint simulator

**Scope.** This note covers the fast alla prima landscape method, where a whole landscape is finished in one sitting and every layer goes into wet paint. It describes the ground, paint, tools, order of work and the physics that makes it hold together. Values tagged **[est]** are engineering guesses to tune, not measured figures.

---

## 1. Ground

- **Support:** a stretched, double-primed canvas, commonly 18×24 in (457×610 mm). The priming makes it nonabsorbent, so the wet ground stays open and the weave stands up through it.
- **Variants:** a black or gray gesso ground, left to dry fully, for night scenes and strong contrast. Contact paper or tape can mask a shape (an oval, a window) before the ground goes on, which gives a vignette with a clean edge.
- **Liquid white:** an oil white thinned with oil and a little solvent. One manufacturer's safety data sheet gives 1–10% hydrotreated heavy naphtha, density 1.2–1.6 g/cm³ and a flash point near 62 °C; the full recipe is unpublished. A home version of about one part titanium white to one part linseed oil is common but unverified. A liquid black (the same medium with carbon black) serves dark grounds, and a pigment-free liquid clear keeps the slip without lightening the colors laid into it.
- **Application:** a thin, even coat over the whole canvas with a wide flat brush, just enough to cover it. Painting starts while it is still wet. No thickness figure is published.
- **What it does:** colors laid into the wet ground slide and blend on the canvas, and brushing across it removes excess paint, so later layers go on easily. Each stroke also picks up some of the white, so color laid into liquid white comes out lighter. That is why the clear version exists.

## 2. Paint

**Palette.** A small fixed set of firm oils: titanium white, phthalo blue, prussian blue, phthalo green, alizarin crimson, a bright red, a cadmium-yellow hue, indian yellow, yellow ochre, sap green, dark sienna, van dyke brown, a chromatic black and a premixed mountain dark.

**Consistency:** the paint is far stiffer than ordinary tube oil, close to dry, and that firmness is what makes the method work. No viscosity or yield data are published.

**Thinning:** thinner goes on sparingly, a touch on the brush so the new paint is a little softer than the paint already on the canvas. Liner paint is thinned until it almost runs. The thinner is an odorless hydrocarbon solvent with kinematic viscosity at or below about 20 mm²/s.

## 3. Tools

The landscape brushes mix natural and synthetic bristle; the liner and the soft blender are natural bristle.

| Tool | Size | Use and handling |
|---|---|---|
| **2-inch flat** | head ≈50 mm wide, hair ≈63 mm long | Skies, water and any large area. Loaded by tapping into the paint so only the top bristles carry it. Clouds: dab with one corner, then small circles with the brush on its side. Mist: tap the base of a shape with a clean, dry brush and lift gently upward. A brush wet with thinner thins the paint and turns it mushy. |
| **1-inch flat** | 25 mm | Base coats for clouds, water, mountains, trees and bushes; crisscross strokes at mountain bases for fog. |
| **1-inch oval** | | Evergreens, foothills and highlights. |
| **#6 fan** | [est] 30–35 mm | Evergreens (heavily loaded on both sides), distant trees (the top corner only), grass (long flat strokes pushed upward, back and forth in a zigzag), trunks, foothills, boats. |
| **#3 fan** | [est] ~20 mm | The same marks, smaller and tighter. |
| **#6 filbert** | | Trunks, waves and detail. |
| **Liner** | point [est] 1–2 mm | Loaded with thin paint, the bristles rolled to a sharp point. Twigs, fine branches and the signature. |
| **#10 knife** | straight blade ≈95×32 mm | Larger and stiffer than an ordinary painting knife, with a straight edge. Mountains. |
| **#5 knife** | smaller | Tight areas. |

**Knife loading:** paint is pressed out flat on the palette, then the knife edge cuts across it and picks up a thin bead of paint along the edge.

**Knife deposit:**
- **Mountain body:** the dark goes on firmly and the excess is scraped back, but not all of it. Scraping too much leaves too little dark for the highlights to read against.
- **Highlights:** almost no pressure. The bead glides over the dark and breaks on the texture, leaving gaps, and the gaps are what make it read as rock. Solid, even coverage looks like frosting.
- **Direction:** highlight and shadow strokes follow the slope of each face.
- **Scratching:** the knife point scratches in sticks and twigs.

**Cleaning:** the brush is washed in odorless thinner, then the excess is shaken and knocked out against something hard, so it goes back to the paint nearly dry.

## 4. Order of work

A winter lake scene shows the usual sequence:

1. Liquid white over the whole canvas with the 2-inch brush, left wet.
2. Sky: alizarin crimson crisscrossed low for a glow. Phthalo blue with the chromatic black above, crisscrossed all the way across the top, then blended downward without overworking.
3. Water: the same colors in horizontal strokes.
4. Mountain: the knife with a dark mix (prussian blue, chromatic black, van dyke brown, alizarin). The body is softened with the 1-inch brush and its base crisscrossed into fog.
5. Mountain highlights: a small bead of white on the lit side; white, phthalo blue and a little of the dark on the shadow side. Tap the base and lift for mist.
6. Background evergreens: the mountain dark plus a little white, on the fan brush.
7. Reflections: the 1-inch brush pulled straight down (an angled pull makes them look wrong), then light horizontal passes across. The water line: a very small bead of titanium white mixed with liquid white, cut in with the knife.
8. Foreground evergreens and bushes pushed up in dark. Scratched twigs.
9. Highlights: white, liquid white and a touch of blue, leaving some dark showing. Wipe the brush whenever it picks up dark.
10. Liner work and the signature.

**Timing:** a complete landscape takes about half an hour of painting time. Complex scenes have parts prepared beforehand.

## 5. Why wet-on-wet works physically

- **The rule:** thin paint sticks to thick paint. The first layers are the firmest; each later layer is slightly softer than what lies under it, so it can sit on top without sinking in.
- **Inference [est]:** stiff paint holds its shape on the thin wet film. A slightly more fluid paint then transfers onto it by adhesion without shearing the lower layer. If the top paint is stiffer, the brush lifts the lower layer instead. A stroke that will not stick gets a touch of thinner.
- **Pickup:** brushes lift wet color from underneath, so a brush that picks up dark is wiped before the next light stroke. Colors also mix on the brush itself: tap two colors in, then push.
- **Layer order:** dark before light and back before front. Broken light paint over dark lets the dark show through.

## 6. Motifs, composition and color schemes

**Usual subjects:** trees in almost every painting (evergreen and deciduous about equally, often with bushes); cumulus skies; mountains, often snowy; lakes and rivers, sometimes a waterfall or the sea; winter scenes; a cabin; occasionally an oval or half-oval vignette.

**Habits:**
- Trees come in pairs or clusters rather than alone.
- Clouds are light and rounded on top and nearly flat underneath.
- Distant trees get little detail, and some stay without highlights.
- Mountains fade into mist at the base, and lakes get vertical reflections plus a water line.
- Vignettes are masked with contact paper or tape.

**Color schemes:**
- **Skies:** phthalo blue over a crimson glow.
- **Mountains:** a dark mix with plenty of crimson, which gives the blue a lavender cast.
- **Foliage:** a blue-black-brown-crimson dark, highlighted with sap green plus cadmium yellow, yellow ochre or indian yellow, sometimes crimson or bright red.
- **Cabins:** van dyke brown and dark sienna, highlighted by grazing a bead of paint off the knife.

## Implications for a simulator

Every [est] value is a tunable guess; none is published.

1. **Rheology:**
   - Model paint as a yield-stress (Bingham or Herschel-Bulkley) fluid.
   - Firm colors: yield stress [est] 3–10× a soft tube oil.
   - Liquid grounds: near-Newtonian and thin.
   - Thinner lowers yield stress, and liner paint is near zero.
   - Density: 1.35–2.0 (colors) and 1.2–1.6 g/cm³ (grounds).
2. **Deposit rule:** transfer happens when tool-paint yield stress is at or below canvas-paint yield stress and contact pressure is low. Otherwise the tool picks up canvas paint. Mixing scales with shear and contact time.
3. **Liquid white:**
   - Thickness [est] 20–50 µm.
   - It stays wet all session, so no drying model is needed.
   - It lowers surface friction and adds white to each stroke.
   - Liquid black adds carbon black; liquid clear has no pigment.
4. **Canvas:** 18×24 in (457×610 mm), nonabsorbent and double-primed. Its weave peaks decide where knife paint breaks.
5. **Tools:**
   - 2-inch flat: 50×63 mm hair.
   - 1-inch flat: 25 mm.
   - #6 fan: [est] 30–35 mm. #3 fan: [est] ~20 mm.
   - Liner point: [est] 1–2 mm.
   - #10 knife: 95×32 mm straight edge.
   - Loading per side and per corner (a tap loads the top bristles only).
6. **Knife:**
   - The bead is [est] 1–2 mm along the edge.
   - Body mode: high pressure, full deposit, partial scrape.
   - Highlight mode: near-zero force, so paint transfers only at texture and paint peaks. Aim for [est] 30–70% broken fill, with more gaps at lower pressure.
   - Point scratch removes paint along a line.
7. **Strokes by motif:**
   - Sky: rows of X strokes, then horizontal blending downward.
   - Clouds: corner taps and small circles with flat bottoms, then a dry upward blend.
   - Mountain: knife ridge, 1-inch body blend, tap-and-lift mist.
   - Evergreen: a center line, then fan dabs widening downward.
   - Bushes: corner push-ups.
   - Grass: a fan brush pushed up in a zigzag.
   - Reflections: a vertical pull, light horizontal passes and a knife water line.
   - Twigs: liner or knife scratch.
8. **Order:** ground → sky → water tone → mountains (dark → highlight → mist) → background trees → reflections and water line → middle ground → foreground darks → highlights → twigs → signature.
9. **Optics:**
   - PB15:3, PG7 and PR83 are transparent and high-tinting; PW6 is opaque.
   - The chromatic black (PR83 + PG7) is not neutral.
