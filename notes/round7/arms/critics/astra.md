## 2. Paint or pixels?

### A — most costly first

- **The crown reads as constructed linework.** Long, nearly straight tapers overlap with hard, clean intersections. Broken segments near their tips resemble interrupted plotting rather than a brush changing pressure. The problem is the geometry, not insufficient surface grain. (`A_crop_center.png`)
- **The water and shore use incompatible edges.** The far land has a crisply scalloped top and a soft, dark blur beneath it. In front, grass remains individually needle-sharp. That combination suggests separate rendering treatments rather than one painter deciding which edges matter. (`A_crop_lowerleft.png`)
- **The sky has mottling without much readable handling.** Small cloudy flecks sit across broad color bands while a fine polygonal crack network crosses them. Neither tells me how a loaded brush traveled. (`A_crop_upperright.png`)
- **Keep:** the muted blue, cream and ocher relationship, some broad horizontal foreground smears and the uneven dark trunk mass. These are more convincing than the exquisitely specified twigs. (`A.png`, `A_crop_lowerleft.png`)

### B — most costly first

- **The large smooth fields overpower the brushwork.** Sky and water fade almost continuously from warm to cool and light to dark. The dark upper corners reinforce a vignette-like finish. Surface cracks do not make those transitions feel painted. (`B_crop_upperright.png`, `B_crop_lowerleft.png`, `B.png`)
- **The trees are too completely filled in.** Their little edge dabs are plausibly brush-made, but they decorate near-solid black interiors. The small openings in the right tree help; most of the foliage offers no comparable negotiation between sky and leaves. (`B_crop_center.png`)
- **The reflections repeat the trees too legibly.** Horizontal interruptions roughen the mirrored shapes without really losing their identities. The water feels like a transformation of the objects above it. (`B.png`)
- **Keep:** the long olive-black bank strokes, their uneven overlaps and the thin yellow gaps at the waterline. Here marks actually build a surface rather than merely decorate one. (`B_crop_lowerleft.png`)

### C — most costly first

- **The sky’s repeated fine mottling competes with its broad strokes.** The pink swipe has a direction, thickness and broken end; the surrounding blue-gray granulation has a much more uniform small scale. That makes the swipe look placed over a textured background. (`C_crop_upperright.png`)
- **The town remains a cutout at its upper edge.** The tower and roofs share a largely continuous gray fill. Their lower edges dissolve much more convincingly, although the repeated short vertical marks under the shore begin to look combed. (`C_crop_center.png`)
- **The water mixes smooth blending with isolated scratch-like streaks.** The lower-left reeds are more individually bent than A’s grass, but their wiry clarity still separates them from the broad painted masses. (`C_crop_lowerleft.png`)
- **Keep:** the pink cloud drag, the overlapping cool water strokes and the town’s partially lost base. C already contains the best argument for using fewer, larger marks. (`C_crop_upperright.png`, `C_crop_center.png`, `C.png`)

### The “JPEG artifact” look

I would call it **patchy fine-scale mottling over smooth fields**, compounded by **a conspicuous crack mesh**. The flecks clump into little islands of similar size; some bands look stippled while adjacent areas are almost airbrushed. The eye reads that mismatch as damaged or processed imagery rather than pigment deposited by a particular stroke. The clearest examples are A’s blue sky and C’s blue-to-yellow cloud transitions. (`A_crop_upperright.png`, `C_crop_upperright.png`)

The cracks are a separate problem: large irregular cells remain readable across sky, water and silhouettes, pulling attention onto a flat surface in front of the scene. R has cracks and mottling too, so their mere presence cannot explain the difference. In R, more visible directional strokes and varied shapes compete successfully with them. I cannot infer an encoding or engine defect from these images, and I do not see a clear regular square-block pattern that would justify diagnosing JPEG compression. (`A_crop_lowerleft.png`, `B_crop_center.png`, `C_crop_lowerleft.png`, `R_reference_crop_center.png`)

## 3. What R has

**A relationship, not just a motif.** A small person stands on a winding route toward an empty ruin. The broken fence marks the near ground; the bare tree and clustered evergreens make unequal companions. I can imagine exposure, hesitation or a destination. These readings come from the arrangement, not simply from adding a moon. (`R_reference_1000.png`)

**Traversable depth.** The path enters from below, turns repeatedly and thins toward the ruin. The fence approaches from another direction. A, B and C mostly stop the viewer at successive horizontal strips; R carries the viewer across them. (`R_reference_1000.png`, `A.png`, `B.png`, `C.png`)

**More consequential irregularity.** R’s left tree is awkward in a specific way: heavy low forks, abrupt elbows and an uneven crown. The ruin has actual light-filled openings rather than merely a recognizable outline. Its base gets lost in pale scumbling. Those differences give separate objects separate kinds of presence. (`R_reference_1000.png`, `R_reference_crop_center.png`)

R is not free of shorthand: the birds are little symbols, the path is conspicuously serpentine and the conifers repeat a similar construction. But those weaknesses do not organize the entire image. Its emotional advantage is not simply “more texture” or “more things.” It is the small figure’s relationship to a large, partly inaccessible world. (`R_reference_1000.png`, `R_reference_crop_center.png`)

## 4. Advice — in order of payoff

1. **Change the pictorial problem before changing the texture.** Make the next studies without a pond, reflected central subject or paired foreground grass curtains. Try an obstructed route, an off-center shelter or a slope hiding the destination. Decide what the viewer wants to reach and what prevents it. R’s path and ruin are the evidence for this priority, not a prescription to copy its props. (`R_reference_1000.png` versus `A.png`, `B.png`, `C.png`)

2. **Make an unaged version.** For the tool builders: expose separate switches for cracks, corner darkening and fine surface mottling, wherever those effects originate. Compare the same painting with each removed. Do not replace them with another universal “paint” filter. The new paintings’ crops show that surface aging is currently easier to see than many brush decisions. (`A_crop_upperright.png`, `B_crop_upperright.png`, `C_crop_lowerleft.png`)

3. **Replace precise inventories with selective masses.** For A, paint a handful of unequal branch groups before adding a few twigs. For B, open the foliage and lose substantial portions of the reflections. For C, merge some roofs into the sky and water instead of describing the entire skyline. Preserve only a few sharp accents. These target the specific linework, filled silhouettes and continuous outlines visible in the center crops.

4. **Give each stroke a job and a history.** Build sky planes with broad overlapping passes; let water strokes interrupt reflections; let foreground dabs join into clods. For the tools, prioritize controllable paint depletion, pickup of underlying color, pressure changes and broken coverage along a stroke over additional all-over noise. Test these in simple swatches first. B’s bank and C’s pink cloud are useful visible starting points. (`B_crop_lowerleft.png`, `C_crop_upperright.png`)

5. **Judge composition small and handling close.** Compare thumbnails without texture, then inspect full-resolution crops with surface effects disabled. Keep awkward decisions that create character; remove repetition that merely advertises a procedure. R’s crooked tree is the former, A’s fan of rays the latter. “More bad painter” should mean a visible, fallible choice—not more simulated damage. (`R_reference_1000.png`, `A_crop_center.png`)
