# Caspar David Friedrich: painting materials and technique

*Process and hand (sequence, studies and their transfer, edges, pace, what copies got wrong) are in `friedrich_process.md`.*

*Sourced technical report for a physical paint simulator. Every claim cites a source key; full URLs are in the "Sources" list at the end. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified, disputed or taken from a single object.*

## Bottom line

Friedrich painted in oil on fine, plain-weave linen that he bought already primed. The grounds had two to four thin layers: a warm lower layer (red or ocher) under a light, lead-white-rich top layer [KÖR p.284; CATS p.127]. Over a precise graphite or ink underdrawing he laid a very thin underpainting, then only one or two very thin paint layers [SMB-proj; CATS p.127]. He used few pigments. Before about 1820 his key blue was smalt, a semi-transparent cobalt glass. After that he used cobalt blue and chrome yellow [ALF pp.341, 348–349]. His smooth gradations come from stippling and from paint pooling in the fine texture of the ground, not from thick blending [CATS p.127; NG p.56]. No accessible source gives numeric thread counts or paint-film thicknesses in microns for his paintings. The simulator values below for those are labeled proxies or assumptions.

**Terms used below.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layers between canvas and paint. *Cross-section*: a microscopic sample cut through all layers. *IRR (infrared reflectography)*: imaging that shows carbon-based drawing under paint. *XRF (X-ray fluorescence)*: elemental analysis. *Glaze*: a transparent paint layer. *Impasto*: thick, raised paint. *Lining*: gluing a second canvas to the back. *Craquelure*: the network of cracks in paint or varnish.

## 1. Supports

- **Fabric.** The Berlin *Monk by the Sea* and *Abbey in the Oakwood* are on "fine linen canvas." Both canvases were cut from adjoining sections of one roll, which was established by thread-count weave matching [CATS p.127 and n.2]. The Dresden paintings use locally grown flax, handwoven in plain weave. In his late work Friedrich preferred "finer, more densely woven canvases" [KÖR p.283].
- **One piece of cloth.** He used a single strip of canvas regardless of size, so even large formats have no seams [KÖR p.283].
- **Thread count.** No numeric count for any Friedrich canvas appeared in accessible sources. Mösl's 2021 dissertation and a 2024–25 journal double issue on 48 paintings probably contain them [ART; SKD]. **Proxy:** the Danish painter Eckersberg, who also bought ready-primed Dresden canvas (1827–34), painted his post-1816 Danish works on canvases of 10–16 threads/cm in both directions [CATS-E pp.45–47].
- **Sizes.** From 32.5 × 45 cm (*Winter Landscape*) [RSC p.42] to 110.6 × 171.4 cm (*Monk*) [CATS p.126].
- **Stretching.** The most common stretcher type in his work is softwood with blind mortise-and-tenon corners and hardwood wedges [KÖR p.283]. Uneven pull marks ("cusping") on only one edge of *Winter Landscape* suggest the piece was cut from a larger, already-primed length [NG pp.51, 55].
- **Panels.** None of the sources I consulted document panel supports for his oils. *Uncertain.*

## 2. Grounds (preparation layers)

- **Commercial, multi-layer and warm underneath.** Friedrich used commercially primed canvas cut from long lengths. Dresden suppliers sold primed cloth in several colors [KÖR p.284]. Dresden grounds have **two to four thin layers** [KÖR p.284]. Lower layers contain inexpensive ocher, burnt red earth, chalk and barium sulfate and "only served to smooth and even out the weave texture." Upper layers are dominated by lead white, probably bound in oil, and Friedrich painted mostly on "a patchy whitish ground" [KÖR p.284].
- **Berlin pair.** Three priming layers: bright red, then two light brown. The first two were applied with a spatula. The third looks roller-applied and has "a finely textured structure" that Friedrich exploited [CATS p.127; SMB-blog]. In a sky cross-section of *Abbey*, the red preparation contains aluminosilicate and iron oxides. It is followed by several yellow-gray lead-white layers containing iron oxides, vermilion grains and calcium-rich grains [NPJ25].
- **London *Winter Landscape*.** Two layers, both chalk and lead white with a little umber and ocher. The upper layer has more lead white, so it is lighter and cooler. The binder is walnut oil [NG p.55].
- **Tool marks.** X-rays show wavy strokes from priming knives in the lower layers. The top layer was brushed, and its bristle striations "shine through the thin upper paint layers" and are visible to the naked eye [KÖR p.284].
- **Colored grounds by motif.** *Two Men Contemplating the Moon* (Dresden) has a reddish-ocher top ground that serves as the mid-tone and stays visible under loose paint [KÖR p.284]. In *The Sea of Ice* a pink or reddish ground shows through the sky and is left bare in places [HH].
- **Weave visibility.** The lower ground layers level the weave [KÖR p.284]. The visible micro-texture comes mainly from the top ground (brush striations or roller texture), not from the weave.
- **Thickness.** No micron values were found. *Gap.*

## 3. Underdrawing

- **Media.** Graphite pencils of different hardness and black chalk, sometimes in two passes: a faint first outline, then a bolder one. Ruled lines were probably drawn against a ruler [CATS pp.128, 131]. Carus described chalk and pencil first, then "quill-pen and ink" [CATS p.132; NG p.49]. In *Winter Landscape*, faint ruled lines (possibly pencil) sit under thicker ink lines [NG pp.49, 58].
- **Change over his career.** Early works are drawn almost entirely in graphite, and the drawing often stays visible through the extremely thin paint. From 1811 to the mid- or late 1820s a pen is added and the paint gets denser. Late works use a pointed brush, and the drawing is almost fully covered [ART, summarizing Mösl 2021].
- **Precision.** "Rich in detail," with "no gradation of detail according to significance" [CATS p.132]. Friedrich often *removed* drawn motifs during painting. *Monk* has three drawn ships that were never painted [CATS p.128].
- **Visible through paint.** Lines "still partly shimmer through" [CATS p.132]. Drawn dune and grass zigzags show through in *Monk* [CATS p.130]. Traces of the drawing appear as dark dots at color boundaries in *The Sea of Ice* [HH].
- **Exception.** Infrared found no underdrawing in the Met's *Two Men Contemplating the Moon* (c.1825–30) [MET PDF pp.19, 45].
- **Tools.** Kersting's 1811 picture of Friedrich's studio shows two palettes, a set square, a T-square, a ruler and a maulstick [KÖR/Busch pp.326–327].

## 5. Binding media and additives

- **Walnut oil** in both ground and paint of *Winter Landscape* [NG p.55]. This is the only published medium analysis I found. Linseed or poppy oil, resins and driers are *unverified* for Friedrich.
- **Egg white** as a temporary coating (see section 7) [CATS p.127; KÖR p.283].

## 6. Layer structure and application

- **Number of layers.** "One to two very thin layers" of paint [SMB-proj]. *Abbey*: primings 1–3, underdrawing, underpainting, one smalt paint layer, egg white, then later varnishes [CATS fig. 3]. *Monk*: a first dark-blue layer, then light blue, pink and white over the central sky [CATS p.127]. *Winter Landscape*: "a single thin layer using remarkably few pigments" [NG p.56]. *The Cross in the Mountains* (1807/08) sky: two ocher ground layers, a patchy white ground, a thin violet layer (smalt splinters and cinnabar), then a very thin pale-yellow layer [KÖR fig. 6].
- **Film thickness.** Described as "very, very fine layers" [SPK] and a "gossamer-thin film" [CATS p.127]. No micron values were found. *Gap.*
- **The pooling effect.** The thin paint "accumulated in the depths of the textured ground layers," producing the finest dots of color. That let him avoid visible brushstrokes and get "almost continuous gradations" [CATS p.127].
- **Stippling.** Skies, mist and distant hills are stippled, which "enhance[s] the transparency and light scattering" [NG p.56]. The moon sky of *Two Men Contemplating the Moon* is stippled [KÖR fig. 7].
- **Strokes.** Early brushwork is "painstaking… small strokes of paint placed side by side like a closely woven textile." Firs are done in short hatched strokes [NG pp.49–50]. Grass is done with "fine upturning" strokes laid over the finished snow [NG p.56].
- **Impasto.** In early works it is limited, for example the slight impasto of foreground snow [NG p.50]. *The Sea of Ice* mixes thick and thin paint and leaves the ground bare in places [HH].
- **Order of work.** Ground (bought), then underdrawing, then a "very thin underpainting," then paint [CATS p.127]. Details go on last: grass over snow [NG p.56]; twenty small gulls were added to the completed *Monk* [CATS p.130]. Brush types (sable or bristle) are *undocumented* in these sources.
- **Glazing.** Friedrich told Carus to lay a dark glaze over a whole moonlit picture except the moon, "growing darker toward the picture's edges" [MET PDF p.35]. This is advice to another painter, not an analysis of his own paintings.

## 7. Varnish

- **Original coating.** Egg white. Friedrich's letters say he coated fresh paintings with a temporary egg-white film. Buyers were to wash it off and apply a mastic resin varnish within a year [KÖR p.283]. Egg-white remains were detected on *Abbey* [CATS p.127] and on Dresden's *View over the Elbe Valley* [KÖR p.283].
- **Later coatings.** *Monk* and *Abbey* had up to **seven** yellowed varnish layers. Parts of them had fine microcracks whose edges reflected light "like a milky veil" [SMB-blog]. *Winter Landscape* had yellowed dammar, possibly with copaiba balsam residues [NG p.55]. The Hamburg *Sea of Ice* was lined in 1938 [HH].

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Fragility.** The thin paint made the paintings "exceptionally prone to damage" [SMB-proj]. Numerous micro-losses come from abrasion during past cleaning [SMB-blog].
- **Smalt discoloration.** In *Abbey*, smalt has partly lost its color. Low potassium (4.9 wt%) and changes in cobalt coordination show it is in an intermediate stage of degradation. A simulation of the original colors comes out cooler and bluer [NPJ25; SMB-blog]. Smalt can turn brown over time [HH].
- **Lining damage.** The 1906 wax-resin lining of *Monk* and *Abbey* left lumps, iron marks and ground and paint damage [SMB-blog]. Restoration (2013–16) found about 97% (*Monk*) and 98% (*Abbey*) of the original paint surviving [SMB-blog].
- **Craquelure.** *The Sea of Ice* has an elongated "ear-of-grain-shaped" craquelure, probably from pressure on the back [HH]. No quantitative crack study of a Friedrich painting was found. **Generic mechanics:** corner cracks form perpendicular to the diagonal within about 5–10% of the diagonal length. A mock-up corner showed about 6 ± 3 mm crack spacing. Central cracks come from isotropic drying shrinkage of the oil paint [NPJ24].
- **Pentimenti** (the painter's own changes). Few in *Winter Landscape* [NG p.49]. The turned figure in *Monk* is a rare case [ART; CATS p.129].

## 9. Greens

*Same conventions as sections 1–8: source keys in brackets, full URLs in the source list below. "Unverified" means I could not see the primary text myself.*

**Bottom line.** Friedrich's greens were mostly **mixed on the palette** from his blues and yellows. He also used two or three true green pigments sparingly. The Dresden team (14 paintings) writes, in my translation, that "the palette was very sparing" but his greens show a "lush variety" ("üppige Vielfalt"). They list the true greens as "copper-containing pigments and green earth." He "often mixed his green from blue and yellow," using Prussian blue "very often" and smalt and cobalt blue as well. The yellow part came from yellow ocher, Naples yellow or chrome yellow [MÄD p.102]. Green passages often have "several (up to four) layers of different greens" stacked on top of each other [MÄD p.102].

### Evidence in Friedrich's paintings

- **Winter Landscape (London, c.1811), grass.** The grass has no true green pigment: "smalt, Naples yellow, bone black, ochre and possibly Prussian blue in varying proportions" [NG p.56]. Section 4 omits the smalt; it should be added to the grass list.
- **Dresden, 14 paintings.** Copper greens and green earth were used; mixtures were frequent (see the bottom line above) [MÄD p.102]. The abstract does not say which paintings or dates contain green earth. *Gap.*
- **Bohemian Landscape with the Milleschauer (1808, Dresden)** [CDF-M]. XRF found Cu plus As, and SEM-EDX placed both in the same layer. Spectroscopy found no intact copper arsenite, so the authors assume a "degraded form" of Scheele's or Schweinfurt green [MÄD p.102]. *Single object.* **My inference:** since 1808 predates Schweinfurt green (1814), this would be Scheele's green unless the layer is a later addition.
- **Rinmann's green, rare.** Found by the Doerner Institut on *Riesengebirgslandschaft mit aufsteigendem Nebel* (c.1819/20) [PIN], by SPSG on *Ländliche ebene Gegend* (c.1822/23) and in Dresden on *Hünengrab im Herbst* (c.1820) [MÄD p.102 n.3; CDF-H]. The SPSG/Hannover study says his mixed greens were supplemented by "the rarely detectable Rinmann's green and a copper green" [MOST-abs].
- **Unverified (search-tool summary of the Most 2024 PDF, which I could not open; page not seen).** In *Ländliche ebene Gegend*, Rinmann's green reportedly lies in a light, pastose top layer applied in short dabs or zigzags over a yellow-green layer. An arsenic-free copper green reportedly appears in the green reflections of boats in *Ansicht eines Hafens*. Check these before use.
- **Berlin, four paintings.** XRF covered only blue and yellow areas. "No Cu was detected in any investigated area" means blue areas only, not greens [ALF pp.346, 348].
- **A cautionary misattribution.** *Landschaft mit kahlem Baum* was de-attributed partly because its leaves are cobalt blue mixed with chrome yellow, which were anachronistic for 1798/99 [MÄD p.102]. The pigment date rule works as a real test.
- **No green data found** for *Hill and Ploughed Field*, *Meadows near Greifswald*, *Morning in the Riesengebirge*, *Chalk Cliffs*, *Lone Tree* or *Summer*. The ZKK 2024–25 issues are paywalled; page not seen.

### How he painted foliage and grass

- **Order.** Trees were painted "on the already painted sky" [ALF p.346]. Grass came last, as "fine upturning" strokes over the finished snow [NG p.56].
- **Strokes.** Firs are "short, hatched strokes" [NG pp.49–50]. The SPSG study notes that he modeled paint while it was still wet ("die noch nicht getrocknete Farbe modellierte") [MOST-abs]. In *Woman before the Setting Sun* the grass blades are straight, made with "a stiff, inflexible paint brush" and probably applied last. The author calls them "entirely unique in Friedrich's oeuvre" [UCL pp.116–117].
- **Drawing habits.** His foliage drawings use "small strokes and hooks" and "jagged lines." He noted distance and viewpoint on the sheet [KH, publisher excerpt; exact page uncertain]. He studied grasses and plants in dated pencil sketches (1799, 1806–07) [KSW].
- **Contemporaries.** Carus mocked painters who "confound all the specific forms of tree growth in an imaginary substance that they call 'foliage'" [CAR p.137]. Field's rule for landscape greens was to mix them from the yellows of the foreground and the blues of the sky, because they "harmonize better… and impart homogeneity" [FIELD p.128]. For Goethe, green is yellow and blue balanced, a color on which "the eye and the mind repose" [GOE §802].

### Recommendation for the simulator palette

**Early (pre-1820).** Add **Prussian blue** (S: "sehr häufig" in greens [MÄD p.102]; "possibly" in grass [NG p.56]) and **green earth** (S [MÄD p.102], dates unassigned). By default, build greens by mixing: smalt or Prussian blue + ochre or Naples yellow + bone black [NG p.56]. **Optional:** a "copper green" tube, flagged rare.

**Post-1820.** Keep the early additions. Cobalt blue + chrome yellow mixes become possible with tubes you already have. Add **Rinmann's green** as a rare tube (three objects, c.1819–1823).

**Leave out:**
- Viridian (1859/1863) and chromium oxide (1845+) [AP3 p.275]
- Emerald green as a standard tube: it was secret until 1822 and poor in oil [AP3 p.222; FIELD p.130]. Keep at most a "degraded Cu-As green" special case (single object)
- A ready-made *Zinnobergrün* tube: there is no Friedrich evidence, so mix it instead
- Sap green: "totally useless" in oil [FIELD p.131]

Rendering: allow up to four stacked green layers [MÄD p.102]. Paint foliage after the sky and grass last.

**In the simulator** (`Palette::friedrich_early_greens`, `friedrich_1820_greens`, `copper_green`; notes/green.md): Prussian blue and green earth join both periods, Rinmann's green the post-1820 one, copper green as an optional rare tube. The base palettes are kept without them (the aimed search picked Prussian blue for skies).
