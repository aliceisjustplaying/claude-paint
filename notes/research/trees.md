# Trees in Friedrich's world: how they are built and how he used them

*Sourced note for painters who work from knowledge, not pictures. Companion to `friedrich_materials.md` (materials) and `friedrich_process.md` (process). Every claim carries a source key in brackets; full URLs are in "Sources" at the end. "General knowledge" marks botanical statements I could not tie to a source I read. "My inference" marks conclusions I drew from sourced facts. "Unverified" marks claims I saw only secondhand. Rule kept throughout: no picture of his was looked at; descriptions of his works come from museum, catalog and scholarly text only.*

## Bottom line

1. **A tree is a branching system that grows at its tips and thickens everywhere.** Strong side branches arise mostly near the top of each year's shoot (acrotony). Upright axes build the frame; side axes spread sideways. When a tip dies, the one or more side shoots below it take over, so the axis forks or zigzags [BC pp.381–387].
2. **Diameter is conserved across forks, roughly.** The cross-section below a fork about equals the sum of the cross-sections above it (Leonardo's rule). The measured exponent is 1.8–2.3 instead of exactly 2 [ELOY p.1; MT p.1]. Every twig is the thin end of a continuous path to the roots [LEH].
3. **A side branch is thickest where it leaves its parent.** It is set into the trunk as a sleeve: the branch's own wood turns down and wraps its base as a collar, and trunk wood wraps over that collar every year [SHIGO]. A swelling underneath (the branch collar) and usually a raised ridge of bark on top (the branch bark ridge) mark the junction [USDA p.10].
4. **Healthy crowns end in a network of long shoots; stressed ones don't.** Roloff's four vitality stages run from a net of long shoots at the crown edge (healthy) to spiky "bottle-brush" tips, then bushy chains of short shoots, then dieback and a shrinking crown [ROL17]. He rates each stage against the tree's age, and asks when the short-shoot stage is simply normal in old oaks [ROL17].
5. **Old trees shrink downward rather than simply dying.** An ancient oak loses its upper crown and keeps a live, often dense lower crown. It becomes "stag-headed," with dead limbs standing above the living foliage, and usually hollow [ATF; HTC; WT].
6. **A pollard willow is a thick trunk cut at 2–4 m with a swollen knuckle (the "head") on top.** Many rods sprout from just below the old cut [DVL]. On a vigorous tree they can grow 1.5–2.5 m in a year [LOGAN]. Rods were harvested every one to ten years, depending on use. Pollards left uncut too long split apart under their heavy limbs [DVL; BL].
7. **Snow sticks best near 0 °C.** Adhesion rises as the air warms toward freezing, and cold dry snow is shaken off bare trees [MIL64 pp.5–6]. Snow lies in bands on smooth bark and in heaps on rough bark [MIL64 p.6]. It slides off sloping or bent supports and lets go first where it touches the branch [MIL66]. Rime (Raueis) grows into the wind; hoarfrost (Raureif) is fragile needles formed in calm, humid cold [DWD].
8. **Friedrich built his trees from dated pencil studies of individual trees, reused for decades.** Roughly 400 of about 1300 drawings put trees at the center, mostly oaks and spruces [ROL]. One 1809 oak study served paintings from 1811 to about 1835 [MET PDF p.37]. He drew with a horizon line marked even on the lower trunk and kept that viewpoint in the painting [BUSCH-V pp.77–78].
9. **Pollard willows:** yes, in at least one painting; he also made several willow studies. *The Hut in the Snow* (1827) shows willows that one scholar calls "pollard willows" [UCL p.26]. The museum text describes their "vigorous young shoots" [CDF-HÜT]. Willow studies are dated 1799, 1808, 1809 and 1812. One tree biologist reports that pictures of lopped trees are nearly absent from his work [ROL]. Details in §5.6.

**Terms used below.** *Acrotony*: side branches strongest near the tip of the parent shoot. *Orthotropic/plagiotropic*: growing upright/growing sideways. *Long shoot/short shoot*: a year's extension that is long and carries branches versus a stubby one. *Epicormic shoot*: a shoot from a dormant bud on an old trunk or limb (German *Wasserreiser*). *Reiteration*: the tree repeating its own basic architecture as a new sub-crown [BC pp.390–391]. *Retrenchment*: natural shrinking of an old crown [ATF]. *Stag-headed*: dead top limbs standing above a live lower crown [HTC]. *Pollard*: tree cut back repeatedly at head height. *Bolling/head*: the permanent trunk and knuckle of a pollard. *Rods*: the shoots from the head. *Cladoptosis*: a tree's regular shedding of twigs at an abscission layer [WP-CLAD]. *Sapwood/heartwood*: conducting outer wood/non-conducting inner wood. *Raureif/Raueis*: hoarfrost/rime.

## 1. How a tree is built

- **Growth rules.** Branches that develop mostly in the distal part of a parent shoot (acrotony) are what give a tree its tree-like rather than bushy habit [BC pp.383–384]. Upright (orthotropic) axes "are generally associated with plant skeleton construction." Sideways (plagiotropic) axes explore horizontal space and carry leaves and flowers [BC p.385]. If a shoot's tip dies, aborts or turns into a flower, one, two or more branches take over below it (sympodial branching). A string of such takeovers can still look like one straight axis [BC pp.381–382].
- **Branch order.** The trunk (order 1) bears branches (order 2), which bear their own (order 3), and so on. In sympodial systems the order climbs fast [BC p.382]. **General knowledge:** a mature broadleaved crown commonly has five or more orders from trunk to current-year twig. I found no source that counts them species by species.
- **The tree repeats itself.** As a crown matures, the tree builds new sub-crowns that duplicate its own architecture on big limbs. This "sequential" reiteration is a normal part of crown construction [BC p.391]. After damage, dormant buds produce "traumatic" reiteration: new sub-crowns on the wounded frame [BC p.390].
- **Long shoots and short shoots set the crown edge.** In a vigorous tree the leader and some side shoots at the top are long shoots, making a "network structure" that extends the crown. Next comes a "spikey outline," with bottle-brush tips of long leaders carrying only short side shoots. Then a "short shoot-mode" with "bushy structure, on hold." Last is "retraction mode": "leader die-back, crown reduction" [ROL17]. Roloff measured these stages on 100- to 160-year-old beech [ROL17]. He sorts species by life expectancy. Short-lived (80–100 years) includes alder and birch. Medium-lived (150–300) includes beech, spruce, pine and white willow. Long-lived (over 400) includes lime and oak [ROL17].
- **What distress looks like.** In an ancient tree the outer crown shrinks episodically, and new epicormic shoots grow in the mid and lower crown [WT]. Old trees survive through epicormic growth, adventitious rooting, layering and suckering [WT]. *My inference:* a crown whose every limb is covered in straight upright shoots reads as a tree reacting to heavy damage or cutting. A healthy mature crown edge is a fine net of long shoots.

## 2. How branches taper and join

- **Leonardo's rule.** Leonardo wrote that "all the branches of a tree at every stage of its height when put together are equal in thickness to the trunk." In modern terms, a mother branch of diameter *d* splitting into daughters *dᵢ* obeys *d*^Δ = Σ *dᵢ*^Δ on average. Measured Δ is between 1.8 and 2.3 for many species (Δ = 2 means cross-sectional area is conserved) [ELOY p.1]. *Arithmetic from the rule:* with Δ = 2, two equal daughters are each about 0.71 of the mother's diameter. A daughter with a quarter of the area has half the diameter, and the mother continuing past it drops only to about 0.87 of its former diameter.
- **Why: two explanations.** The *pipe model* (Shinozaki et al. 1964) pictures a tree as a bundle of unit pipes, each connecting a set of leaves to the roots. So the stem area at a height tracks the leaves above it [LEH]. Below the crown, the pipes of branches long since shed stay in the trunk as "disused pipes." That is why trunks keep thickening toward the base even where no branches remain [LEH, quoting Shinozaki]. Eloy objects that the conducting sapwood can be as little as 5% of a mature trunk's cross-section [ELOY p.1]. He shows instead that Leonardo's rule follows if a self-similar tree skeleton sizes its diameters to resist wind [ELOY]. Whatever the cause, cross-sections sum roughly at forks [LEH; MT].
- **When the rule bends.** Models depart from the rule as side branches get heavier or leave at wider angles. In real branches the departure is small. Beech branches fit a constant-stress design best; fir branches often follow Leonardo's rule [MT p.1]. *My inference:* a heavy, near-horizontal limb is somewhat thicker at its base than the area rule alone would give.
- **What a junction is.** The cambium runs continuously from trunk to branch. In the upper junction, though, its cells are oriented at "approximately right angles" to the normal direction. Early in the season the branch xylem turns downward at the branch base and "encircles it to form a collar." Later in the season a collar of trunk xylem envelops it. "The branch is structurally attached to the trunk by a series of trunk collars that envelop the branch collars every growing season." Water enters and leaves the branch through the branch collar [SHIGO]. Outwardly there is a branch collar on the underside of the branch base and a branch bark ridge on top, running roughly parallel to the branch angle [USDA p.10].
- **Why a side branch never starts thinner than it is farther out.** *My inference, from [LEH] and [SHIGO]:* at its base a branch carries the pipes for every leaf beyond it, and each year's collar adds wood there first. Its cross-section therefore shrinks outward, stepwise at each fork and gradually between forks, and never the other way. A branch drawn as a constant-width rod, or narrower where it leaves the trunk, contradicts both.
- **Angle of attachment.** Wide, U-shaped unions are strong. Narrow V-shaped unions "often form included bark," a wedge of inward-rolled bark between two stems. Codominant stems of about equal size from one point often do the same [USDA p.6]. When a leader is damaged, several branches may become codominant [USDA p.7].
- **Dead branches and stubs.** Around a dead branch, the collar and branch bark ridge "continue to grow" and can be told apart from the dead wood [USDA p.13]. *General knowledge:* over years a living collar can form a sleeve around a dead stub.
- **Twigs end in buds.** Each twig is the current year's shoot and ends in a terminal or false-terminal bud. Buds sit in the leaf scars along it (species details in §3) [VT]. Nothing in a tree floats. Every twig connects through its parent to the trunk, as the pipe model presumes [LEH].

## 3. The species

### 3.1 Oak (*Quercus robur*, pedunculate; *Q. petraea*, sessile)

- **Habit.** In *Q. robur* the main trunk "tends to disappear in the crown, developing irregular boughs with twisting branches." *Q. petraea* usually keeps a main stem with boughs that gradually decrease in size [JRC-Q]. Heights are usually about 30 m, with diameters up to 1 m. Individuals can live over 1000 years and reach 3–4 m in diameter [JRC-Q]. The crown is round [VT oak].
- **Twigs and buds.** Twigs are moderate in thickness and yellow-brown. Buds are "angled, short and round" [VT oak]. **General knowledge:** oak buds cluster at the shoot tip, so several shoots start from one point, which gives oak its crooked, clustered branching.
- **Twig shedding (cladoptosis).** In cladoptosis an abscission layer forms and the twig "is shed cleanly," as leaves are [WP-CLAD]. In oak, most shed twigs are one year old and leafless. They fall in autumn and mostly come from vigorously growing trees, so the shedding is not a sign of drought or age [RUST]. In a 165-year-old stand, weak shoots had 27% lower hydraulic conductance, with bottlenecks at nodes and abscission zones [RR02].
- **Bark.** Gray and fissured into rectangular elongated blocks, thicker in *Q. robur* [JRC-Q]. "Deeply fissured with age" [VT oak].
- **Old and damaged oaks.** Oaks coppice and pollard easily. Lateral epicormic shoots leave knots in the wood [JRC-Q]. Ancient oaks retrench: the crown shrinks through dieback and branch loss ("growing downwards"). The trunk is very wide for the species and very likely hollow, and the tree may live on for centuries [ATF]. "Stagheaded oaks are classic examples of early stage retrenchment" [HTC]. In natural retrenchment, single branches die back while the rest of the crown stays healthy. Serious disease looks different [ATF].
- **Winter and leaf.** A museum text on Friedrich's *Oak Tree in Snow* (1829) describes the tree still carrying the previous year's brown leaves, which it drops only when new leaves grow in spring [CDF-EICH]. **General knowledge:** young and lower oak branches often keep dead leaves through winter (marcescence); old crowns do so less.

### 3.2 Beech (*Fagus sylvatica*)

- **Habit.** Usually single-stemmed, with silver-gray bark. Typical life span 150–300 years. It keeps growing fast into late maturity and is the most shade-tolerant broadleaf in its range [JRC-F]. A stocky trunk and round crown [VT beech].
- **Twigs and buds.** Twigs are "slender, zigzag, light brown." Buds are long (about 2.5 cm), slender and "widely divergent from stems" [VT beech]. Beech was Roloff's model species for the long-shoot/short-shoot vitality stages [ROL17].
- **Bark.** "Smooth, thin, and dark gray-blue … smooth even on the largest stems, may be mottled" [VT beech]. The thin bark protects poorly against fire and stripping [JRC-F].
- **Taper.** Japanese beech branches fit a uniform-stress design best [MT p.1].

### 3.3 Birch (*Betula pendula*, silver; *B. pubescens*, downy)

- **Habit.** Short-lived (commonly 90–100 years, rarely 150), relatively small, with a light canopy [JRC-B]. The crown is open and pyramidal, "with distinctly drooping fine branches" [VT birch]. In *B. pendula* the branches droop; in *B. pubescens* they grow upward or horizontally [JRC-B].
- **Twigs.** "Slender, reddish brown … noticeably drooping," with slender pointed buds [VT birch].
- **Bark.** Brown when young, then "silvery-white, with horizontal dark grey lenticels, that with age darkens and develops fissures." Young trees show dark scars where shoots dropped [JRC-B]. At the base the tree "develops thick bark which splits into narrow, vertical furrows which are nearly black" [VT birch].

### 3.4 Lime (*Tilia cordata*, small-leaved; *T. platyphyllos*, large-leaved)

- **Habit.** 30–40 m, with straight trunks up to about 1 m. Crowns are "quite neat and narrow, becoming untidier as they age," with a branch-free trunk of 10–15 m possible. Both species are largely free of suckers and epicormic growth, unlike the hybrid common lime [JRC-T]. Open-grown trees are dense and round, and become as wide as tall [VT lime].
- **Twigs and buds.** "Slender, zigzag, green-brown or red-tinged (particularly in the winter)." The terminal bud is false; buds are plump with one side bulging [VT lime].
- **Bark.** Gray or brown, with shallow furrows and flat ridges [VT lime]. "Numerous burls and sprouts often found around the base" [VT lime].
- **Old limes.** They live over 1000 years even when coppiced. Very old stools may have lost their heartwood to rot [JRC-T]. Limes were coppiced for long straight poles [JRC-T] and can be pollarded [BL].

### 3.5 Willow (white willow, *Salix alba*; other tree willows) and the pollard

- **The tree.** A fast-growing tree of riverbanks and floodplains, very tolerant of flooding. It can be pollarded or coppiced, and it "can readily reproduce from suckers or from adventitious roots" [JRC-S]. The bark is dark gray and deeply fissured with corky ridges. The narrow leaves are silver-gray, and white willow is "cultivated as osiers, characterised by having pliable branches" [JRC-S]. Medium-lived [ROL17]. Willows used as pollards include white, hybrid crack (*S. rubens*), crack (*S. fragilis*) and basket (*S. viminalis*) willow [DVL; NM].
- **How a pollard is made.** The trunk is cut through at 2 to 4 m. The tree then resprouts "vieltriebig" (in many shoots) from buds just below the cut, and repeated cutting thickens that place into the "head" [DVL]. While the tree is being trained, all side shoots on the trunk are removed except in the top 10–20 cm, and the head grows from those [NM]. In the 2nd to 4th year, 40–60% of the new shoots are cut back to about 5 cm, mostly the suppressed ones [DVL].
- **Rods: age, use and number.** Traditionally the thin rods were taken one to two years after cutting, for baskets, fish traps and house building. The rest were thinned after three to five years for tool handles. Whatever remained was left until about the tenth year for firewood, and then the whole head was cut again [DVL]. Tall pollards are cut every 3–5 years and carry "a gnarled head and thick, several-year-old branches." Low basket willows are cut every year [BL]. Old pollards should be cut only about every 5 years, and a cut is overdue once the branches are 10 cm thick [BL]. Poles over 5 cm thick are heavy [DVL]. **Number of rods:** I found no source that gives a count per head. The sources say "many shoots" and describe thinning by half [DVL]. **Growth:** "on a vigorous willow five to eight feet of growth in a year is not uncommon," and cut sprouts are replaced in the same year [LOGAN].
- **How rods stand and bend.** No source I read describes rod angles. *General knowledge:* new rods on a pollard head grow nearly vertical at first. As they lengthen and age over several years they lean outward under their own weight, so old uncut heads spread. White willow's "pliable branches" [JRC-S] bend under load without breaking. Logan describes Somerset pollards as "like shocks of hair, others like a sack thrown over the trunk's back, and some like rows of Corinthian columns" [LOGAN].
- **Old pollards.** Heads develop holes, niches and cavities used by owls, other birds and bats. Trunks become hollow [DVL; BL]. Old pollards left uncut "threaten to break apart," because the trunk, especially a hollow one, can no longer carry the heavy branches [DVL].

### 3.6 Alder (*Alnus glutinosa*, black alder)

- **Habit.** Short-lived (normally about 60 years, maximum about 160), 10–25 m, on riverbanks, lake shores and marshes [JRC-A]. The crown is narrow and upright [VT alder]. Shaded branches die off, so the live crown shrinks relative to the tree [JRC-A]. It can be coppiced [JRC-A].
- **Twigs and winter features.** Young twigs are green and sticky. Buds are stalked, purplish brown and somewhat three-sided [VT alder]. Catkins form in autumn and open the next spring. The fruits are small woody "cones" that stay on through winter and release seed gradually [JRC-A; VT alder].
- **Bark.** Brown and smooth at first, then darker, rough and fissured [JRC-A]. Later gray-brown with small broken patches [VT alder].
- **Damage.** *Phytophthora* disease kills first the fine branches, then the larger ones [JRC-A].

### 3.7 Norway spruce (*Picea abies*)

- **Habit.** A straight, regular trunk up to 50–60 m, usually living 200–300 years [JRC-PA]. "The crown is regularly conic, columnar, with whorled, short and stout branches, the upper level ascending and the lower drooping" [JRC-PA]. Free-standing trees keep green branches down to the ground [WP-FI]. Branches are inclined to hanging, as an adaptation to snow load [WP-FI].
- **Branching types.** Young spruces branch in flat "plates" up to about 20 years. After that they develop either the *Plattenfichte* type, with horizontal second-order branches, or the *Kammfichte* ("comb spruce") type, whose second- and higher-order branches hang down like a fringe. A third type is the *Bürstenfichte* ("brush spruce") [WP-FI]. Comb spruces dominate on well-supplied sites. Plate spruces dominate on poor bogs and at extreme altitudes [WP-FI].
- **Bark.** Orange-brown [JRC-PA]. Red-brown and scaly, later gray with flaking scales or plates [VT spruce]. Drops of resin are common on trunk and branches [JRC-PA].
- **Spruce and fir.** A Hamburg catalog entry notes that spruce and fir are hard to tell apart even in nature, "all the more so painted in oil," and that Friedrich's *Early Snow* shows both [CDF-FRÜH]. Roloff finds that Friedrich usually distinguished them, but not always [ROL].

### 3.8 Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*)

- **Habit.** 23–27 m on average, sometimes over 40 m. It lives 400 years or more [JRC-PS]. Crowns are rounded when young and round-topped when old. The trunk is often twisted, depending on seed origin [VT pine]. In some conifers, flattening and broadening of the crown may be the only sign of old-age retrenchment [ATF].
- **Bark.** The upper stem is "a distinct reddish-orange," while the lower part is "furrowed brown to grey-brown and becomes deeply fissured" [JRC-PS]. "Upper crowns always show orange peeling bark" [VT pine].
- **Needles.** In pairs, twisted [VT pine]. Friedrich drew pine twigs with the paired needles botanically correct [ROL].

## 4. Snow and hoarfrost on bare trees

- **Bare trees do hold snow.** "Winter bareness of hardwoods may deceptively lead one to the assumption that they intercept no snow, when this is not true." Sheltered deciduous trees that are overtopped and out of the wind can hold snow on branches and forks as deep as on conifers, 20 cm [MIL64 p.7]. Measured over whole stands, deciduous forests kept up to 14% (average 11%) of snowfall, against an average 43% in conifer stands [HEL20 p.2557].
- **Temperature decides.** "Adhesion of snow to foliage presumably increases as temperature rises toward the freezing point." One Japanese series found interception rising about 30% of snowfall per degree Celsius of warming just below freezing [MIL64 p.5]. Storms in which a tree held little snow were cold, below −3 °C. "Dry snow in strong winds is generally intercepted less than wet snow" [MIL64 p.6]. Little dry snow stays in deciduous trees because they vibrate more, not because snow fails to hit them [MIL64 p.6]. *Unverified (abstract known only from secondary summaries):* on boards, catch below −3 °C rose with board width because crystals bounce off near edges. Above −3 °C cohesion made width irrelevant, and wet snow bridged gaps of 10 cm; board inclination and shape mattered [PS99].
- **Mechanisms.** Snow bridges between supports by cohesion, bounces off by elastic rebound, and is shed as branches bend. Light fluffy snow is caught best; catch falls as the snow gets denser [SG91].
- **Where it lies on a branch.** Bark texture matters: "snow forms in bands on deciduous trees if bark is smooth, in heaps if it is rough" [MIL64 p.6]. Wet snow driven by gales built up on the windward and upper halves of crowns [MIL64 p.3]. *My inference, from the above:* snow sits as a ridge along the upper side of limbs that are near horizontal and thick enough to stop bounce. Forks and crotches collect it. Steep limbs and thin, springy twigs hold little except in wet snow near 0 °C, which can bridge between twigs.
- **What falls off, and when.** Snow slides off branches "if the branches provide an unstable or slanting support." Loads balanced on branch ends bend them into steep slopes, which then release. Slight warming melts snow first at "the points of attachment to their supports," so masses slide off before they melt away [MIL66]. Wind shakes crowns, and deciduous trees lose snow mostly to that shaking, not to direct wind [MIL66].
- **Hoarfrost and rime (DWD definitions).** *Raureif* (hoarfrost) is "mostly thin, only loosely attached and fragile ice needles or scales," formed almost entirely by deposition from vapor. It needs humidity around 90% or more, weak wind and usually temperatures below −8 °C [DWD]. Its crystals are like snowflakes under a lens, often needle-shaped [DWD-T]. *Raueis* (rime) is a granular, "very firmly attached" deposit of supercooled fog droplets, milky white from trapped air. It forms mainly in fog at −2 to −10 °C, and strong wind favors it [DWD]. Its "ice flags" point into the wind [DWD-T]. *Klareis* (clear ice) forms at 0 to −3 °C [DWD]. Fog-frost deposits "show their typical growth against the wind," mostly on near-vertical surfaces, and can grow into heavy loads [DWD]. Deposits on tree branches often fall to the ground below [DWD-T].
- **Friedrich on snow.** He called snow "the great white cloth, the epitome of the highest purity, under which nature prepares for a new life" (cited from his letters in a museum text) [CDF-EICH].

## 5. How Friedrich studied and used trees

### 5.1 How much, and which trees

- Of about 1300 drawings and studies, about 400 have trees at the center or use them for expression. The arborist Roloff went through all of them for the Dresden museums [ROL]. Friedrich began tree studies in 1797, and they became very detailed from 1804. Oaks and spruces dominate, and he "hardly" drew limes [ROL]. In pine, spruce and fir studies he drew "twig by twig, in part even needle by needle" [ROL].
- The Berlin museum: "Friedrich gave no tree as much attention as the oak." He drew particular characterful trees precisely from nature and then used them several times in different pictures. Most stand around Gothic ruins, graveyards or dolmens, and it is often late autumn or winter. The trees are marked by storms or lightning, and their age shows in dead branches [CDF-EICH].
- Grummt assigns 404 of 1014 attributed sheets to 17 sketchbooks [KH p.47].

### 5.2 How he drew trees outdoors

- **Kit and habits.** He carried pencil, paper and eraser [KH p.47]. He wrote "in der Ferne" (in the distance) beside each trunk of two distant pines, drawn "by small strokes and hooks in a style familiar from many later drawings" [KH p.47]. On 20 April 1799 he traced "the intricate ramifications of the branches" of a leafless tree on a slope "down to the smallest detail." The shaded, branchless side of the trunk is given only by its "sinuous outline" [KH p.48]. A 1797 oak "after nature" has foliage "outlined in jagged lines" and coarse hatching [KH p.48]. A soft-pencil 1806 oak is built of parallel hatching with almost no modeling of volume [CDF-STORCH].
- **Viewpoint recorded.** He marked the horizon line "even in studies of tangled roots" and even on the lower part of a tree trunk. That means he drew sitting on the ground, and "whenever he translated a drawing into a painting, he consistently retained the perspective and spatial relationships recorded in the drawing" [BUSCH-V pp.77–78]. Without a horizon line he wrote "unten" or "von unten" (from below) [BUSCH-V p.78]. He also noted time of day and the sun's position [BUSCH-V p.78]. Busch links this to Valenciennes (German edition 1803): foliage below the horizon shows its top, foliage above it shows its underside [BUSCH-V p.78]. Valenciennes also advised noticing "all the little things about the bark, the moss, the roots, the sweep of the branches" [KÖR p.285].
- **Reworking.** He reworked pencil studies, mostly in the studio, with pen or brush in carbon or iron-gall ink and brown washes [KH p.48]. He kept to pencil outdoors all his life, with written color notes; only three sky oil sketches of his survive [CDF-ABEND].

The Solitary Tree's oak is described as a composite of studies made between 1806 and 1810 [CDF-EIN; MET PDF p.40]. The bare oak in winter recurs in *Dolmen in the Snow* (1807), *Winter* (1808, burned 1931), *Abbey in the Oakwood*, *Monastery Graveyard in the Snow* (1819, lost) and *Oak in the Snow* (Cologne, 1827). *Oak Tree in Snow* (Berlin, 1829) is the most concentrated version [CDF-EICH]. For *Abbey* he wrote in 1809: "the oaks stretch their arms upward out of the fog, while below they have already quite vanished" [WP-ABT, quoting the letters].

### 5.4 Which paintings have which trees (from text)

- **Oaks.** *Dolmen in the Snow* (1807): three leafless oaks; the two upright ones have broken-out crowns, and the third has been bent by wind [WP-HÜN]. *Abbey in the Oakwood*: leafless oaks "with lopped branches," four on each side of the ruin [WP-ABT]. *Winter Landscape* (Schwerin, 1811): the "twisted trunks of two dead oak trees" and rows of stumps. Its London pendant sets "erect forms of the young fir trees" against "the tangled, disordered limbs of the dead oaks" [NG p.44]. *The Solitary Tree* (1822): a great oak whose top branches are dead [CDF-EIN]. *Two Men Contemplating the Moon* (Dresden, 1819): an uprooted, moss-covered oak held by a boulder, a spruce, a cut stump and a dead branch on the ground [WP-ZM]. In the Berlin variant the oak has "a much denser network of branches, and writhing, moss-covered roots." In the New York variant some green leaves remain on the oak [MET PDF pp.17, 19–20, 34]. *Oak Tree in Snow* (1829): an old oak, crownless and probably dead at the top, with gnarled, bent branches and "filigree twigs," and remains of a fallen trunk before it [CDF-EICH].
- **Spruce and fir.** *The Chasseur in the Forest* (c.1813): spruces with red-brown trunks, groups of young firs dusted with snow and two sawn stumps [CDF-CHASS]. *Early Snow* (1828): firs lining a path, a wall of spruces and one gently snow-covered spruce on the center line [CDF-FRÜH]. *Spruce Thicket in the Snow* (Munich) is the pendant of the Dresden *Trees and Bushes in the Snow* [CDF-GEB].
- **Bushes.** A reviewer of *Trees and Bushes in the Snow* (1828) wrote of "a group of shrubbery that crosses and intertwines leaflessly, rendered with the utmost truth to nature" [CDF-GEB].
- **Birch.** A late moonlit transparency shows moonlight on "the white trunk of the birch tree," the other trees making "dark filigree" [MET PDF p.44].
- **Willow.** See §5.6.
- **Beech, lime, alder.** A beech study fed *The Solitary Tree* [WP-EIN]. Lime studies of April–May 1808 survive [SKD-W], but Roloff says he hardly drew limes [ROL]. For alder I found no text naming it in any painting or study; evidence either way is missing.

### 5.5 How he painted them, according to conservators and scholars

- **Underdrawing includes the trees.** In the London *Winter Landscape* "an underdrawing for the rocks and trees is also visible." The painted layers "obey the outlines of the underdrawing," with pentimenti only "at the left and right-hand edge of the largest fir tree" [NG p.49]. In the unfinished *Entrance to a Graveyard*, infrared shows "the fluid drawing of foliage" in pen. He apparently drew the whole composition in pencil and then went over all of it in ink, "including fine details such as the grass and delicate leaf structure" [NG p.58].
- **Order.** In *Forest Interior by Moonlight* (1823/30), XRF maps show "the paint of the trees was applied on the already painted sky" [ALF p.346].
- **Strokes.** The firs of the London picture "are thinly painted with short, hatched strokes" [NG p.50]. In the New York *Two Men Contemplating the Moon* the forms are "less detailed … rendered with a fluid, assured brush" that needed no underdrawing [MET PDF pp.19–20].
- **Brushwork of bare twigs.** I found no technical text that describes how he painted fine bare twigs, such as brush type, stroke direction or wet versus dry paint. This is a gap.
- **Damage as he drew it.** Roloff notes that from 1806/10 on, his old oaks often have dead tops. Many limbs up to 25 m high look "sawn off" with smooth cuts, some 30–40 cm thick. That was hardly possible with the saws and climbing of the time, and no other Romantic painter shows so many "mutilated" trees. He reads it as artistic invention, not as a record of forestry [ROL]. The Berlin museum reads the dead branches as signs of great age [CDF-EICH].
- **Contemporary standard.** Carus, his friend, faulted painters who cannot see "the differences of outline between different species of trees" [CAR p.124]. He also faulted those who "confound all the specific forms of tree growth in an imaginary substance that they call 'foliage'" [CAR p.137].

### 5.6 Did he paint pollard willows?

**Yes, in at least one painting that the sources name, and he studied willows repeatedly. The motif is rare in his work.**

- *The Hut in the Snow* (*Verschneite Hütte*, 1827, 31 × 25 cm, Berlin). The museum text says the young shoots of "the willows behind" it are "vigorous" (*kräftigen jungen Trieben*) and herald spring [CDF-HÜT]. German Wikipedia, citing Hannesen 1985, calls them *Korbweiden* (basket willows) [WP-HÜT]. Clinger's UCL thesis names "pollard willows" in the picture and describes "the last season's willow growth scrambling upwards, their rise silhouetted against the murky winter's sky" [UCL p.26].
- Clinger also finds "pollard willows" on a floodplain, inclined diagonally, in a small *Winterlandschaft* (1827, 21 × 30 cm, private collection, Zittau). Its central trees are "nearly identical to those behind the Hut in the Snow" [UCL pp.56, 59]. That is a single scholar's identification.
- *Willow Bush under a Low Sun* (c.1832/35, Frankfurt): two willows "with burst trunks" grow from a thicket of twigs and undergrowth, with few leaves left [FDH-MD]. The catalog does not call them pollards.
- **Willow studies:** *Stump of a Willow with New Shoots*, 12 August 1799, pen and pencil (Berlin) [CDF-STUMPF]. A willow noted "ganz grau" (quite gray), 21 April 1808 (Dresden) [SKD-W]. *Study of a Willow, Study of Two Branches*, 18 April/2 June 1809 (Berlin) [CDF-WEIDE; KH caption]. *Willow Struck by Lightning*, 19 March 1812 (Prague) [BUSCH-V fig.3]. A willow stands behind a figure group in a 1799 design [CDF-KRIEB]. Whether the 1799 "stump with new shoots" is a lopped pollard is not stated. The title fits one, but that is my reading.
- **Against frequency:** Roloff, who went through all his drawings, writes that lopping (*Schneitelung*) and limbing of the lower trunk were occasionally practiced then, but "there are almost no pictures of it by CDF," though more by other painters [ROL].

## Gaps

- No source gives the number of rods on a willow pollard head or measured rod angles. The same goes for per-species branching angles and the number of branch orders.
- Pfister and Schneebeli (1999) on snow and board width was read only through secondary summaries [PS99].
- No technical study describes Friedrich's brushwork for bare twigs. Grummt's catalogue raisonné (2011), which would list every willow and tree study, was not accessible; I relied on museum records and the SKD 2024 catalog excerpt.
- The *Winterlandschaft* (1827, Zittau) pollards rest on one thesis.
