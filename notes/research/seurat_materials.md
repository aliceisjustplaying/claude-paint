# Seurat: materials and method

*A sourced technical report, researched 10 October 2026. [NG24] is the main
technical evidence, not a pigment list inferred from reproductions. Source keys
are listed below. “Interpretation” means a proposed reading or studio decision;
“uncertain” means the evidence does not establish the claim. Research used text,
not reference images.*

## What is documented, and for which works

Kirby and colleagues examined fifteen oil sketches and four finished paintings
in the National Gallery and Courtauld collections; they examined *Le Bec du Hoc*
visually without sampling it. Their survey spans about 1882–90, but is not a
complete analysis of every Seurat. Its tables separate support, medium and
pigment findings by painting [NG24 pp.5–9, Tables 1–3]. Do not turn their combined
findings into one palette used unchanged throughout his career.

## 1. Drawing, studies, support and ground

- **Tonal drawing.** Conté crayon on textured, laid Michallet paper catches the
  tooth and leaves minute areas of paper uncovered. In the study *L'écho*, forms
  are made by adjacent tonal masses, not sharp outlines [YALE]. The Met's
  *Aman-Jean* is a finished drawing, not a preparation for a painting: not every
  drawing belongs to a mandatory painting sequence [MET-D].
- **Croquetons.** The Met describes about seventy early small oil studies on
  portable wooden panels, often held in the hand outdoors [MET-S]. In [NG24],
  many are approximately 16 × 25 cm, on hardwood, sometimes tentatively called
  mahogany. Many are unprimed; some have a thin white priming. These are studies
  of motif and arrangement, often with broad strokes and figures in two or three
  touches, not miniature fully dotted paintings [NG24 Table 1, pp.19–20, 27–28].
- **Finished supports.** The examined exhibition pictures use plain-weave linen
  (the identification of the fine cloth in *Gravelines* is qualified in the
  table). *Bathers* has a commercially applied greyish-white lead-white/carbon-
  black ground. Other grounds include white lead paint, chalk and double
  preparations. All analytically examined grounds in this survey are oil-bound
  [NG24 Table 1, pp.18–19]. A pure white ground is not a universal early rule.
- **Absorbent-ground exception.** Seurat tried a glue/chalk *toile à plâtre* for
  *Les Poseuses* in 1887 and was dissatisfied. Do not make absorbent chalk/glue
  ground the default “Seurat surface” [NG24 p.19].
- **Transfer and reserves.** Grids occur in some *Grande Jatte* studies;
  *Bathers* has evidence of charcoal or Conté drawing but no detected squaring.
  X-rays establish reserves for its principal figures. Reserves also occur in
  *Bridge at Courbevoie* and other works, but could not be established in the
  densely painted *Gravelines*. Blue painted outlines occur in later sketches;
  their use in the finished pictures is uncertain [NG24 pp.19–20].

## 2. Pigments: findings rather than a shopping list

| evidence | documented findings and limits |
| --- | --- |
| Core palette across the surveyed works | Lead white; cobalt blue and synthetic French ultramarine; vermilion and red lakes; chrome and cadmium yellows; viridian and emerald green [NG24 p.21, Table 3]. These are paired pigments with different hue and mixing behavior, not redundant alternatives. |
| Early studies and *Bathers*, to about 1884 | Red/yellow earth pigments and occasional carbon/bone black occur. In *Bathers*, earths tend to be in the lower paint. Earths are also reported for the first 1884–85 phase of *Grande Jatte* [NG24 pp.21–22]. |
| Later work | Earths appear largely relinquished, **possibly excepting yellow ochre**. “No earths or black after 1885” is too categorical for this evidence [NG24 p.22]. |
| Yellows | The range includes chrome/cadmium yellows and strontium yellow. Zinc yellow occurs in later additions to *Bathers*, *Bridge at Courbevoie*, and the second campaign of *Grande Jatte*. No zinc yellow was found in the *Grande Jatte* preparatory studies discussed [NG24 pp.22–25, Table 3]. |
| Red lakes | Both madder and cochineal lakes were identified, sometimes together. An unspecified “red lake” is not proof of one exact modern tube formulation [NG24 pp.25–26, Table 3]. |
| Violet exception | Most purples are mixed from red lake and ultramarine, sometimes cobalt blue or earth red. The text identifies manganese violet in the late *Channel of Gravelines* border; its plate 27 caption also identifies cobalt violet and cadmium orange there. These are work-specific findings, not a general violet prescription [NG24 pp.21–22, Table 3]. |

Prussian blue and commercial chrome green were **not found in the works of this
survey**. Cerulean's greenish cast is discussed as an unattractive option for this
purpose; that discussion is not proof it was never used in his entire oeuvre
[NG24 p.22]. Do not add pigments solely because they were available in Paris.

**Physical mixing remained essential.** Violet shadows combine lake and blue;
greens combine viridian with white or yellow, or cobalt blue with viridian.
Almost all applications in *Bathers* incorporate lead white; the authors describe
its light, opaque appearance as a general feature of his work. Vermilion is often
tempered by red lake. “Unmixed dots” means separated color touches, not an absolute
ban on mixing paint or tinting it with white [NG24 pp.22–23, 30–31].

## 3. Oil and surface

Lead white was usually in poppy oil in the analysed samples; red lakes and
vermilion predominantly in linseed; blues sometimes in poppy; greens/yellows
usually in linseed. Some later paints contain heat-bodied oil, chiefly linseed.
The suggestion that its handling suited pointillism is the authors'
interpretation, not a statement by Seurat [NG24 pp.26–27, Table 2].

No proprietary resin-containing painting medium was detected in the paints
discussed. White-containing body paint, not a stack of transparent colored
glazes, is the main evidence here [NG24 p.31]. This does not establish that every
picture was originally unvarnished or that no transparent passage exists.

## 4. Order of work: not one universal recipe

1. **Study the masses and motif.** Drawings and oil studies precede most works;
   the outdoor study is subordinate to a planned, revised exhibition composition
   [NG24 pp.19–20; AIC].
2. **Establish drawing/reserves and thin colored masses.** Broad landscape areas
   of *Bathers* and *Grande Jatte* were brushed in with lean, appropriately colored
   paint. *Bridge* and *Bec du Hoc* also begin with thin lay-ins [NG24 pp.20–21].
3. **Work the underpainting.** In *Bathers*, interlaced colored *balayé* (sweeping)
   strokes lie over a more solid underpaint in the grass; water is horizontal,
   flesh smoother, other passages mottled. In *Bridge*, directional/criss-cross
   strokes were worked wet-in-wet before superimposed dots [NG24 pp.27–28].
4. **Separate touches, and later revision.** The late dotted phase is not the
   beginning of every picture: *Bathers* was revisited around 1886–87, and
   *Bec du Hoc* of 1885 was dotted later (reworked 1888 in Table 1). The earlier
   brushwork remains visible beneath [NG24 pp.28–29]. The 2006 *Grande Jatte*
   reconstruction study dates the divisionist campaign from October 1885 [BERNS].
5. **Border where appropriate.** Painted borders were added from 1888 onward,
   including to earlier pictures; not every early sketch needs one [NG24 note
   24]. They respond locally to neighboring colors, rather than forming one
   uniform decorative strip [NG-G].

**Uncertain:** a fixed drying interval, fixed number of layers, or universal
“balayé everywhere, then dots everywhere” order. The evidence records particular
paint structures and campaigns; it does not supply a daily studio timetable.

## 5. Dot size, spacing and density

The term *divisionism* concerns the division of color into separate touches;
*pointillism* names the dotted appearance. Neither requires identical circles.

- **Early versus late.** Early studies and the original *Bathers* use sweeps,
  hatching, mottling and smoother passages. *Grande Jatte* and *Bridge* are
  transitional; later *Young Woman powdering Herself* and *Gravelines* sustain
  discrete dots and strokes, with form determined partly by their density
  [NG24 pp.27–30].
- **Within one picture.** The woman's skirt has broader directional marks than
  the tiny touches in her face. *Gravelines* has fatter, freer foreground beach
  dashes than marks in the distance. *Bec du Hoc* diminishes both brushstroke
  size and spacing toward the point of the cliff. Its water includes short
  horizontal and tiny vertical marks, not circular beads [NG24 pp.28–29].
- **Density is a modeling choice.** Change relative amounts of colored touches
  and how much earlier paint remains visible, not simply the darkness of one
  paint. This last sentence is a studio interpretation of the documented use of
  density, not a recovered formula from Seurat.
- **No monotonic “later = smaller” rule.** The small 1888 *Poseuses* version is
  broader/looser than the large 1886–88 version. Signac criticized the latter's
  tiny background dots for making it grey [NG24 p.29].
- **Absolute dimensions are uncertain here.** The sources read do not give a
  representative measured distribution of dot diameters, nearest-neighbor
  distances or dots/cm². Magnified details have stated magnifications but are
  not a published census. Do not claim “Seurat used 2 mm dots” as a universal
  fact. Easel mark-size trials below are proposed craft, not historical data.

## 6. Color theory, optical mixing and borders

Chevreul's simultaneous contrast concerns changes in the **appearance** of a
color beside another: hue and light/dark contrast. Charles Blanc mediated these
ideas for Seurat. Whether Seurat read Chevreul's whole book is uncertain. Seurat
did copy Rood's color wheel and take notes from his *Modern Chromatics* (1879;
French translation 1881), which distinguishes pigment mixture from optical
mixture. Charles Henry's ideas on directional line and mood enter after 1886
[NG24 pp.10–16].

In his August 1890 letter to Maurice Beaubourg, as discussed in [NG24], Seurat
described harmony through related and contrary tones, colors and lines. Treat
this as his stated theory, not evidence for a geometrical algorithm hidden in
every canvas. The present note relies on the technical study's discussion, not
an independently inspected autograph letter.

The surveyed paintings favor orange–blue, orange-red–blue and green/yellow-green
against purple; simple textbook red–green and yellow–purple pairings are less
frequent. In late *Gravelines*, contrasts between light and dark shades within a
color become particularly important [NG24 pp.30–31].

**Optical mixing is not emitted-light mixing.** Adjacent touches remain actual
pigmented paint. At a distance their reflected colors integrate, while
simultaneous contrast can intensify their appearance where they remain resolved.
Very small complementary dots can also average toward grey; the technical
authors explicitly note the difference between the theory's promise and the
paintings' effect [NG24 pp.13–15, 29–30]. Do not simulate this by RGB gradients
or promise that blue and yellow dots necessarily become a brilliant green.

The *Gravelines* border is locally adjusted: orange above the blue sky, deep
indigo beside yellow sand, red touches near the lower-left green. It mediates
between picture and frame and affects the adjacent passage; it is not a black
outline or a constant complement chosen from the picture's average [NG-G].

## 7. Degradation: intention is not today's color

**Zinc yellow is the clearest work-specific warning.** In *Grande Jatte*, yellow,
yellow-green and orange touches have become ochre-like, olive green and reddish
brown [BERNS]. Alteration was noticed as early as 1892. Spectroscopic work
found Cr(III) and Cr(VI) dichromate in an altered sample; reconstructions under
light, SO₂ and different humidity produced different altered species. “It all
turns brown by one mechanism” is too simple [EELS; XANES]. [NG24 pp.24–25] also
documents altered zinc-yellow touches in *Bathers* and *Bridge*.

Red lakes have a fading risk, chromate yellows an alteration risk, and the oil
and subsequent surface history change appearance. **Uncertain at work level:**
do not assign a measured fade amount to a particular Seurat without that work's
analysis. The rejuvenated *Grande Jatte* is explicitly a simulation, not recovered
ground truth [BERNS]. For a first painting, seek the intended fresh relationships;
do not imitate browned spots as his aesthetic signature.

## 9. Easel tube mapping

This repository appendix maps §2's findings to the dedicated `seurat` box,
not to additional historical pigment findings. Lead white → lead white;
chrome yellow → chrome yellow; cadmium yellow → cadmium yellow;
strontium yellow → strontium yellow; zinc yellow → zinc yellow;
vermilion → vermilion; madder/cochineal red lakes → rose madder;
early yellow/red earths → yellow ochre and red earth;
occasional early carbon/bone black → bone black;
cobalt blue → cobalt blue; synthetic French ultramarine → ultramarine blue;
viridian → viridian; emerald green → emerald green.

The earth and black choices support early studies, not a prescription for
late work. The lakes use one practical proxy rather than an exact analysed
batch. The work-specific Gravelines exceptions are outside this box's
core-and-early-study remit; the method note declares mixed violet as an
approximation where needed. See `notes/r20/TUBES.md` for the box decision.
