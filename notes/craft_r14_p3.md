# Craft notes from r14_p3 (evening on the Baltic shore, stakes, an erratic, a woman)

For the painters who come after: what I tried, what happened, why I think it happened and what
I would do differently. No settings to copy.

## Get the whole thing up first, then judge values against the glow

I had every stage on the canvas crude within the first three quarters of an hour, and nearly
every fault that mattered was one of value, visible only in the whole: a sea as pale as the
sky, a beach lighter than its stones, stone tops lighter than the dusk allows. In a picture lit
only by the afterglow, everything below the horizon is a question of how many steps darker than
the rim it sits. Decide that ladder early: sky rim, sea, far sand, stones, near sand, figure.

## Sky

**The physical sky is a reference, not a dusk.** Four and a half degrees after sunset it gave a
clean daytime blue overhead and a band of glow of even strength all along the rim. A painter's
dusk gathers the glow over the sun's place and lets it fall away sideways and upward. I wrote
my own plan of piles by height above the rim, with the glow reaching higher over the sun's
place, and it read at once as evening.

**Warm ground through thin sky makes rust blotches.** My first skies had soft orange patches in
the upper blue. They weren't clouds; they were the red-brown ground showing where the thin
lay-in and the badger thinned it. A lean dead color in the sky's own tones, laid first and
dried, removed them entirely. Keep that dead color close in value to the sky it lies under:
a full step darker made the whole upper sky dull and gray.

**Yellow into lilac goes gray if you mix it as pigment.** Blending my sky plan between its stops
as paint sent the glow's yellow into the lilac through a muddy green-gray band, which is what
those two piles make on a palette. Blending the plan as light kept the transition luminous.
A painter would lay a pale pearly band between them; the lesson is that the mixing rule of a
color field is itself a choice about paint.

**Streaks across the glow want long, not patchy.** Level cloud streaks written into the lay-in
sat in the sky, as the notebook promises, but where their noise broke them into short pieces
they read as smudges. Letting the low ones run long and thin across the glow, and keeping the
high ones faint, gave the quiet evening structure without cotton wool.

**Look at the plan unpainted, and at the sky before the finish.** Writing my color plan
straight onto the canvas, unpainted, took ten seconds and showed me a clean, luminous dusk,
while the painting looked gray. Stopping after the sky stage showed the paint had matched the
plan within a few levels. The graying came entirely at the end: the aged yellow varnish and
my umber veil took the blue out of the upper sky. A yellow film over blue is gray. Thin the
varnish over a blue picture and make the darkening veil a neutral dark, not umber. And
compare the whole with a render stopped before the finish now and then, because the finish
runs on every render and you stop seeing it.

**Streaks thinner than the brush vanish.** My plan's thin evening streaks were narrower than
the broad lay-in brush, so the lay-in averaged them into soft blurs. Laid again right after
the badger with a small filbert in long level strokes, still into the wet, they kept their
line and still sat in the sky. Give each band its own wander, or they run parallel like
contour lines.

## Sea

**A calm sea at a grazing angle is a mirror, and a mirror reads as fog.** Taking the reflected
sky at its physical strength made the sea as pale as the sky. The sea at dusk sits a clear
step darker than the rim and cooler. I cut the reflection's share hard and took the reflected
color a step down.

**Mix reflections as light over a dark body.** Mixing the warm reflected glow into a
blue-gray water body as pigment made khaki. The aged varnish, yellowing everything a little
more, finished the job. Reflection is light added; mixed that way over a slate body, and a
little cooler than the sky, the sea turned slate at once, and I kept the warmth for the glow
path alone.

**Long faint swell lines give the sea its plane.** A few level lines barely lighter or darker
than the water, breaking off and crowding together toward the horizon, did for the sea what
the wind ripples did for the sand.

**Glints are few, long, straight and dull.** My first glints were many short curved touches:
a sequined rectangle, then commas. A handful of long level straight pulls, barely lighter
than the path, read as light on water.

## Beach

**Seen from standing height, a pebble is a flat dash.** Pebbles as round touches came out as
polka dots, then as soft round disks like out-of-focus lights. Foreshortening flattens
anything lying on the ground to a sliver whose height is its depth times the eye's angle.
Dragged level, thin, few, and gathered in drifts along the wrack line, they became a stony
beach.

**Small touches into a wet lay-in vanish.** The same pebbles pressed into the wet sand fused
into it and could not be seen at all; larger ones dabbed into wet made soft dark clusters
that read as heath. Let the sand dry first, or the pebbles are part of the sand.

**Wind ripples do the recession.** Thin broken lines a shade darker than the sand, lying
across and crowding together as they go back, gave the flat beach its plane better than any
tonal gradient.

**Brown next to a violet sky reads as olive or as desert.** My first sand read green, then as
orange desert sand. A mauve-gray brown sat under the dusk.

**A tide pool is harder than it looks.** I dug a hollow for a pool in the foreground. First
it couldn't be seen at all: its water lay below the sand in front of it, hidden by the lip,
because a pool that high on a beach can't hold water at sea level. In a broad trough it came
out as a bright blue lens that looked pasted on, because near water gives back the upper sky
and that is far lighter than dusk sand; darkened, it shrank to a pale jagged sliver like
litter. A foreground this dark wants to stay quiet. I took it out.

## Stones

**In contre-jour after sunset, the stone family must sit below the sand.** My stones came out
as pale ghosts, then as snow-capped cakes. Measuring the pixels showed they were painted
exactly as asked: my shadow color was lighter than the dusk sand, and small flat stones show
mostly their tops, so the cool top color became the whole stone. Faces toward the viewer in
deep shadow, tops only a little lighter and only on the big stone, and the small ones nearly
the value of the sand around them.

**Less rounding makes granite.** A heavily rounded block was a loaf or a whale. The same block
with small edge rounding, a long broken back sloping away and a chip off the shoulder read as
split granite, and the sky light on the broken back plane gave it its form.

**A cut plane can take the whole top off.** A second plane meant to step the stone's back
sliced off everything above it and left a low coffin. Coarser roughening of the whole mass
made the long slope undulate and the shoulder round off, which read as a glacial boulder
without any new plane.

**Feet: never carry sand color over a stone's foot.** A pass of short strokes in the sand's
color along each foot laid a pale beaded strip, because the sand a little below was lighter
than the dark round the stone. A few dark pebbles lying across the bottom of the outline, and
a dark glaze in the contact, seated them.

## Stakes and their reflections

**A row of stakes needs stumps and one that leans.** Evenly tall posts at even spacing were a
fence. Some stumps barely out of the water, a close pair and one leaning hard, on a line that
walks away at an angle, made them fishermen's stakes.

**Mirror each post yourself.** The reflections I got for thin posts were short pale stubs.
Mirroring each post about its own waterline, fading with depth and broken into level bars by
a ripple field, gave calm-water reflections that read at once, the leaning one leaning.

**Painted posts need wood, not fill.** Filled from its silhouette, each stake was a clean dark
rectangle. A few wobbling drags up each post along its own line, in slightly different darks
and grays, clipped to the post, made weathered timber.

## The figure

**Mixed dark paint on a small figure can dry much lighter.** A dark red-brown shawl came out as
a bright orange cape. Asking for a much darker pile gave the quiet warm dark I wanted. Judge
small figure paint on the canvas, not by its masstone.

**Asymmetry makes a figure a person.** The figure helper paints a symmetric woman: a pattern.
Three small gestures of my own, one end of the shawl hanging free on one side, the knot of hair
shifted as her head turns toward the glow, the hem dipping on the side she stands on, did
more than any modeling could at that size.

**The head on the horizon.** With the eye lower than her head and her feet at the water's edge,
her head breaks the sea's rim by a hair. That small overlap does more for her than any
detail on her.

## Working method

- The sky took four fifths of every whole render, so I changed it in batches and the land
  freely. What I would do differently: from the start, mark the code that builds the scene as
  belonging to the stages that use it. Mine sat ahead of everything, so the program counted it
  as part of the sky, and moving a stone made the whole sky stale; I paid for that half a
  dozen times before I noticed.
- When a passage looks wrong in a way the eye can't place, measure it. The pale stones and
  the khaki sea were both painted exactly as asked: the plan was wrong, not the paint.
- I would decide the stone's particular shape first. It is the second thing the eye goes to,
  and it was a loaf, a whale and a lump before it was a stone.
