# Before you compose: what makes a picture Cézanne's

Read this before drawing. Materials and evidence are in
`notes/research/cezanne_materials.md`; source keys below refer to that file.
`notes/cezanne.md` translates the hand to this easel. Work from written
knowledge, never reference images. Don't copy a known painting.

## The principles

1. **Build weight out of colour relationships.** An apple turns through
   neighbouring green, yellow, orange and red marks; a light cloth turns
   through pale warm and cool colours [AIC22]. Keep a value order, but don't
   shade a perfect solid in grey and tint it afterwards. Each colour needs
   its neighbour to explain what plane it belongs to [NG].

2. **Let a few marks act together as a plane.** Parallel, often diagonal
   strokes make a patch, and the next patch changes the plane [BUT]. Keep
   that rhythm local: another region may need horizontal, vertical, curved
   or tapering marks [NG; NA]. His mature hand isn't one diagonal texture
   applied over everything.

3. **Give the space around an object as much thought as the object.** The
   wall comes forward around fruit; colour can cross a boundary and join
   what a contour would separate [AIC22; AIC10]. Lose a contact where the
   two planes need to meet, then find another where they need to part.
   Don't enclose everything in one dark line [LETTER].

4. **Give each thing its weight without forcing one perfect viewpoint.**
   A tabletop can rise toward us, its edges not quite aligning beyond the
   cloth; a vessel's mouth can offer more of its interior than its body
   would predict [AIC10; MET]. Keep these adjustments answerable to the
   whole arrangement, not a general rule that everything must be crooked.

5. **Keep the light ground in the painting.** Leave room between marks,
   keep an earlier wash where it helps, and let some edges stay unresolved
   [NG; BUT]. These are the painting's available light and its earlier
   thoughts, not blank holes to distribute for an effect. Bare ground does
   not mean raw unprimed cloth.

6. **Begin lightly; draw again as you paint.** A few searching lines and
   uneven dilute blue or green touches can place the relations [NG]. Colour
   and drawing develop together. Later blue marks may move or clarify a
   contour rather than obediently tracing the first sketch [NG; AIC22].

7. **Use paint of different body, without disguising the marks.** Much of
   his oil paint is thin but opaque; translucent overlaps and thicker
   passages coexist [NG; BUT]. Don't turn the whole painting into either
   impasto or a transparent watercolour imitation. Mix a few piles, try
   their thin and thick marks, and let a load serve several strokes.

8. **Return to relationships, not to equal coverage.** Work one region,
   look at the whole, and answer it in another. Rest and revisit where
   paint needs to set; preserve earlier decisions that still help. Long
   reworking is documented, but isn't the rule for every painting [NG].
   Keep the assigned sitting budget. Stop without polishing every boundary
   into certainty.

## First study

Invent a small still life with a few uneven green-red apples, one plain
pale vessel and a rumpled light cloth on a warm wooden table, before a
quiet cool wall. Choose the arrangement yourself. View enough of the table
from above for its plane to press forward. This is a study subject, not
a description of a particular Cézanne to reproduce.

Before the first mark, confirm the supplied tube box with `tubes()`;
emerald green, viridian and ultramarine are in the existing Impressionist
box, not the short default-box table. Try one group of related strokes and
one overlap on scratch. Then paint in small campaigns: place, mix, look,
revise, rest. The choice of which plane to paint next is yours.

## Check before you paint (the imitator's mistakes)

- Do the marks build the planes, or merely decorate already-shaded objects?
- Does a change of colour give a form weight, or does everything depend on
  a continuous light-to-dark gradient?
- Do the objects and the space between them belong to the same painting?
- Have all the strokes become one direction and one size, or all the edges
  one kind of outline?
- Are the ground and earlier marks still doing useful work, rather than
  being covered simply to call the picture finished?

These questions guide the work, not a scorecard for the result. Show the
plain full painting; the integrator chooses evidence crops. Keep your
process notes separate from Alice's first view and give no self-verdict.
