# Before you compose: what makes a picture Friedrich's

Read this before you draw anything. It is about composition, light and restraint.
Materials are in `notes/research/friedrich_materials.md`. Every claim here has a
source in `notes/friedrich.md` ("What makes a picture Friedrich's", round 7); the
key is in brackets. Don't copy a known picture: find your own subject and give it
his order.

## The principles

1. **Decide the order before the scene.** His landscapes "usually adhere to a severe
   underlying symmetry" and are "relatively empty" [MW]. Start from an axis, a few
   parallel bands and a simple figure (a triangle, an arch, a curve that opens upward),
   then clothe it with land. Symmetry holds the whole, and the irregularity lives
   inside the parts: gnarled branches inside a symmetric frame [MAD cat.29]. A diagonal
   sweep of land from one corner is the Baroque way. That was Dahl's way, and it is
   the thing that separated his borrowed dusk and fog from Friedrich's [MW cat.13].

2. **Cut the near from the far.** Give a shallow foreground and an unreachable
   distance, and take away the middle ground a viewer could walk through [RV
   Rosenblum]. Stack zones parallel to the picture plane. If you need a barrier (a
   ledge, a wall, a band of growth), let it stop the eye, not lead it in. There is no
   path into depth and no repoussoir that ushers the viewer along.

3. **Give most of the canvas to the air.** In most of his pictures the sky takes more
   than half [MW cat.8]. Rule the horizon level. Soften it only where mist lies on it
   [MAD cat.55].

4. **Use one figure or a few, still and on the axis.** Seen from behind, doing
   nothing, never looking up at the sky: straight ahead or down [MW]. Put them where
   the geometry points: on or near the axis, at the apex, often overlapping the
   horizon so the head reads against the light [MW cat.7]. A small figure off to the
   side is staffage, a measure of scale, and that is what his followers did [MW
   cat.13]. You can have no figure at all.

5. **Paint light, not the sun.** The sun has set or sits behind something. The
   brightest light is a band where the dark land zone meets the light sky zone
   [MAD cat.29]. Twilight, fog and haze make the distance strange [MW cat.8]. A moon is
   small and partly veiled [MW cat.9]. One light and one time of day govern the whole
   picture: no sunset sky over a sunlit foreground [CAR].

6. **Build the sky in bands.** Horizontal bands of cloud and color, maybe one wedge of
   cloud and light across them [MAD cat.74]. The color runs from the brightest at the
   horizon to deeper color above, without a break [MAD cat.72]. Give the sky your most careful work
   [MW]. Don't let cloud shapes echo each other or the moon.

7. **Keep water calm and let it echo the sky.** His seas are calm [MW cat.7]. The water
   repeats the sky's colors "more transparently" [MW cat.7] and keeps its own value
   order: it may darken toward the horizon and lighten again just below it [MAD
   cat.89]. Mirror the geometry (the horizon as a hinge) rather than copying every
   form.

8. **Choose one key and a few zones.** One dominant tonality, sometimes near
   monochrome [RV Asvarishch]. Each zone holds its own color rather than all of them
   blending into one atmosphere [MAD cat.72; MW]. Allow one small accent (a coat, a
   dress) at most.

9. **Leave out, then leave out more.** He drew ships and nets and never painted them
   [CATS]. A visitor summed up his pictures as a beach, the sea, clouds, a man "that is
   all" [MW Monrad]. Keep the foreground bare. Carus's plants and flowers are the
   follower's mistake [MW cat.10]. Every object you keep must be needed. If the picture
   is not about the light, it is about one object: a ruin, a tree, a cross, a ship [MW
   cat.6].

10. **Be exact where it matters and let the distance go.** Forms against the light are
    drawn exactly, down to "every needle on the firs" [Ramdohr, in WP-en Tetschen].
    Distance is less detailed and dissolves in haze [HH]. That is a decision you make
    shape by shape, not a blur applied everywhere. He subordinates parts "by correct
    grouping and by the distribution of light and shadow", not by neglect [WQ].

11. **Let restraint carry the mood.** No action, no weather drama, no story, no
    display of feeling [RV Asvarishch]. One definite feeling rather than all of them
    mixed [WQ]. Symbols stay few and open, not a rebus [RV Rosenblum].

## Check before you paint (the imitator's mistakes)
- Is there an axis, and does the main thing sit on it or answer to it?
- Can the eye walk from the front edge to the horizon? Then cut the middle.
- Count the kinds of things. The *Monk* has four: monk, shore, sea and sky [WP-de
  Mönch]. As a rule of thumb (mine), more than five and it drifts toward a
  follower's picture.
- Is the figure small, at the side, looking up or doing something? Fix it or drop it.
- Is the sun's disk visible? Is the moon bright, bare and centered? Is the sky
  dramatic? Are the waves breaking?
- Is the light one light at one hour over the whole picture?
- Did any cloud, tree or wave come from a formula, the same shape repeated? Carus
  called that "the handy cursive script" of landscape [CAR].
- Is this the *Monk* again? That picture "stands alone" in his work [RAD]. Most of his
  pictures are ordered, symmetric and hold one object of contemplation.
