# Edward Hopper: materials and method

*A technical report from documented sources. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified,
disputed or taken from a single object. The evidence assembled here
comes mainly from his record books, a 1959 interview, his wife's diary
as quoted by a biographer and the technical notes of a few museum
catalogues. Much of the record-book evidence and the biographical
evidence was read through search excerpts. The two 1939 museum technical
accounts were also read through search excerpts; the preparatory-drawing
and 1935 conservation reports were read through search summaries.*


## Summary

Hopper's mature oils are on commercially primed canvas, mostly linen.
He bought it ready-primed from colormen and did not prepare his own
grounds. He worked out the design in drawings
on paper first. Then he drew on the canvas in charcoal or graphite; in
one recorded case he went over the lines in blue paint. He started with paint thinned almost wholly with turpentine and
added linseed oil as the work went on, "until the medium becomes pure
oil," using "as little oil as I can possibly help". Examined
canvases include successive layers, mostly wet over dry in one example,
and thin opaque paint with localized thick passages in another. Record-book entries report several weeks of painting for
individual canvases. He "rarely" glazed.
He scraped paint off to correct passages and applied only a
retouching varnish, never a final one. His record books name the
canvas, the brand of colors, the white and the oil for most paintings
from the late 1930s on, and individual pigments now and then.
The documented pigments are listed in §4.

**Terms.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layers between canvas and
paint. *Single/double prime*: a canvas given one or two coats of priming
by the maker. *Tacking margin*: the canvas folded over the side of the
stretcher. *Squaring*: a grid ruled over a drawing and over the canvas
so the drawing can be copied square by square at a larger size. *IRR
(infrared reflectography)*: imaging that shows carbon-based drawing
under paint. *X-radiograph*: an image made with X-rays, which shows
lead-rich paint. *Wet over dry*: a new layer laid on paint that has
dried. *Glaze*: a transparent layer. *Traction crackle*: wide cracks
that form when a layer shrinks over paint that was not yet dry.
*Cross-section*: a microscopic sample cut through all layers. *Metal
soaps*: compounds that form when metal ions from a pigment react with
the fatty acids of the oil.

## 1. Supports

- **Linen, bought from colormen.** In 1959 he reported buying linen from
  a London colorman. He did not make his own stretchers. In 1966
  he recorded a "thick single white prime linen canvas" from the same
  maker.
- **Named canvases in the record books.** The entries name several
  commercial canvases by grade and origin: "Belgian canvas" (by 1938);
  "Belgian smooth, double prime canvas" (1939–41); "Winton canvas," a
  grade of the London colorman (late 1930s to 1943, and 1958); an
  American colorman's "tested artist's canvas (linen)" (1944); a Belgian
  colorman's "heavy linen canvas" (1944); "Irish linen primed in
  America"; the London colorman's "(National) double prime canvas" (from
  1949); and its "Herga" single-prime linen (1956–66). One entry of 1945 reads "Linen canvas, smooth prime of
  doubtful character".
- **Choosing canvas.** For one picture he spent "several days trying to
  locate canvas with a surface he liked that he believed strong enough"
  before starting. *Single account.*
- **Weave.** Two examined canvases of 1939 are medium-weight plain weave.
  One is described as single-thread. *Read through search
  excerpts.*
- **Formats.** The Whitney holds 222 of his paintings. Of these, 121 are
  on canvas and six on linen. The others are small panels of paperboard,
  wood, composition board or canvas board, mostly about 24 × 33 cm, and
  most of them date from his student years, his stay in Paris (1906–07)
  and the late 1910s. Most of his canvases there from the 1920s
  on measure between about 64 × 92 cm and 102 × 153 cm; a few are
  smaller. One of 1939 measures 91.9 × 127.2 cm. His
  biographer mentions a "ten-by-twelve-inch sketch on canvas board"
  (1914) and "small paintbox-panel-sized studies" made one summer "for
  quick preliminary effects".
- **Original size kept.** Both examined canvases of 1939 keep their
  tacking margins, so they are close to their original dimensions. *Read through search excerpts.*

## 2. Grounds

- **Commercial priming.** "I use the ground of the already prepared
  canvas. I can't tell what this ground is exactly … I paint directly
  upon it".
- **Color and thickness.** On one canvas of 1939 the ground is a "thin
  cream-colored layer that does not obscure the weave". On another of the
  same year it is white. In both it runs over all the tacking margins,
  which shows it was applied before the painting, by the maker. *Read through search excerpts.* The record books distinguish
  single from double priming. In 1966 he preferred a thick single white
  priming, "smooth as double would be, but less apt to crack than
  double".
- **Ground left visible.** On one canvas, thin early layers leave the
  ground showing through. On another, he scraped paint away down
  to the ground and exposed the canvas texture. *Read through
  search excerpts.*

## 3. Drawing and lay-in

- **Drawings first.** Hundreds of his drawings in conté and charcoal
  survive, most of them preliminary studies. Before one canvas of
  1940 his wife noted that he "is doing things with no end of
  preparation" and "had 2 highly finished crayon sketches". A
  museum report describes extensive preparation before painting and few
  alterations in X-rays of two canvases. *Read through a search
  summary.*
- **Charcoal on the canvas.** For several canvases his biographer,
  drawing on his wife's diary, records that he had the canvas "drawn in
  charcoal" before painting.
- **Blue paint over the charcoal.** She also noted that he "had charcoal
  drawing half covered by permanent blue in which he covers charcoal
  lines". "Permanent blue" was a trade name for synthetic
  ultramarine. X-radiography of a canvas of 1943 found only a
  partial underdrawing, with outlines of some areas in dark blue paint. *Uncertain* (read through a summary).
- **Squaring and pencil.** On a canvas of 1939, graphite squaring lines
  show along the edges and faintly through the paint. They suggest the
  design was transferred from a smaller drawing or a photograph.
  On another canvas of the same year, IRR shows underdrawing in two
  places. Pencil marks set the ends of a series of horizontal lines, and
  an incised line fixes one vertical edge. *Read through search
  excerpts.*
- **Lay-in thin.** He began "with almost pure turpentine" as the medium. On one canvas the first layers are "opaque but quite thin" and
  let the ground show through.

- **Brands.** He named one London colorman as his maker in 1959. The record books show a Dutch colorman's paints in the early
  entries and in 1938. From 1939 to 1945 he used paints from a Belgian
  colorman together with the London colorman's. After that he used the
  London colorman's alone. When he changed to
  the London colorman, his wife took over his remaining Dutch paints,
  "including the zinc white, as Edward changed to lead white".
- **A fugitive yellow.** On one canvas of 1939 a green passage has turned
  blue where it was exposed to light. Paint under the frame rabbet keeps
  the original green. The cause is probably a fugitive yellow in the
  mixture. *Inference by the cataloguers; the yellow was not
  identified. Read through search excerpts.*

## 5. Media and varnish

- **Turpentine to oil.** "I use almost pure turpentine to start with,
  adding oil as I go along until the medium becomes pure oil. I use as
  little oil as I can possibly help".
- **Linseed, earlier poppy.** In 1959 he reported discontinuing poppy oil
  because of concerns about cracking, and using linseed oil and lead
  white. The record books match. Early entries pair zinc white
  with poppy oil. Entries of 1939–45 name poppy oil with the lead white.
  Linseed oil appears in 1938, linseed oil with turpentine in 1943–44,
  and linseed oil in every entry seen from 1947 on.
- **Two oils, two whites in one painting.** One entry of the later 1930s
  reads "1st lead white and linseed oil. 2nd Zinc white and poppy oil". Another, of 1944, reads "Started with [a New York
  supplier's] linseed oil, finished with [the London colorman's] poppy
  oil".
- **Slow drying.** He once wrote that a new linseed oil "seems to take a
  long time drying … it is still tacky about a month after I last worked
  on it". *Single account.*
- **Handbooks.** He said he received no instruction in materials at art
  school. What he knew came "from perhaps experience or having read"
  published painting-materials handbooks.
- **Varnish.** "I never use a final varnish. I use a retouching varnish
  which is made in France … and that's all the varnish I use." He applied
  it "almost at once, as soon as there is no tackiness in the pigment."
  Its purpose: "it brings out the areas that have dried in and gives them
  their proper value." A final varnish he left to later owners or
  restorers. The two canvases of 1939 now carry synthetic
  varnishes from later treatments.

## 6. Layers and application

- **Direct and in layers.** He reported painting directly on the
  prepared canvas. On one canvas the paint was applied "in
  layers, mostly wet over dry". *Read through search excerpts.*
  A conservation report describes eight or more layers in one sampled
  passage of a 1935 canvas. *Read through a search summary.*
- **Thin and opaque, with a few thick passages.** On one canvas of 1939
  most of the paint is in "opaque but quite thin layers." One passage is
  built up in many thick layers, repeatedly applied and dragged with the
  brush into a convoluted texture. Another is laid on thickly with a
  palette knife. *Read through search excerpts.*
- **Few glazes.** "No, I rarely use glazes, rarely".
- **Reserves.** On one canvas he left unpainted reserves around some
  forms and then emphasized the edges by scraping away ground to expose
  the canvas texture. *Read through search excerpts.*
- **Graphite over paint.** On one canvas of 1939, fine lines were drawn
  with graphite pencil over the paint. *Read through search
  excerpts.*


## 7. Working sequence and reworking

- **Order.** Accounts describe preparatory drawings and drawing on the
  canvas; one diary account records blue paint over charcoal lines. He reported beginning with turpentine-rich paint and adding oil
  as work progressed, then applying retouching varnish once the surface
  was no longer tacky.
- **Duration.** The record books give "1 month painting" and "About 4
  weeks painting" for single canvases; a third reads "1 2 month
  painting," probably one and a half months (*OCR reading*). His wife wrote of one canvas: "Each day I don't see how E.
  can add another stroke". On another: "Each day one thinks the
  picture done, but no".
- **Scraping out.** His wife recorded passages being scraped off and
  repainted. He expressed concern that covered paint might become
  visible through later layers as they aged.
- **Few changes on the canvas.** A museum report describes few
  alterations in X-rays of two canvases. *Read through a search
  summary.* A canvas of 1943 shows changes mainly in one position and in
  some outlines. *Uncertain* (summary).
- **Late additions.** One early canvas had a passage added in June 1959,
  decades after it was begun.
- **Studio work.** The record books note where each canvas was painted,
  usually his New York or South Truro studio. In
  Paris in 1906–07 he painted outdoors almost every day, on small
  panels of about 24 × 33 cm and canvases of about 60 × 73 cm.

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Zinc white.** He believed zinc white could scale and crack, and
  thought a zinc-white passage in one returned canvas had required
  restoration. On a canvas of 1935 whose record-book entry lists
  zinc white, a conservation report describes extensive old flaking,
  fractures through and between its layers, and zinc soaps. *Read
  through a search summary.* In oil, zinc white "tends … to form hard,
  often brittle films" that crack over softer layers.
- **Traction crackle.** In the thickest passage of one canvas, wide
  traction cracks expose an earlier, darker layer. It was probably not
  fully dry when the next layers went on. *Read through search
  excerpts.*
- **Faded yellow.** See §4: a green turned blue where it was exposed to
  light.
- **Linings.** Both canvases of 1939 were wax-lined and put on new
  stretchers: one in 1964, the other early in its museum life and again
  in 1980. *Read through search excerpts.*
- **Few other problems.** In 1959 he knew of only one painting that had
  needed attention. *His own account.*

## 9. The tubes here

The historical names in this note are mapped to these tubes: lead white
(flake, silver or Cremnitz white) → lead white; zinc white → zinc white;
cerulean → cerulean blue; cobalt → cobalt blue; ultramarine and
"permanent blue" → ultramarine blue; viridian → viridian; "cad. yellow
light" → pale cadmium; cadmium yellow → cadmium yellow; "cadmium deep" →
deep cadmium (a deep yellow-orange, *inferred*); light red → red earth (a
proxy); yellow ochre → yellow ochre; black → bone black (a proxy);
burnt sienna → burnt sienna. Burnt sienna rests on an uncertain reading
(§4).

## 10. At this easel

- **Support.** Plain-weave linen with ground layers laid from the tubes.
- **Paint.** Pigment from the tubes with an oil-medium share.
- **Taking paint off.** A brush picks up wet paint as it passes; that is
  how paint comes off the canvas.
- **Everything else** the easel does is in the easel guide.
