# Six winter paintings: critique

Looked at G, H, J, K, M, N (wholes) and all 18 crops. Positions below are given as (x, y) in the 1000x700 crop images.

## 1. General impressions and ranking

**G. Dolmen, two trees, top-hatted wanderer.** Yellow-to-blue dusk with a crescent, a capstone on three uprights, a big oak right, a smaller tree left, a tiny man with a cane. The composition is the most literal Friedrich quotation (Dolmen in Snow) and it is a pleasant picture at thumbnail. It does not survive the crops: the sky is a stippled gradient (G_crop_upperright, everywhere), the trees are smooth vector silhouettes with uniform taper and odd dark leaf-flags on the twigs (G_crop_center 300-700, 30-200), the snowfield is a flat fill with a crack net. Nothing was painted; things were placed. It could charm someone, but it does not move.

**H. Ruined abbey, dead oak, winding path, firs, fence.** Pink-gray dusk, crescent with a glow, two birds, a snow-lined tree, a path with footprints, a figure walking toward the ruin. This is the only one where the biggest area, the snow, has visible strokes: H_crop_lowerleft shows broad horizontal lanes of blue-gray and cream laid over each other, and the trunk is built from drybrush streaks (H_crop_lowerleft 330-450, 0-630). The abbey is a cut-out gray silhouette with a white rim (H_crop_center 380-700, 320-600) but it sits far enough back to pass. The path pulls the eye to the ruin and the figure is small enough to be lonely. This one moves me, a little. It looks like a person with a flat brush and gouache.

**J. Squat dolmen, black tree, firs, dark sky.** Heavy blue-gray sky pressing down on a yellow horizon, a chunky stone hut with three snow-capped boulders, a wanderer, flat blue mountains. The dolmen is the best painted passage in the whole set: J_crop_lowerleft 360-960, 40-700 is dabbed patches of ochre, gray and brown that overlap and leave gaps, which is how stone gets painted. Around it the picture falls apart: the tree trunk is a flat black shape with one highlight stripe (J_crop_lowerleft 0-180), there are two floating brown blobs with a stick near (300, 130) that read as a bug, and the crack net on the sky is the heaviest of the six after M. The oppressive sky is genuinely atmospheric. It is the clumsiest picture, which by the owner's yardstick is not entirely a bad thing.

**K. Gnarled tree, frozen pond, tiny figure, firs, distant town.** Enormous empty sky and snowfield, a pale pond of broken ice, a figure the size of a comma, a steeple on the far shore. The proportions are the most Friedrich of the six: most of the canvas is nothing, and the nothing is the point. The pond is quiet and right (K_crop_lowerleft 460-1000, 460-700: soft blue plates with white rims). The tree trunk is stacked square-ended dark strokes with lighter streaks (K_crop_lowerleft 0-300), which reads as brush marks. Then the moon: a crescent with a ring of white dots around it (K_crop_upperright 340-450, 170-300) that looks like a particle sprite, and the firs are dark cones with white dabs like a Christmas card (K.png 750-900, 430-610). Moving in composition, undercut by two cheap things.

**M. Bleached noon, tree, pond, figure, scribbled firs, fence.** Pale blue over pale yellow, everything washed toward white, a dark tree as the only weight, birds, footprints leading to the figure, a faint steeple. The light idea is good: flat winter glare with no shadows. The firs are the only honestly hand-drawn thing in six pictures, scratchy pen-like strokes that vary (M.png 770-920, 440-640). The picture is then buried under a crack net so dense it reads as a Photoshop filter (M_crop_center: cracks every 30-60 px across the whole sky; M_crop_upperright the same). The trunk has a soft blurred edge like an airbrush (M_crop_lowerleft 220-300, 300-700). Could move someone if the net were gone. As is, it looks like a filter applied to a sketch.

**N. Overcast, big bare tree, curved path, cloaked wanderer with staff.** Gray sky with a warm band at the horizon, a wide brown-gray tree with fine twigs, a path of pale blue ice bending toward a far hill with firs and a small tower, rocks and grass in the foreground. No crack net, no stipple. The sky is soft blotchy cloud patches (N_crop_upperright) and the trunk is built from strokes of bark color (N_crop_lowerleft 180-300, 200-700). The branches are smooth uniform-taper lines (N_crop_center 0-600, 250-650) and the snow is one flat gray-white plane with no form. The figure with the staff on the path is the best figure in the set. It is the most coherent light and the calmest picture. It reads as a clean soft digital painting rather than oil, but nothing in it is fake in the way a sprite or a filter is fake.

**Ranking (owner's yardstick first, then whether it moves):**

1. **H**: the only one whose snow was painted, and the composition earns its melancholy.
2. **N**: no gimmicks, the best-drawn tree and the truest light, held back by an unpainted snowfield and vector branches.
3. **K**: the most Friedrich in spirit and the emptiest in the right way, sabotaged by a sprite moon and card-shop firs.
4. **J**: one great painterly passage (the stone) inside a picture that is otherwise flat shapes under a crack filter.
5. **G**: an attractive illustration, but every surface is a fill or a vector; the least paint of the six.
6. **M**: a good idea of light drowned under the heaviest filter in the set.

## 2. Paint or pixels?

Most costly first for each.

**G**
- Pixels: the sky is a fine speckle noise over a gradient (G_crop_upperright, whole crop). This is dithering, not brushwork; no stroke direction anywhere in the sky.
- Pixels: both trees are single-path silhouettes with mathematically even taper and identical small dark scraps at every twig end (G_crop_center 300-700, 30-200 and G_crop_upperright). Winter oak does not carry leaf-flags and a brush does not produce identical tips.
- Pixels: the snow caps on the capstone and boulders are clean outlined white shapes (G_crop_center 200-740, 440-500; G_crop_lowerleft 700-830, 250-300).
- Pixels: grass tufts are one stamp repeated (G_crop_lowerleft 200-460, 530-560 and 600-740). Birds are one glyph (G_crop_upperright 540, 480 and 650, 570).
- Pixels: a uniform crack net over sky and snow.
- Paint: the far hills with the low mist (G_crop_lowerleft 0-500, 230-300) are soft-edged and believable. The wanderer silhouette is a good three-mark figure.

**H**
- Pixels: the crack net across the sky (H_crop_upperright, entire crop) is uniform and sits on top of everything, including the moon's glow.
- Pixels: the abbey is a flat gray cut-out with a hard white edge along every top surface (H_crop_center 380-700, 320-600). It is the same value all the way down with no atmosphere at its base.
- Pixels: the trunk stops in a flat horizontal cut at the snow line with a faint blue rectangle under it (H_crop_lowerleft 330-450, 600-640). Snow does not meet a tree that way.
- Pixels: birds are a glyph (H_crop_center 240, 150 and 330, 210). Grass tufts are stamped.
- Paint: the snowfield. H_crop_lowerleft shows overlapping horizontal lanes of blue-gray, warm gray and cream with ragged ends and gaps between them; the values shift across the field. This is the single best large passage of paint in all six.
- Paint: the trunk's drybrush streaks of lighter brown over dark (H_crop_lowerleft 340-440, 100-600). The snow on the branches is laid as single white strokes with varied width (H_crop_lowerleft 120-720, 30-360).
- Paint: the sky's horizontal stroke bands under the noise (H_crop_upperright 0-1000, 400-700).

**J**
- Pixels: the crack net on the sky is dense and even (J_crop_center 0-1000, 0-450; J_crop_upperright).
- Pixels: the tree trunk is a flat black shape with a single continuous highlight line (J_crop_lowerleft 0-180, 0-700; J.png 100-230, 130-640). Nothing in it says brush.
- Pixels: two floating brown blobs with a stick between them at J_crop_lowerleft (250-350, 120-160). Probably meant as birds; reads as a glitch.
- Pixels: the snow caps on the boulders are hard-edged white lozenges (J_crop_lowerleft 150-300, 550-600; J.png 190-570, 550-600). Firs are one stamp at three sizes (J.png 600-870, 300-500).
- Pixels: the distant mountains are a single flat blue-gray shape and the band under them is a stipple (J_crop_center 0-1000, 400-520).
- Paint: the stone. J_crop_lowerleft 360-960, 40-700: irregular dabs of ochre, gray, brown and blue-gray overlapping, with the darker pocket in the doorway built from the same dabs. This is the most painterly passage in the set.
- Paint: the small grass tufts at the base of the stones (J_crop_lowerleft 430-520, 510-580) have individual bent stalks.

**K**
- Pixels: the moon halo, a ring of scattered white dots around the crescent (K_crop_upperright 340-450, 170-300). It looks like a particle effect. It is the single most expensive mistake in all six pictures because it sits in the emptiest, most important part of the sky.
- Pixels: the sky speckle and the crack net (K_crop_center whole crop; K_crop_upperright lower half).
- Pixels: the firs, flat dark cones with regular rows of white dabs (K.png 750-900, 430-610).
- Pixels: the pink clouds are soft smeared shapes with no edge variety (K_crop_center 100-1000, 200-320). The distant town is a row of identical little pagoda shapes (K_crop_lowerleft 350-640, 180-200).
- Pixels: grass tufts stamped, the snow plane is a fill with a faint blue drift shape.
- Paint: the trunk, built from short square-ended strokes stacked and offset with lighter streaks between (K_crop_lowerleft 0-300, 0-620).
- Paint: the pond, soft blue plates with broken white edges and a warmer center (K_crop_lowerleft 460-1000, 460-700; K.png 300-740, 520-570).

**M**
- Pixels: the crack net. M_crop_center and M_crop_upperright are almost entirely crack net at 30-60 px spacing over a stipple gradient. It is denser than any real craquelure and does not follow thickness, so it reads as an overlay.
- Pixels: the trunk's outer edge is a soft blur, like an airbrush or a Gaussian-feathered mask (M_crop_lowerleft 220-300, 300-700). Oil paint has an edge.
- Pixels: the pond is a pale oval with a dotted ring around it (M.png 330-780, 520-600).
- Pixels: the horizon hills are a flat band and the steeple is a faint double line (M_crop_center 0-1000, 530-600; 850-900, 490-600).
- Paint: the firs, scratchy scribbled strokes of varying weight that do not repeat (M.png 770-920, 440-640). The fence at right is drawn the same way (M.png 890-990, 480-680).
- Paint: the white snow strokes on the branches (M_crop_lowerleft 130-400, 100-280) and the grass tufts here have individual stalks (M_crop_lowerleft 520-850, 470-650).

**N**
- Pixels: the branches are smooth uniform-taper curves and the twig ends are identical thin lines (N_crop_center 0-600, 250-650; N_crop_lowerleft 0-1000, 0-200). It is a very good tree drawing, but it is drawing, not paint.
- Pixels: the snowfield is one plane of soft gray-white with no strokes and no form (N_crop_lowerleft 300-1000, 150-700). The whole lower two-thirds of the picture is unpainted.
- Pixels: the distant forest band is a stipple (N_crop_center 0-1000, 430-560). Firs on the hill are one stamp repeated (N.png 780-990, 400-460).
- Pixels: rocks are soft gray blobs with a single lighter top (N.png 60-140, 620-660; 820-880, 600-640).
- Paint: the trunk, strokes of green-gray and brown laid along the grain with gaps (N_crop_lowerleft 180-300, 200-700).
- Paint: the sky, soft cloud patches that vary in size and value with no noise or net (N_crop_upperright, whole crop). Not oil, but hand-made.
- Paint: the path's ice, pale blue plates with soft edges bending with the ground (N.png 500-680, 420-700).

## 3. Advice, in order of payoff

**For the painters**

1. **Turn off the crack net and the sky stipple.** Five of six carry a uniform craquelure and four carry a speckle gradient. Real craquelure follows paint thickness, lives mostly in dark thick passages and is invisible across a thin sky. A uniform net is the first thing a viewer's eye catches and it says "filter" before it says anything else. M and J would jump two places with these gone. If you must age the picture, put it in the darks only at very low opacity.

2. **Paint the snow.** It is 50-70% of each canvas and in five of six it is a fill. Do what H did and go further: three or four values of near-white (warm cream, cool blue-gray, violet-gray, straight white), big flat-brush strokes that follow the ground's slope, overlapping so the underlayer shows in gaps, drifts that pile against every trunk, stone and post. Snow in low sun has long blue shadows from every upright; nobody painted one.

3. **Lose edges.** Every object sits on the snow like a sticker (H's trunk cut flat at the base, G and J's outlined snow caps, H's abbey with a white rim). At the horizon the hills should dissolve into the sky wet-in-wet; at the base of a tree the snow should cover the roots; the far side of a stone should be softer than the near side. One lost edge does more for "painted" than any texture.

4. **Build trees from strokes, not paths.** K's trunk and N's trunk are the right idea: many short marks with varied width and value. Extend that to the branches, which in G, J, M and N are single smooth curves. Vary the taper, let one branch be thicker than the rule, break the silhouette with a stroke that overshoots. Drop G's leaf-flags on a winter oak.

5. **Stop stamping.** Grass tufts, birds, firs and distant houses repeat as one glyph across every picture. Three tufts drawn differently beat thirty copies. A bird is two strokes; draw it fresh each time. Firs should vary in height, lean and density, and the ones farther away should be paler and softer, not just smaller.

6. **Moons are three strokes.** G's is a tiny clean sprite, K's has a particle ring. A crescent in oil is one pale stroke and a soft scumble around it. If the moon is not important, make it nearly invisible (M's is close to right).

7. **Fewer things.** K is the most moving because it has the least in it. H would gain from losing the fence; N from losing half the rocks and tufts; G from losing one tree. Friedrich's power is two or three masses and a great deal of nothing.

8. **Pick one light and commit.** N and M both chose a light (overcast, glare) and hold it; that is why they feel coherent. G and K have a dusk sky over a snow that is lit from nowhere.

**For the tool builders**

1. **No global post-process passes.** Any texture that appears in the sky at the same density as in the trunk is a tell. Crack, noise and canvas weave must come from the stroke model or the substrate, not from a layer on top.

2. **Strokes need a beginning and an end.** Paint load should run out along a stroke so the tail goes drybrush, and the start should show a heavier deposit. Right now strokes are uniform from start to finish (see N's branches, G's trees).

3. **Wet-in-wet at edges.** When a stroke lands on a wet passage the edge should pull and mix. This is what makes lost edges possible and what none of the six has at the horizon.

4. **Bristle structure and impasto shading.** H's trunk shows streaks; nothing shows ridges. If the engine has height, light it from the picture's light direction so thick strokes cast tiny shadows. This is the cheapest way to make a flat fill look like paint.

5. **Kill the stamp primitives, or randomize them hard.** Tufts, birds and firs should be generated from a few strokes with per-instance variation in pressure, angle, color and count, and some should be allowed to fail.

6. **Expose the substrate.** Let thin passages show canvas weave or a toned ground under the sky and snow, and let heavy passages cover it. Uniformity of coverage is one of the strongest "rendered" signals in all six.

7. **Give the painter a way to glaze.** A transparent warm or cool pass over a finished area is how a real painter unifies light. M's bleached look and N's overcast look would both be stronger with a real glaze rather than a low-contrast palette.
